Adding the potential effect of options, restricted stock, convertible securities, and contingent shares, and presenting EPS.

A puzzle to start

Cullowhee Creek Financial reports basic EPS of $2.00. But executives hold options to buy 4,000,000 shares at $30, bondholders can swap their bonds for 5,000,000 shares, and preferred shareholders can convert into 2,000,000 more. If all of that happened, would $2.00 still describe what each common share earns? Investors want to know the “worst case.”

The answer is to compute EPS twice. Basic EPS uses only shares actually outstanding. Diluted EPS pretends that every potential common share that would reduce EPS has already become a common share.

The continuing example

Cullowhee Creek Financial earned net income of $62,000,000 (tax rate 25%). Weighted-average common shares are 29,500,000 and the 6%, $50 par cumulative preferred dividend is $3,000,000, so basic EPS is $59,000,000 ÷ 29,500,000 = $2.00. The average market price of the common shares during the year was $40. These notes add one potential common share at a time.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

Diluted EPS is also in Topic 260, Earnings per Share, mostly in ASC 260-10-45 (computation and presentation) with illustrations in 260-10-55 and disclosures in 260-10-50.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-16 describes diluted EPS: it measures performance over the period while giving effect to all dilutive potential common shares outstanding.
  • ASC 260-10-45-17 excludes potential common shares whose effect would be antidilutive.

Options, rights, and warrants: the treasury stock method

For diluted EPS, assume options, rights, and warrants were exercised at the beginning of the year (or on the date they were issued, if later). The company would have received cash from the exercise. To make every company’s calculation comparable, the standards assume one use of that cash: buying back as many shares as possible at the average market price for the year. Only the net new shares are added to the denominator. This is the treasury stock method. The numerator does not change.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: executive stock options

Executives hold fully vested options to buy 4,000,000 common shares at an exercise price of $30. The average market price was $40.

Treasury stock method
ItemAmount
Shares assumed issued on exercise4,000,000
Assumed proceeds (4,000,000 × $30)$120,000,000
Shares assumed repurchased ($120,000,000 ÷ $40)(3,000,000)
Net increase in shares1,000,000

Diluted EPS so far = $59,000,000 ÷ (29,500,000 + 1,000,000) = $1.93.

A quick dilution test for options

If the exercise price is below the average market price, fewer shares can be bought back than are issued, so shares increase and EPS falls: the options are dilutive. If the exercise price is above the average market price, more shares would be bought back than issued: the options are antidilutive and are ignored. (A rational holder would not exercise them anyway.)

What counts as “proceeds”

Assumed proceeds have two components:

  1. Cash, if any, the company would receive on exercise (exercise price × shares).
  2. Compensation from the award that has not yet been expensed. The company will still “receive” those future services. The part already expensed is excluded because it has already reduced earnings; including it would count the dilution twice.

For example, if Cullowhee Creek’s options had a grant-date fair value of $8 and were only halfway through their vesting period, unexpensed compensation would be 4,000,000 × $8 × ½ = $16,000,000. Proceeds would be $136,000,000, which buys back 3,400,000 shares at $40, for a net increase of 600,000 shares.

Restricted stock and RSUs

Unvested restricted stock and RSUs are potential common shares too, and they also use the treasury stock method. Employees pay nothing, so the only proceeds are the unexpensed compensation. (Vested shares are already outstanding and are in basic EPS.)

Cullowhee Creek Financial: RSUs

At the beginning of this year, Cullowhee Creek granted RSUs for 600,000 shares when the share price was $36. The units vest over four years. At year-end, one-fourth of the $21,600,000 total compensation has been expensed.

Treasury stock method for unvested RSUs

Shares assumed issued at vesting600,000
Unexpensed compensation ($21,600,000 × ¾)$16,200,000
Shares assumed repurchased ($16,200,000 ÷ $40)(405,000)
Net increase in shares195,000

(To keep the continuing example simple, the rest of these notes leave the RSUs out.)

Options exercised during the year

If options are actually exercised during the year, the shares issued are added to both basic and diluted EPS, weighted from the exercise date. For diluted EPS only, also add the incremental shares from the treasury stock method for the part of the year before exercise, using the average price for that part of the year. Suppose Cullowhee Creek’s 4,000,000 options were exercised on September 1, when the average price since January 1 had been $38: both calculations add 4,000,000 × 4/12 = 1,333,333 shares, and diluted EPS also adds [4,000,000 − ($120,000,000 ÷ $38)] × 8/12 = 561,404 shares.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-23 describes the treasury stock method: assume exercise at the beginning of the period (or at issuance, if later) and the use of the proceeds to buy common shares at the average market price during the period.
  • The same treasury stock method applies to share-based awards, with unrecognized compensation cost included in the assumed proceeds (see ASC 260-10-45-22 through 45-29).

Convertible securities: the if-converted method

Convertible bonds, notes, and preferred stock can be exchanged for common shares at the holder’s option. For diluted EPS, assume they were converted at the beginning of the year (or when issued, if later). This is the if-converted method, and it changes both parts of the fraction:

The if-converted method
SecurityNumeratorDenominator
Convertible bonds or notesAdd back the interest, after tax, that would have been avoidedAdd the shares that would have been issued
Convertible preferred stockDo not subtract the preferred dividends that would have been avoidedAdd the shares that would have been issued

Cullowhee Creek Financial: convertible bonds

Cullowhee Creek has $100,000,000 of 8% bonds, issued at face value in an earlier year, convertible into 5,000,000 common shares. The tax rate is 25%.

Effect of the assumed conversion of the bonds
ItemAmount
Interest expense avoided (8% × $100,000,000)8,000,000
Less: tax deduction lost (25% × $8,000,000)(2,000,000)
After-tax interest added to the numerator6,000,000
Shares added to the denominator5,000,000

Without the interest, income would be higher, but so would taxes. That is why only the after-tax interest, $8,000,000 × (1 − 25%), is added back.

Three refinements for convertible debt

  • Issued at a discount or premium. Add back the full interest expense, including amortization. If the bonds had been issued for $96,000,000 with straight-line amortization over 10 years, the add-back would be [$8,000,000 + $400,000] × 75% = $6,300,000.
  • Issued during the year. Assume conversion on the issue date, not before. Bonds issued on May 1 would add $6,000,000 × 8/12 = $4,000,000 to the numerator and 5,000,000 × 8/12 = 3,333,333 shares to the denominator.
  • Actually converted during the year. Diluted EPS comes out exactly the same as if they had not been converted. The actual shares (weighted after conversion) are in basic EPS, the actual interest savings after conversion are already in net income, and the assumed conversion covers the months before the conversion date.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: convertible preferred stock

Now suppose the 6% cumulative preferred stock is convertible into 2,000,000 common shares.

If the preferred had been converted, there would be no preferred dividend. So diluted EPS does not subtract the $3,000,000 (it is added back to the basic numerator) and adds 2,000,000 shares to the denominator. Basic EPS still subtracts the dividend; it is unaffected by the conversion feature.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-40 describes the if-converted method: add back preferred dividends on convertible preferred, add back interest (net of tax) on convertible debt, and assume the shares were issued at the beginning of the period or at issuance, if later.

Antidilution and the order of inclusion

A potential common share is antidilutive if including it would increase EPS (or reduce a loss per share). Antidilutive securities are left out of diluted EPS, because the goal is to show the maximum dilution.

Testing convertible securities

For options, compare the exercise price with the market price. For convertible securities, both parts of the fraction change, so compute each security’s incremental effect (earnings per incremental share):

Incremental effect of each convertible security
SecurityNumerator effectShares addedIncremental effect per share
Convertible bonds$6,000,0005,000,000$1.20
Convertible preferred stock$3,000,0002,000,000$1.50

The ordering rule

Start with basic EPS and add the options and warrants first (they change only the denominator). Then add the convertible securities one at a time, lowest incremental effect first. Include each one only if its incremental effect is lower than the EPS figure calculated so far; once a security fails, stop. A security that looks dilutive on its own can turn out to be antidilutive after a more dilutive security is included.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: computing diluted EPS

Cullowhee Creek Financial earned net income of $62,000,000 (tax rate 25%). Weighted-average common shares are 29,500,000 and the 6%, $50 par cumulative preferred dividend is $3,000,000, so basic EPS is $59,000,000 ÷ 29,500,000 = $2.00. The average market price of the common shares during the year was $40. The company also has the options (4,000,000 shares at $30), the convertible bonds (5,000,000 shares; $6,000,000 after-tax interest), the convertible preferred (2,000,000 shares; $3,000,000 dividends), and warrants to buy 1,500,000 shares at $48.

Building diluted EPS step by step

When the order matters. Suppose the preferred were convertible into only 1,600,000 shares. Its incremental effect, $3,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = $1.875, is below basic EPS ($2.00), so it looks dilutive. But after the bonds are included, EPS is already $65,000,000 ÷ 35,500,000 = $1.831, and adding the preferred would raise it to $68,000,000 ÷ 37,100,000 = $1.833 (shown to three decimals because the difference is small). It is antidilutive in combination and is excluded; diluted EPS would be $1.83.

Net losses

When a company reports a loss from continuing operations, adding shares makes the loss per share smaller, so every potential common share is antidilutive. Diluted loss per share equals basic loss per share. Potential common shares left out because they are antidilutive must be disclosed.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-17 through 45-20 exclude antidilutive potential common shares, consider each issue or series separately in sequence from the most dilutive to the least dilutive, and use income from continuing operations as the “control number” for these tests.

Contingently issuable shares

Some agreements promise additional shares if a future condition is met, for example, to the former owners of an acquired company if it reaches an earnings target, or to executives if net income reaches a set level.

  • If the condition is currently being met (assuming the current level continues to the end of the contingency period), add the shares to the diluted EPS denominator. No numerator change.
  • If the condition is not currently being met, ignore the shares.
  • If all conditions have actually been satisfied, the shares are no longer contingent and are included in both basic and diluted EPS.

For example, if Cullowhee Creek must issue 1,000,000 shares to executives next year if next year’s net income is at least $60,000,000, and this year’s net income is $62,000,000, the target is currently being met: diluted EPS adds 1,000,000 shares. If the target were $65,000,000, the shares would be ignored. A share-price condition works the same way, using the share price at the end of the year.

In every case: include the effect from the beginning of the year or the issue date, whichever is later, and only if it is dilutive.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-48 includes contingently issuable shares in diluted EPS if the conditions would be satisfied if the end of the reporting period were the end of the contingency period.

Reporting EPS in the financial statements

Public companies present basic and diluted EPS on the face of the income statement for every period shown, with equal prominence. A company with a simple capital structure presents basic EPS only.

  • When the income statement reports discontinued operations, basic and diluted EPS are reported for income from continuing operations and for net income. The per-share amount for discontinued operations is shown either on the face of the statement or in the notes.
  • The same potential common shares used for continuing operations are used for every other diluted per-share amount, even if they would be antidilutive for one of them.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: discontinued operations

Suppose Cullowhee Creek’s $62,000,000 of net income is made up of $66,000,000 of income from continuing operations less a $4,000,000 loss (net of tax) from discontinued operations. Using the diluted shares and adjustments computed above:

Disclosure note

The notes reconcile the numerators and denominators of basic and diluted EPS, show the preferred dividend adjustment, list potential common shares excluded as antidilutive, and describe any transactions after year-end that would materially affect EPS.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-2 and 45-3 require basic and diluted per-share amounts for income from continuing operations and net income on the face of the income statement.
  • ASC 260-10-50-1 requires the reconciliation of basic and diluted EPS, the effect of preferred dividends, antidilutive securities excluded, and significant subsequent transactions.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

IAS 33 and Topic 260 are largely converged. The differences that remain involve the treasury stock method (for example, how year-to-date diluted EPS is computed for interim periods), contracts that can be settled in shares or cash, and contingently issuable shares. In this course, use U.S. GAAP.

Common mistakes

  • Using the year-end share price in the treasury stock method. Use the average market price for the period.
  • Adding back pretax interest. Convertible debt adds back interest net of tax.
  • Subtracting preferred dividends on convertible preferred in diluted EPS. They would have been avoided, so they stay in the numerator.
  • Including antidilutive securities. Test each one, and include convertibles in order from most to least dilutive.
  • Assuming conversion before a security was issued. Weight from the issue date.
  • Including options or convertibles in basic EPS. Only shares actually issued are in basic EPS.
  • Forgetting contingent shares when the condition is currently met, or including them when it is not.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: Options and convertible bonds

Glenville Lake Partners earned $24,000,000 and has 10,000,000 weighted-average common shares and no preferred stock. Options for 900,000 shares have an exercise price of $20; the average market price was $30. Convertible 6% bonds with a face value of $20,000,000, issued at par, convert into 1,000,000 shares. The tax rate is 25%. Compute basic and diluted EPS.

Show the answer

Basic EPS = $24,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = $2.40. Options: 900,000 − ($18,000,000 ÷ $30 = 600,000) = 300,000 net shares. Bonds: after-tax interest = $1,200,000 × 75% = $900,000; incremental effect $900,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.90, below the $24,000,000 ÷ 10,300,000 = $2.33 after the options, so the bonds are dilutive. Diluted EPS = ($24,000,000 + $900,000) ÷ (10,000,000 + 300,000 + 1,000,000) = $24,900,000 ÷ 11,300,000 = $2.20.

Problem 2: Is the preferred dilutive?

Webster Valley Grocers has basic EPS of $1.50 and, after including its options and bonds, a running diluted EPS of $1.40. Its convertible preferred pays $2,000,000 of dividends and converts into 1,250,000 shares. Should the preferred be included?

Show the answer

Incremental effect = $2,000,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = $1.60. Because $1.60 is higher than the $1.40 computed so far (and even higher than basic EPS), including the preferred would raise EPS. It is antidilutive and is excluded.

Problem 3: Unvested RSUs

A company has 400,000 unvested RSUs with $6,000,000 of compensation not yet expensed at year-end. The average share price was $25. How many shares are added to diluted EPS?

Show the answer

Shares assumed repurchased = $6,000,000 ÷ $25 = 240,000. Net shares added = 400,000 − 240,000 = 160,000. No numerator change.

Problem 4: Contingent shares

A company must issue 500,000 shares next year if next year’s net income is at least $30,000,000, and another 500,000 if it is at least $40,000,000. This year’s net income is $34,000,000. What is added to diluted EPS?

Show the answer

The $30,000,000 target is currently being met, so 500,000 shares are added to the diluted denominator. The $40,000,000 target is not being met, so those shares are ignored.

Key terms

Diluted EPS
EPS computed as if all dilutive potential common shares had become common shares.
Treasury stock method
Assumes options, warrants, and unvested shares are exercised or vested and the proceeds buy back shares at the average market price; adds the net shares.
Assumed proceeds
Cash from an assumed exercise plus compensation not yet expensed.
If-converted method
Assumes convertible securities were converted; adds back after-tax interest or preferred dividends and adds the shares.
Antidilutive security
A potential common share whose inclusion would increase EPS or decrease a loss per share; excluded from diluted EPS.
Incremental effect
The numerator effect of a convertible security divided by the shares it adds; used to test and rank convertibles.
Contingently issuable shares
Shares to be issued if a future condition is met; included in diluted EPS if the condition is currently being met.
Control number
Income from continuing operations, the figure used to decide whether potential common shares are dilutive.
