A puzzle to start

A pipe bursts over a weekend and floods the stockroom at Cullowhee Valley Books. The insurance adjuster wants to know how much inventory was lost, but the last physical count was four months ago. How can the store estimate what was on the shelves the night before the flood?

Counting inventory is expensive. Companies that use a periodic system, or that need monthly or quarterly statements, often estimate ending inventory instead. The two estimation techniques in these notes start with the same idea: goods available for sale either were sold or are still on hand. If we can estimate the cost of what was sold, the rest is ending inventory.

The equation behind every estimate

Beginning inventory + Net purchases = Goods available for sale = Cost of goods sold + Ending inventory. Estimate one of the last two, and the other follows.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Cost-to-retail percentages are rounded to two decimal places when they don’t come out even.

Where these rules live

Estimation methods are tools, not separate accounting principles. They are acceptable for interim reports and for special purposes such as insurance claims. The retail inventory method is also an accepted way to cost inventory for the annual statements; many large retailers use it, and the IRS accepts it for tax purposes.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 330-10-35-1C: inventory measured with the retail inventory method is reported at the lower of cost or market.
  • ASC 270-10-45-6: for interim reports, companies may estimate cost of goods sold, for example with the gross profit method, if they disclose the method and any significant adjustments later.

The gross profit method

The gross profit method uses the company’s historical gross profit ratio to estimate cost of goods sold from net sales. It is not acceptable for annual financial statements, but it is useful for interim estimates, for testing the reasonableness of a physical count, and for estimating inventory destroyed by fire, flood, or theft.

  1. Compute goods available for sale: beginning inventory + net purchases.
  2. Estimate cost of goods sold: net sales × (1 − gross profit ratio).
  3. Estimated ending inventory = goods available − estimated cost of goods sold.

Markup on cost versus gross profit ratio

The method needs the gross profit as a percentage of sales. Sometimes a company states its markup as a percentage of cost instead. Convert it first:

Converting a markup on cost

Gross profit ratio = Markup on cost ÷ (1 + Markup on cost). A 50% markup on cost means an item that costs $1.00 sells for $1.50, so gross profit is $0.50 ÷ $1.50 = 33⅓% of sales. Equivalently, cost of goods sold = net sales ÷ 1.50.

If Cullowhee Valley Books had said “we mark books up 50% on cost,” estimated cost of goods sold would be $600,000 ÷ 1.50 = $400,000, and estimated inventory before the flood would be $560,000 − $400,000 = $160,000.

Limits of the method

  • It assumes the gross profit ratio is stable. A change in product mix or pricing makes the estimate less reliable.
  • It averages across all products, which can hide the margin of the specific goods that were lost.
  • It produces an estimate, so it can’t replace a physical count for the annual statements.

The retail inventory method

Retailers price their goods for sale, so their records naturally track inventory at retail (selling price) as well as at cost. The retail inventory method uses both:

  1. Track goods available for sale at cost and at retail.
  2. Subtract net sales (a retail amount) from goods available at retail to get ending inventory at retail.
  3. Compute a cost-to-retail percentage from the goods available.
  4. Convert: ending inventory at cost = ending inventory at retail × cost-to-retail percentage.

Because the retail method uses the store’s actual pricing, it is generally more reliable than the gross profit method and is acceptable for annual statements. A physical count, taken at retail prices, is still needed at least once a year to catch theft and spoilage.

Markups and markdowns

Prices change after goods are first priced. Retail amounts must be adjusted for these changes.

Average cost

Cost-to-retail = $360,000 ÷ $600,000 = 60%. Ending inventory = $144,000 × 60% = $86,400; cost of goods sold = $360,000 − $86,400 = $273,600.

Conventional (approximates lower of average cost or market)

Leave markdowns out of the denominator: $360,000 ÷ ($600,000 + $40,000) = $360,000 ÷ $640,000 = 56.25%. Ending inventory = $144,000 × 56.25% = $81,000; cost of goods sold = $279,000.

LIFO retail

Keep beginning inventory as its own layer and compute the percentage from the current year’s activity: $300,000 ÷ ($500,000 + $20,000 − $40,000) = $300,000 ÷ $480,000 = 62.5%. Ending inventory at retail grew from $120,000 to $144,000, so a $24,000 layer was added.

Sylva Main Street Mercantile: LIFO retail layers
LayerRetailCost-to-retailCost
Beginning layer120,00050%60,000
This year’s layer24,00062.5%15,000
Ending inventory144,00075,000

Cost of goods sold = $360,000 − $75,000 = $285,000. If ending retail had been below beginning retail, the beginning layer would be reduced and no new layer added.

Why leaving out markdowns approximates LCM

Markdowns usually signal that the goods have lost value. Leaving them out of the denominator makes the percentage smaller, which gives a lower ending inventory. The result approximates the lower of average cost or market. That is why the conventional approach is the one most retailers use: it satisfies the LCM requirement for the retail method without a separate test.

Which approach can approximate LCM?

Only the average-cost approach can be converted into the conventional (LCM) version. A LIFO retail company that must apply LCM does so separately.

Other issues: freight, returns, discounts, and shrinkage

Several items have to be placed carefully. Ask two questions: does it affect cost, retail, or both? And does it belong before the cost-to-retail percentage is computed, or after?

Where special items go
ItemCost columnRetail column
Freight-inAddedNot affected

Purchase discountsSubtracted (if gross method)Not affected
Abnormal shortage (theft, fire)SubtractedSubtracted, before the percentage

Normal shortages and employee discounts are part of doing business, so they are subtracted like sales, after the percentage is computed. Abnormal shortages are unusual, so they are taken out of both columns before the percentage is computed.

Common mistakes

  • Using a markup on cost as a gross profit ratio. Convert it first: markup ÷ (1 + markup).
  • Using the gross profit method for the annual statements. It is for estimates only.
  • Including markdowns in the conventional percentage. The conventional approach leaves them out of the denominator.
  • Including beginning inventory in the LIFO retail percentage. It is a separate layer with its own percentage.
  • Subtracting normal spoilage or employee discounts before computing the percentage. They are subtracted afterward, like sales.
  • Adding freight-in to the retail column. Freight is a cost; it doesn’t change selling prices.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: gross profit method with a markup on cost

Beginning inventory is $80,000, net purchases are $260,000, and net sales are $375,000. The company marks up goods 25% on cost. Estimate ending inventory.

Show the answer

Cost of goods sold = $375,000 ÷ 1.25 = $300,000. Goods available = $340,000. Estimated ending inventory = $340,000 − $300,000 = $40,000. (The gross profit ratio is 25% ÷ 125% = 20%.)

Problem 2: conventional retail

Goods available at cost are $252,000. At retail: beginning inventory and net purchases total $380,000, net markups are $20,000, and net markdowns are $15,000. Net sales are $315,000. Estimate ending inventory with the conventional approach.

Show the answer

Percentage = $252,000 ÷ ($380,000 + $20,000) = 63%. Ending retail = $380,000 + $20,000 − $15,000 − $315,000 = $70,000. Ending inventory = $70,000 × 63% = $44,100.

Problem 3: placing special items

Where does each item go in the retail method: (a) freight-in, (b) normal breakage, (c) a theft discovered after a break-in?

Show the answer

(a) Added to the cost column only. (b) Subtracted from retail after the percentage is computed. (c) Subtracted from both cost and retail before the percentage is computed.

Key terms

Gross profit method
Estimates cost of goods sold as net sales × (1 − gross profit ratio); not acceptable for annual statements.
Gross profit ratio
Gross profit as a percentage of net sales.
Markup on cost
Gross profit as a percentage of cost; convert before using the gross profit method.
Retail inventory method
Estimates ending inventory at retail and converts it to cost with a cost-to-retail percentage.
Cost-to-retail percentage
Goods available at cost ÷ goods available at retail, with the items included depending on the approach.
Net markups
Additional markups minus markup cancellations.
Net markdowns
Markdowns minus markdown cancellations.
Conventional retail method
The average-cost retail method with markdowns left out of the percentage, approximating lower of average cost or market.
LIFO retail method
Keeps beginning inventory as a separate layer and adds a new layer, at the current year’s percentage, when ending retail exceeds beginning retail.
