A puzzle to start

Catamount Coffee Roasters reports the same profit before tax in each of three years, yet the tax it pays is $22,500 in the first year and $45,000 in each of the next two. Why would a company that earns the same amount each year pay such different amounts, and what should the income statement show?

Tax paid versus tax expense reported (Catamount Coffee Roasters)
ItemYear 1Year 2Year 3Three-year total
Income tax paid (payable for the year)22,50045,00045,000112,500
Income tax expense in the income statement37,50037,50037,500112,500

The totals match, so the company is not escaping tax. It is only paying some of it later. Everything in these notes is about how to report that timing gap honestly. The short answer is that the income statement reports the tax that belongs to each year’s activity, and a deferred tax account on the balance sheet carries the difference until the cash catches up.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. To keep arithmetic readable, the examples use one combined income tax rate of 25% (federal plus state). Tax laws and rates change, so when you solve a problem, always use the rates and rules stated in that problem.

Why two sets of numbers exist

A company really does keep two sets of books about income. One follows financial reporting standards. Its job is to give investors and lenders a useful picture of performance. The other follows the tax code, written by lawmakers who want to raise revenue and who also use taxes to steer behavior, for example by rewarding investment in equipment or by refusing a deduction for illegal conduct.

Because the goals differ, the two rulebooks sometimes count a revenue or an expense in different years, and sometimes treat an item differently altogether. The result is two measures of income:

  • Pretax accounting income: income before income taxes on the income statement.
  • Taxable income: the amount on the tax return that the tax rate is applied to.

Most of the time the two agree. These notes deal with the cases where they do not.

Temporary differences

A temporary difference exists when the tax return and the income statement both include an item, but in different years. The question is never whether the amount is taxed or deducted. The question is when. Over the whole life of the item the two measures add up to the same total. That is why the difference is called temporary: it appears (originates) in one year and disappears (reverses) in later years.

Following one asset through three years

Catamount Coffee Roasters buys a roaster

Catamount buys a commercial roaster for $90,000 at the start of Year 1. It expects to use it for three years, so the income statement records straight-line depreciation of $30,000 a year. The tax rules let Catamount deduct the entire $90,000 on the Year 1 tax return. Income before depreciation is $180,000 each year, so pretax accounting income is $150,000 each year. The tax rate is 25%.

In Year 1 taxable income is $60,000 lower than book income because the tax return took the whole deduction up front. In Years 2 and 3 the roaster still produces $30,000 of book depreciation, but the tax return has nothing left to deduct, so taxable income runs $30,000 higher than book income each year. The totals over three years are equal at $450,000. Only the timing differs.

Two phrases describe what a temporary difference leaves behind for the future:

  • Future taxable amounts will push taxable income above book income when the difference reverses (the roaster in Years 2 and 3).
  • Future deductible amounts will pull taxable income below book income when the difference reverses.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 740-10-20 (the glossary) defines a temporary difference as a difference between the tax basis of an asset or liability and its reported amount in the financial statements that will produce taxable or deductible amounts in future years, when the asset is recovered or the liability is settled.
  • ASC 740-10-25-23 separates the two kinds. Differences that will produce taxable amounts are taxable temporary differences, and differences that will produce deductible amounts are deductible temporary differences. Those are the Codification’s names for the future taxable and future deductible amounts in these notes.
  • ASC 740-10-25-20 lists examples of events that create temporary differences.

Tax expense has two parts

It would be simple to report as tax expense whatever tax is payable that year. Catamount would show $22,500 of expense in Year 1 and $45,000 in Years 2 and 3. That would mislead readers. Year 1 profit would look unusually strong, and no one would be warned that taxes will be twice as high in the following years.

Accounting follows the accrual concept for taxes just as it does for wages or interest: record the expense in the period whose activity caused it, whenever the cash is due. A year’s income tax expense therefore has two parts:

  • The current portion: tax payable on this year’s tax return.
  • The deferred portion: the change in deferred tax assets and deferred tax liabilities during the year.

A deferred tax liability (DTL) records tax the company has been allowed to postpone. A deferred tax asset (DTA) records tax the company has already paid in advance, which will save it money in future years.

Key idea

Tax expense is not calculated on its own. It is the balancing figure that falls out of two things: the tax payable this year and the change in the deferred tax accounts.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 740-10-10-1 states the goals of income tax accounting: to record what the company owes or will get back for the year, and to record deferred tax balances that capture the future tax consequences of events already reflected in the financial statements or on tax returns.
  • ASC 740-10-25-2 sets two basic requirements. First, record the tax the company owes or is due back on its returns for the current and earlier years. Second, record deferred tax balances for the expected future tax effects of temporary differences and carryforwards.
  • ASC 740-10-30-3 and ASC 740-10-30-4 explain the two pieces of expense. A year’s total tax expense combines the tax payable or refundable for the year with deferred tax expense, and deferred tax expense is simply how much the deferred tax liabilities and assets changed over the year.

Vocabulary. The Codification calls the tax payable on this year’s return current tax expense (or benefit).

The four-step process

Use the same four steps for every deferred tax problem, however many differences it contains.

  1. Compute income tax payable. Start with pretax accounting income, adjust it to taxable income, and multiply by the tax rate.
  2. Find the ending deferred tax balances. Multiply the future taxable amounts that remain by the enacted rate to get the DTL. Multiply the future deductible amounts that remain by the enacted rate to get the DTA.
  3. Find the change in each deferred tax account. Subtract the beginning balance from the ending balance. The result tells you whether to debit or credit the account.
  4. Solve for income tax expense. Combine tax payable with the changes from step 3. Expense is the figure that makes the journal entry balance.

The entry always has the same shape. Expense is usually a debit, but it can be a credit (a tax benefit) when a large debit to a deferred tax asset outweighs the tax payable.

Shape of the income tax journal entry
AccountWhen it appears
Income tax expense (the plug)Debit, or credit if the entry produces a tax benefit
Deferred tax assetDebit if it increases, credit if it decreases
Deferred tax liabilityCredit if it increases, debit if it decreases
Income tax payableCredit for the tax owed on this year’s return

A memory aid

Pay less now, owe more later: liability. Pay more now, save later: asset. If the tax return lets you postpone tax, you get a deferred tax liability. If the tax return makes you pay ahead of the income statement, you get a deferred tax asset.

Four kinds of temporary differences

Every temporary difference involves either a revenue (or gain) or an expense (or loss), and the item shows up first on either the income statement or the tax return. That gives four combinations.

The four kinds of temporary differences and what each creates
ItemIncome statement first, tax return laterTax return first, income statement later
Revenue or gainDeferred tax liability. Installment sales taxed as cash is collected. Investment gains recorded at fair value but taxed only when the investment is sold.Deferred tax asset. Cash received in advance (subscriptions, rent, gift cards) that is taxed when received but earned later.
Expense or lossDeferred tax asset. Estimated warranty cost, accrued lawsuit losses, and bad debt estimates that are deductible only when actually paid or written off.Deferred tax liability. Faster depreciation on the tax return than in the income statement. Prepaid expenses deducted when paid.

The next two sections work one full example of each kind. Notice that each example has the same rhythm: the difference originates in Year 1 and then reverses, and the deferred tax balance rises and falls with it.

In the FASB Codification

ASC 740-10-25-20 gives examples of events that create temporary differences. They cover all four combinations in the table above: revenues or gains that are taxable before or after they appear in the income statement, and expenses or losses that are deductible before or after they appear. The paragraph also lists further, less common examples.

Deferred tax liabilities

A deferred tax liability appears when a temporary difference will increase taxable income in the future, in other words when it creates future taxable amounts. Two common causes are an expense that the tax return deducts early and a revenue that the tax return taxes late.

In the FASB Codification: recognition and measurement

  • ASC 740-10-25-29: deferred tax balances are recorded for every temporary difference and carryforward, apart from the limited exceptions in ASC 740-10-25-3. Those exceptions (for example, certain goodwill and some differences tied to foreign subsidiaries) are beyond the scope of these notes.
  • ASC 740-10-30-5(b): multiply the total taxable temporary differences by the applicable tax rate to get the deferred tax liability.
  • ASC 740-10-30-2: measurements rely on the tax law as enacted today. Possible future law changes are not factored in (see Detailed Notes B).

Deferred tax assets

A deferred tax asset appears when a temporary difference will reduce taxable income in the future, in other words when it creates future deductible amounts. The company has effectively paid some tax early, and the asset shows the savings it will collect later.

In the FASB Codification: deferred tax assets

  • ASC 740-10-25-29 calls for a deferred tax asset for every deductible temporary difference and carryforward, with the same limited exceptions found in ASC 740-10-25-3.
  • ASC 740-10-30-5(c): multiply the total deductible temporary differences and operating loss carryforwards by the applicable tax rate to get the deferred tax asset.
  • ASC 740-10-30-2: a deferred tax asset is cut back, when necessary, by the benefits that the evidence says will not be realized. That reduction is the valuation allowance, covered below.

A manager’s point of view

Managers usually prefer deferred tax liabilities to deferred tax assets. A liability means taxes are being delayed, which is helpful because the company keeps its cash longer. An asset means the company is paying taxes sooner than the income statement suggests, and cash paid out earlier costs the company the use of that money in the meantime.

Book value versus tax basis

There is a second route to the same answer, and it works from the balance sheet. The tax basis of an asset or liability is its original amount for tax purposes, reduced by whatever has already been counted on tax returns. A gap between the amount on the balance sheet (book value) and the tax basis is a book-tax difference. Multiply the gap by the enacted rate and you have the ending deferred tax balance, which is step 2 of the four-step process.

In the FASB Codification

The Codification takes this balance sheet approach, often called the asset and liability method. The glossary definition of a temporary difference (ASC 740-10-20) is written in terms of tax basis and reported amount, and ASC 740-10-25-23 ties taxable and deductible temporary differences to the recovery of assets and the settlement of liabilities.

Valuation allowance

A deferred tax asset only saves money if the company has taxable income later to apply the deductions against. So the accounting is in two stages. First, record a deferred tax asset for every deductible temporary difference. Then reduce it with a valuation allowance if it is more likely than not (more than a 50% chance) that some or all of the asset will never be realized.

The idea is not new. You already reduce accounts receivable with an allowance for amounts you do not expect to collect. A valuation allowance does the same thing for a deferred tax asset.

How management decides

The test is whether enough taxable income will exist in the years the deductions become available. Evidence from every angle counts:

  • Negative evidence: losses in recent years, expected changes that will hurt future profits, deductions that will expire unused.
  • Positive evidence: a record of consistent profits, signed contracts that will generate future income, and prudent tax-planning strategies that management could use to create taxable income in time, such as selling an appreciated asset.

The allowance is re-evaluated every reporting period, and it is adjusted up or down to the balance that is now required. Because it depends on judgment about the future, analysts watch it closely: a rising allowance is often a sign that management has become pessimistic.

In the FASB Codification: valuation allowance

  • ASC 740-10-30-5(e): a valuation allowance is required when, weighing all the evidence, there is more than a 50 percent chance (“more likely than not”) that part or all of a deferred tax asset will go unrealized. The allowance is sized to leave the asset at the amount that is more likely than not to be realized.
  • ASC 740-10-30-17: weigh all available evidence, favorable and unfavorable.
  • ASC 740-10-30-18: realization depends on having enough taxable income of the right kind. The paragraph lists four possible sources: reversals of existing taxable temporary differences, future taxable income apart from those reversals, taxable income in carryback years, and tax-planning strategies (ASC 740-10-30-19).
  • ASC 740-10-30-21 and ASC 740-10-30-22 give examples of negative evidence and positive evidence. Cumulative losses in recent years are a leading example of negative evidence, and they make it hard to conclude that no allowance is needed.
  • ASC 740-10-30-23: the weight given to a piece of evidence should match how objectively it can be verified.
  • ASC 740-10-55 (implementation guidance) includes worked illustrations on valuation allowances and tax-planning strategies.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

Under U.S. GAAP a company records the full deferred tax asset and then reduces it with an allowance. Under IFRS (IAS 12, paragraphs 24 and 34) a company records a deferred tax asset only to the extent that realizing it is probable (more likely than not), so no separate allowance appears. As a result, U.S. GAAP financial statements can show larger deferred tax assets together with an offsetting allowance.

Sometimes the plug flips. If the deferred tax assets rise by more than the tax payable, income tax expense becomes a credit, reported as an income tax benefit. For instance, a company with 300 of tax payable and a 450 increase in its net deferred tax asset would debit the deferred tax asset for 450, credit income tax payable for 300, and credit the benefit for 150.

In real reports the pieces may not tie out perfectly. The expense table covers continuing operations only, while the balance table covers every part of the company, and acquisitions or other items can move deferred taxes in between.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 740-10-50-2: disclose the components of the net deferred tax liability or asset, including the total of all deferred tax liabilities, the total of all deferred tax assets, and the total valuation allowance.
  • ASC 740-10-50-6: public companies also disclose the approximate tax effect of each type of temporary difference and carryforward that gives rise to a significant part of the deferred tax liabilities and assets. That is the line-by-line table in the example above.
  • ASC 740-10-50-9: disclose the significant components of income tax expense for continuing operations. The list includes current expense or benefit, deferred expense or benefit, the benefit of operating loss carryforwards, adjustments for enacted changes in tax laws or rates, and adjustments to the beginning-of-year valuation allowance when management’s judgment about realizability changes.

Because a deferred tax asset is measured net of any allowance (ASC 740-10-30-2), changes in the allowance flow through deferred tax expense, which is why the allowance appears in the reconciliation of the deferred taxes in a tax note.

Common mistakes

  • Computing expense first. Expense is always the last number. Start with tax payable and the deferred balances.
  • Using the wrong direction. Pay less now means a liability; pay more now means an asset. If you are unsure, use the book value versus tax basis table.
  • Forgetting that the balance is a running total. The journal entry records the change in the account, not the ending balance.
  • Applying the tax rate to the whole difference. Multiply only the amounts that remain to reverse, using the enacted rate for the years they will reverse.
  • Treating the valuation allowance as a deferred tax liability. It reduces the deferred tax asset, and changes in it run through income tax expense.
  • Mixing up the DTA with a receivable from the government. A deferred tax asset is the value of future deductions, not a refund that is owed right now.

Problem 3: Which kind is it?

For each item, say whether it creates a deferred tax liability or a deferred tax asset: (a) rent collected in advance and taxed when received but earned in later years; (b) a gain on an investment recorded at fair value, taxed only when the investment is sold; (c) an estimated lawsuit loss, deductible only when paid; (d) tax depreciation that is faster than book depreciation; (e) revenue from a sale that is recognized now but collected and taxed over the next three years.

Show the answer
  • (a) Deferred tax asset. The tax return reports the revenue first, so tax is paid in advance.
  • (b) Deferred tax liability. The income statement reports the gain first, and tax comes later.
  • (c) Deferred tax asset. The expense is in the income statement first; the deduction comes later.
  • (d) Deferred tax liability. The tax return takes the deduction first.
  • (e) Deferred tax liability. The income statement reports the revenue first, and the tax return reports it later.

Key terms

Pretax accounting income
Income before income taxes, as reported in the income statement.
Taxable income
Income as determined by the tax rules; the base to which the tax rate is applied.
Temporary difference
A difference between book and tax treatment that reverses over time because the item is recognized in different years.
Origination and reversal
A temporary difference originates when it first arises and reverses when it unwinds in later years.
Future taxable amounts
Amounts that will increase taxable income relative to book income when a temporary difference reverses. They create a deferred tax liability.
Future deductible amounts
Amounts that will decrease taxable income relative to book income when a temporary difference reverses. They create a deferred tax asset.
Deferred tax liability (DTL)
The tax a company expects to pay in the future because of income already reported in the income statement.
Deferred tax asset (DTA)
The tax savings a company expects in the future because of deductions or prepaid taxes it has already recorded.
Tax basis
The amount an asset or liability is worth for tax purposes: its original value reduced by what tax returns have already counted.
Book-tax difference
The gap between the book value and the tax basis of an asset or liability.
Valuation allowance
A contra account that reduces a deferred tax asset when it is more likely than not that some of it will not be realized.
More likely than not
A probability of more than 50%.
Income tax payable
Tax owed on the current year’s tax return.
Current and deferred portions of tax expense
Current is tax payable for the year; deferred is the change in the deferred tax accounts.
