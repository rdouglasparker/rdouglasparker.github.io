Loss Contingencies

Reading Notes

What a Loss Contingency Is

The feature that distinguishes loss contingencies from the liabilities in Part A is uncertainty as to whether an obligation really exists. The circumstance giving rise to the contingency has already occurred, but whether a liability exists will be resolved only when some future event occurs (or doesn't occur).

A loss contingency is an existing, uncertain situation involving potential loss depending on whether some future event occurs. Whether it is accrued and reported as a liability depends on (a) the likelihood that the confirming event will occur and (b) what can be determined about the amount of loss.

Consider a car manufacturer facing lawsuits from customers who say they were injured by a faulty part in cars that were recalled. The company sold those cars in the past, so the event giving rise to the potential losses has already happened. What's uncertain is whether, and how much, it will eventually have to pay. Notice what that means for the three liability characteristics: the contingency is accounted for only when the event that gave rise to it occurred before the financial statement date. If it occurred afterward, no liability existed at the balance sheet date, however likely the outcome.

Choosing an amount within a range

If one amount within a range of possible loss appears better than the others, that amount is accrued. When no amount within the range appears more likely than the others, the minimum amount is recorded under U.S. GAAP, and the possible additional loss is disclosed.

Example

A large software company reports in its notes that it has accrued aggregate legal liabilities of $306 million, and that adverse outcomes it estimates could reach approximately $500 million beyond the recorded amounts are reasonably possible. That's exactly the rule at work: the probable and reasonably estimable $306 million is accrued and disclosed, and the additional reasonably possible range is only disclosed.

What disclosure involves

If one or both criteria for accrual aren't met but there is at least a reasonable possibility that a loss will occur, a disclosure note describes the contingency and gives an estimate of the potential loss or range of loss, if possible. If an estimate can't be made, the note says so.

Product Warranties and Guarantees

Quality-assurance warranties

Most consumer products come with a guarantee. When it is included in the contract between the seller and customer, a quality-assurance warranty isn't a separate performance obligation. It is a guarantee that the customer will be satisfied with what the seller provided. Sellers offer these to boost sales, so the costs of making good on them should be estimated and recorded as expenses in the same period the products are sold (matching expenses with revenues). That is also when the company becomes obligated.

Much of the cost occurs later, sometimes years later, so this is a loss contingency: a possible future sacrifice of economic benefits (cost of satisfying the guarantee) due to an existing circumstance (the products have been sold) that depends on an uncertain future event (a customer claim). Even though the liability for any single sale is unpredictable, prior experience usually allows a reasonably accurate estimate of the total for a period. So the criteria for accrual are almost always met for product warranties.

Example

Tuckasegee Home Health introduced a new in-home air purifier carrying a two-year warranty against defects. Industry experience indicates warranty costs of 2% of sales in the first 12 months after the sale and 4% in the next 12 months. During December 2026, its first month of availability, the company sold $3,000,000 of purifiers.

Expected cash flow approach

The traditional measure of a warranty obligation is the "best estimate" of future cash flows, ignoring the time value of money as immaterial. When the warranty spans more than one year and probabilities can be attached to possible cash outcomes, the expected cash flow approach gives a more plausible estimate: weight each possible outcome by its probability to get the expected cash flow for each year, then discount at the risk-free rate.

Extended warranty contracts

An extended warranty provides protection beyond the manufacturer's original warranty. Because it is priced and sold separately from the product, it is a separate performance obligation. The accounting question isn't just how to recognize a contingent liability but when to recognize the revenue. Cash is received up front, but coverage extends over time, so the revenue is recorded as deferred revenue at the time of sale and recognized over the contract period, typically on a straight-line basis. The costs of satisfying claims are recorded in those same years.

Example

Mountain Home Appliances sells appliances with a one-year manufacturer's warranty. At the time of purchase, customers can also buy a three-year extended warranty. On January 3, 2026, the company sold a $120 extended warranty covering 2027, 2028, and 2029.

Rebates, premiums, and coupons

Promotions that obligate a company to do something in the future used to be viewed as contingent liabilities. With the revenue recognition standards, most are now part of the agreement between the seller and customer and are accounted for through revenue recognition:

Accounting for promotions
PromotionHow it's accounted for
Cash rebatesAn obligation to return cash is a reduction in the net amount paid by the customer. Estimate the rebates customers will take and reduce revenue by that estimate in the period revenue is recognized. Also recognize a liability for the estimated rebates, similar to a refundable deposit.
Premiums (noncash items such as toys or small appliances)A separate performance obligation. Allocate a portion of the original sales price to the premium (based on relative stand-alone selling prices), record it as deferred revenue, and recognize it as revenue when the premiums are delivered. No contingent liability is recognized.
Coupons that aren't part of a sales contractNo revenue recognition is involved. Issuing coupons creates a contingent liability recognized in the period the coupons are issued. In practice firms either recognize the whole estimated redemption expense when the coupons are issued, or record the expense only when coupons are redeemed. The difference usually isn't material.

Litigation Claims

Pending litigation is common; most medium and large corporations report multiple loss contingencies from litigation every year. But accrual of a loss from pending litigation is rare in practice. If a company's own financial statements say it is probable it will lose, and for how much, those statements become welcome ammunition for the other side's lawyers. And in today's legal environment, the outcome of litigation is highly uncertain, so likelihood predictions are difficult.

So while companies may accrue estimated lawyer fees and other legal costs, they usually don't record a loss until the ultimate settlement has been reached or negotiations for settlement are substantially completed. They should provide extensive disclosure, including a description of the range of reasonably possible losses, but practice has been uneven, and the SEC has pushed firms for more complete disclosure.

  • Even after a judgment has been issued against it, a firm may not accrue if it is appealing and doesn't consider a significant loss probable. The matter is disclosed instead.
  • For a loss to be accrued, the cause of the lawsuit must have occurred before the accounting period ended. It isn't necessary that the lawsuit actually was filed during that reporting period.

Subsequent Events

Several weeks usually pass between the end of a company's fiscal year and the date the financial statements are issued (or, for entities that don't widely distribute their statements, available to be issued). Events during that period can help clarify the nature of financial statement elements at the report date.

When the cause of the contingency occurs
Cause of the contingencyTiming of the new informationTreatment
Before the fiscal year-endBetween year-end and the issuance of the financial statementsThe clarifying information is used to decide whether to accrue and how much. For example, a lawsuit settled after year-end can lead to an accrual of the settlement amount at year-end.
After the fiscal year-endBefore the financial statements are issuedA liability cannot be accrued, because it didn't exist at year-end. But if failing to disclose it would make the statements misleading, a disclosure note describes it and its effect on key numbers.

Any event occurring after year-end but before issuance that has a material effect on the company's financial position must be disclosed in a subsequent events note. Examples are an issuance of debt or equity securities, a business combination, and discontinued operations. Companies must also disclose the date through which subsequent events were evaluated.

Contingent liabilities a company assumes when it acquires another company may be treated differently. If the acquirer can determine the acquisition-date fair value of the contingent liability (a warranty obligation, for example), it is measured at fair value. If not, the usual criteria apply (probable and reasonably estimable).

Unasserted Claims and Assessments

Sometimes a company is aware of a potential claim that hasn't been made yet. Whether to accrue or disclose it follows a two-step process:

  1. Is it probable that a claim will be asserted? If not, stop: no accrual or disclosure is necessary. If so, go to step 2.
  2. Treat the claim as if it has been asserted. Evaluate (a) the likelihood of an unfavorable outcome and (b) whether the dollar amount can be estimated, just as for any other loss contingency.
Example

A trucking company that frequently transports hazardous waste is subject to environmental regulations. Management has identified several sites where it is or may be liable for remediation. For sites where no penalty has been asserted, management must assess the likelihood a claim will be made and then the likelihood the company would be held liable.

And if step 1's answer is "no" (it isn't probable that a claim will be asserted), no disclosure is required even if an unfavorable outcome would be probable if an assessment were made.

Gain Contingencies

A gain contingency is an uncertain situation that might result in a gain. In a pending lawsuit, the defendant faces a loss contingency, but the plaintiff has a gain contingency. Loss contingencies are accrued when probable and reasonably estimable, but gain contingencies are not accrued. That nonparallel treatment is conservatism: it's desirable to anticipate losses, but recognizing gains should await their realization.

Material gain contingencies are disclosed in the notes, with care that the note doesn't give "misleading implications as to the likelihood of realization."

GAAP vs. IFRS

Accounting for contingencies under IFRS is part of a broader standard on provisions, contingent liabilities, and contingent assets. It is quite similar to U.S. GAAP: a contingent loss is accrued if it's both probable and can be reasonably estimated. But there are important differences.

Terminology

IFRS calls accrued liabilities provisions, and calls possible obligations that are not accrued contingent liabilities. U.S. GAAP uses “contingent liabilities” for all of these obligations.

Definition of “Probable”

IFRS defines probable as “more likely than not” (greater than 50%), a lower threshold than the one typically associated with “probable” in U.S. GAAP. So a loss with, say, a 55% chance may be accrued under IFRS but not under U.S. GAAP.

Measuring a Range of Equally Likely Outcomes

If a liability is accrued, IFRS measures it at the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the obligation. With a range of equally likely outcomes, IFRS uses the midpoint; U.S. GAAP uses the low end of the range.

Present Values

If the time value of money is material, IFRS requires the liability to be stated at present value. U.S. GAAP permits present values in some circumstances, but liabilities for loss contingencies such as litigation are typically not discounted.

Onerous Contracts

IFRS recognizes provisions for “onerous” contracts, in which the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the expected benefits. U.S. GAAP generally doesn't recognize or disclose losses on money-losing contracts, with some exceptions (for example, losses on long-term construction contracts and on terminated contracts).

Disclosure

IFRS requires disclosure (but not accrual) of two types of contingent liabilities: possible obligations whose existence depends on uncertain future events the company doesn't control, and present obligations for which an outflow isn't probable or the amount can't be measured reliably. U.S. GAAP doesn't make this distinction but typically requires disclosure of the same contingencies.

Gain Contingencies

Under U.S. GAAP, gain contingencies are never accrued. Under IFRS, they are accrued if their future realization is virtually certain. Both disclose contingent gains when realization is probable, but “probable” has the lower IFRS threshold.
