Current Liabilities

Reading Notes

What Makes Something a Liability

Most liabilities obligate the debtor to pay cash, and the amount and timing are specified by a legally enforceable contract: notes, bonds, and the monthly payments on a car or a home are all examples. But to be reported as a liability, an obligation doesn't need to be payable in cash. It may require the company to transfer other assets or to provide services. It doesn't have to be represented by a written agreement or be legally enforceable, and even the amount and timing of repayment need not be precisely known.

From a financial reporting perspective, a liability has three essential characteristics:
  1. It is a probable, future sacrifice of economic benefits.
  2. It arises from a present obligation (to transfer assets or provide services) to other entities.
  3. It results from past transactions or events.
Notice that the definition involves the past, the present, and the future: a liability is a present responsibility to sacrifice assets in the future because of a transaction or other event that happened in the past.

The FASB has proposed a simpler definition: a liability is a present obligation that requires an entity to transfer or otherwise provide economic benefits to others. The word "probable" would be dropped because it has sometimes been misunderstood as a probability threshold for recognition. That change isn't expected to affect how the liabilities in these notes are accounted for.

What Is a Current Liability?

On a classified balance sheet, liabilities are either current or long-term. The general definition says current liabilities are obligations payable within one year from the balance sheet date or within the firm's operating cycle, whichever is longer. A more discriminating definition says current liabilities are those expected to be satisfied with current assets or by the creation of other current liabilities.

Classification helps investors and creditors assess the risk that liabilities will require cash or other assets in the near term. If a debt is due years from now, resources can be used for other purposes without risking default. If it is due soon, payment will use current assets and reduce the liquid funds available for other uses. A major factor in the collapse of Bear Stearns in 2008 was its reliance on short-term liabilities that it couldn't refinance when lenders and trading partners grew concerned.

Conceptually, liabilities are recorded at the present value of all anticipated future cash payments (principal and interest).

In practice, liabilities payable within one year are ordinarily recorded at their maturity amounts, because the short term makes the interest (time value) component immaterial. Trade payables due within a year are specifically exempted from present value reporting.

The most common current liabilities are accounts payable, notes payable, commercial paper, income tax liability, dividends payable, and accrued liabilities. Income taxes are covered in a later chapter; the others are covered here.

Accounts Payable and Trade Notes Payable

Accounts payable are obligations to suppliers of merchandise or services purchased on account in the ordinary course of business. Most trade credit is offered on open account, which means the only formal credit instrument is the invoice. Because the time until payment is usually short (often 30, 45, or 60 days), these liabilities are typically noninterest-bearing and are reported at their face amounts.

The key accounting considerations are determining that the payables exist and ensuring they are recorded in the appropriate accounting period. Cash discounts on purchases are handled as part of inventory accounting.

Trade notes payable differ from accounts payable in that they are formally recognized by a written promissory note. They are often of somewhat longer term and bear interest.

Short-Term Notes Payable and Lines of Credit

The most common way for a corporation to obtain temporary financing is a short-term bank loan. When a company borrows cash from a bank and signs a promissory note (essentially an IOU), the liability is reported as notes payable (sometimes called bank loans or short-term borrowings). Smaller firms often rely heavily on short-term financing, and even large companies use it as an important part of their capital structure because short-term funds usually carry lower interest rates and give managers flexibility.

Lines of credit

Short-term loans are usually arranged under an existing line of credit: an agreement to provide short-term financing, with amounts withdrawn by the borrower only when needed. Even though each loan is short-term, the line of credit itself typically lasts several years.

Noncommitted line of credit: an informal agreement that lets the company borrow up to a prearranged limit without formal loan procedures and paperwork. The bank may require a compensating balance on deposit (say, 5% of the line).

Committed line of credit: a more formal agreement that usually requires the company to pay a commitment fee to keep the amount available (a typical annual fee is a fraction of a percent of the total committed funds), and may also require a compensating balance.

No journal entry is made when a line of credit is arranged; an entry is made only when a loan is actually taken. The existence and terms of the line are described in a disclosure note. A compensating balance (a deposit kept in a low-interest or noninterest-bearing account) increases the borrower's effective interest rate.

Interest on notes

Interest is stated as a percentage rate applied to the face amount, making this an interest-bearing note. Because the stated rate is an annual rate, the calculation must be adjusted for the fraction of the year the loan spans:

Interest = Face amount × Annual rate × Time to maturity

Noninterest-Bearing Notes

Sometimes a note takes the form of a so-called noninterest-bearing note. Nobody lends money without interest, so these loans do bear interest, but the interest is deducted (discounted) from the face amount to determine the cash proceeds made available to the borrower at the outset. The difference is recorded in a contra-liability account, Discount on notes payable, which is recognized as interest expense over time.

Sometimes the interest is described with a discount rate applied to the face amount, as in a note "discounted at issuance at a 9% discount rate." The interest is then the face amount × discount rate × the fraction of the year.

The same idea applies when a company buys something with a noninterest-bearing note. If inventory worth $760,000 is purchased with an $800,000 noninterest-bearing note, the inventory is recorded at its $760,000 value, and the $40,000 difference is the discount on notes payable.

Secured Loans and Commercial Paper

Short-term loans are sometimes secured, meaning a specified asset of the borrower is pledged as collateral. The assets most frequently pledged are inventory or accounts receivable. When receivables serve as collateral, the arrangement is called pledging accounts receivable. Sometimes the receivables are sold outright to a finance company as a means of short-term financing, which is called factoring receivables.

Commercial paper refers to unsecured notes sold by large, highly rated corporations to obtain temporary financing, often purchased by other companies as a short-term investment.

Statement of cash flows: cash received from short-term notes, and cash used to repay them, are financing activities. Most other current liabilities (accounts payable, interest payable, bonuses payable) are integrally related to a company's primary operations and are part of operating activities.

Accrued Liabilities

Accrued liabilities represent expenses already incurred but not yet paid (accrued expenses). They are recorded by adjusting entries at the end of the reporting period, before the financial statements are prepared. Common examples are salaries payable, income taxes payable, and interest payable. Although recorded in separate liability accounts, accrued liabilities are usually combined and reported under a single caption (or perhaps two) on the balance sheet.

Accrued interest payable

Back to Cullowhee Creek Outfitters' nine-month, 10% note. The fiscal year ends December 31, four months after the note was issued, so four months of interest has accrued and must be recorded to avoid misstating expenses and liabilities.

Salaries, commissions, and bonuses

Compensation can take the form of hourly wages, salary, commissions, bonuses, stock compensation, and pensions. Accrued liabilities arise when employees have provided services but will be paid after the financial statement date, and they are recorded by adjusting entries at the end of the period. Annual bonuses tied to performance objectives (earnings per share, net income, operating income, customer satisfaction, and so on) are compensation expense of the period in which they are earned, so unpaid bonuses are accrued as a liability at year-end.

Paid Future Absences

Suppose a firm grants two weeks of paid vacation each year. Some employees take the vacation in the same period it's earned. Others save it for a future period. Should the firm recognize compensation expense only for those who are paid during the period? Accrual accounting says no. An employer should accrue an expense and the related liability for employees' compensation for future absences (such as vacation pay) if the obligation meets all four of these conditions:

  1. The obligation is attributable to employees' services already performed.
  2. The paid absence can be taken in a later year: the benefit vests (will be compensated even if employment is terminated) or can be accumulated over time.
  3. Payment is probable.
  4. The amount can be reasonably estimated.

These are just the characteristics of a liability, adapted to employees' future absences. The liability is usually accrued at the existing wage rate rather than a rate estimated to be in effect when the absences occur, so if wage rates have risen, the difference between the accrual and the amount paid increases compensation expense in the year the vacation is taken.

Custom and practice matter

  • Company policy and actual practice should be considered when deciding whether rights to payment have been earned by services already rendered. Sabbatical leave granted only to perform research that benefits the employer isn't accrued. If past practice shows it is intended as unrestricted compensated absence for past service, it is.
  • Rights that expire aren't accrued. Holiday time, military leave, maternity leave, and jury time typically don't accumulate if unused, so no liability is usually accrued. But if it's customary that a particular paid absence can be carried forward and payment is probable, a liability is accrued.
  • Sick pay quite often meets the conditions, but accrual isn't mandatory because future absence depends on future illness. If company policy or custom pays employees sick pay even when they aren't ill (for example, accumulated unused sick pay is paid at retirement), it is appropriate to record a liability.
  • If every condition is met except that the amount can't reasonably be estimated, a disclosure note should describe the situation.

Liabilities from Advance Collections

Deposits and advances from customers, and collections for third parties, create liabilities to make payments or to provide goods or services in the future.

Refundable deposits

In some businesses it's typical to require a cash deposit that will be refunded when a specified event occurs: security deposits on apartments, deposits for utility service, deposits on returnable containers. Collecting the cash creates a liability to return the deposit.

Example

Balsam Chemical Company sells combustible chemicals in expensive, reusable containers. Customers are charged a deposit for each container and receive a refund when the container is returned. Deposits collected on containers delivered during the year were $500,000. Deposits are forfeited if containers are not returned within one year. Eighty percent of the containers were returned within the allotted time. Deposits charged are twice the actual cost of the containers, and the inventory of containers stays on the books until deposits are forfeited.

Advances from customers

Businesses sometimes require advance payments that will be applied to the purchase price when goods are delivered or services are provided: gift certificates, magazine subscriptions, layaway deposits, special order deposits, and airline tickets. These customer advances (also called deferred revenue or unearned revenue) are liabilities until the related product or service is provided. Revenue recognition must be delayed until the seller has fulfilled its performance obligation.

Example

Purple Ledger Publications collects magazine subscriptions at the time subscriptions are sold and recognizes subscription revenue over the term. In its first year of operations it collected $18,000,000 in subscription sales. At December 31, the average subscription was one-third expired.

Deferred revenue is reduced when revenue is recognized, either because the seller delivered as promised or because the buyer forfeited the advance (for example, by not redeeming a gift certificate before it expires). Less often, it is reduced when the seller returns the advance because it didn't deliver.

Interest on advances. If a customer pays far in advance of delivery, the customer is effectively loaning money to the seller, and the seller should recognize interest expense on the deferred revenue liability. Companies can ignore the interest component if it isn't significant, which is assumed to be the case if the period between payment and delivery is less than one year.

Gift cards

When a company sells a gift card, it records the cash received as deferred revenue. It recognizes revenue either when the card is redeemed or when the probability of redemption is viewed as remote, called gift card breakage (based on expiration or the company's experience).

Collections for third parties

Companies often collect amounts from customers or employees on behalf of third parties and periodically remit them to a governmental (or other) unit. These amounts are liabilities until remitted. Current vs. Noncurrent Classification

Given a choice, management would rather report an obligation as noncurrent. Outsiders (banks, bondholders, shareholders) usually consider debt that is payable currently to be riskier because the company must be able to access cash relatively soon. A long-term classification also allows the company to report higher working capital (current assets minus current liabilities) and a higher current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities), and both are often explicitly restricted in loan contracts. So the manager's real question is "What amount can I exclude from current liabilities?"

Current maturities of long-term debt

Long-term obligations (bonds, notes, lease liabilities, deferred tax liabilities) are reclassified and reported as current liabilities when they become payable within the upcoming year (or operating cycle, if longer). A 20-year bond issue is long-term for 19 years and normally current on the balance sheet prepared during the 20th year.

Obligations callable by the creditor

The requirement to classify currently maturing debt as current includes debt that is callable (due on demand) by the creditor in the upcoming year (or operating cycle), even if the debt isn't expected to be called. It also includes situations in which:

  • the creditor has the right to demand payment because of an existing violation of a provision of the debt agreement (say, working capital has fallen below a minimum covenant), and
  • debt isn't yet callable but will be within the year if an existing violation isn't corrected within a specified grace period, unless it's probable the violation will be corrected within the grace period or waived by the creditor.

Short-term obligations expected to be refinanced

Suppose a second 20-year bond issue is sold specifically to refund the first issue when it matures. Substance over form says that's a single, continuing noncurrent obligation. So short-term obligations (including callable obligations) that are expected to be refinanced on a long-term basis can be reported as noncurrent only if both conditions are met:

(1) the company intends to refinance on a long-term basis, and (2) the company has demonstrated the ability to do so, either by an existing refinancing agreement or by actual financing before the financial statements are issued.

Several weeks usually pass between the end of the fiscal year and the date the statements are issued, and events during that period can be used to clarify the nature of balance sheet items at the reporting date. The specific form of the long-term refinancing (bonds, bank loans, even shares of stock) is irrelevant.

Example

Sylva Dairy Cooperative had $18 million of notes that mature in May 2027 and $6 million of bonds issued years earlier that mature in February 2027. On December 31, 2026, its fiscal year-end, management intended to refinance both on a long-term basis. On February 7, 2027, the company issued $6 million of 20-year bonds and used the proceeds to repay the bonds that matured that month. In early March, before the 2026 financial statements were issued, it negotiated a line of credit with a commercial bank for up to $10 million any time during 2027. Any borrowings mature two years from the date of borrowing.

The ability to refinance the $6 million of bonds was demonstrated by actual financing before the statements were issued. GAAP vs. IFRS: Liabilities to Be Refinanced

Under U.S. GAAP, liabilities payable within the coming year are classified as long-term if refinancing is completed before the date the financial statements are issued. Under IFRS, refinancing must be completed before the balance sheet date.

Evaluating Liquidity

Current liabilities affect a company's liquidity: its cash position and overall ability to obtain cash in the normal course of business. A company is liquid if it has enough cash (or assets quickly convertible to cash) to pay currently maturing debts. Lack of liquidity can end an otherwise healthy company, so managers, investors, and creditors watch it closely.

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Acid-test (quick) ratio = (Cash and cash equivalents + Short-term investments + Accounts receivable) ÷ Current liabilities

A current ratio of 1 to 1 or higher is often a rule of thumb, but it should be judged in the context of the company's industry and circumstances. The acid-test ratio is more conservative because it leaves out inventories and prepaid expenses, which are less readily convertible to cash. A ratio below the industry's is a red flag, not proof of a problem; profitability is probably the best long-run indication of liquidity.

Some companies deliberately run high current liabilities. They treat accounts payable as a free loan from suppliers and pressure customers to pay quickly. That lowers the current ratio but can be an intelligent way to manage cash.

Analysts should watch for manipulation. Because accounts payable is in the denominator, the timing of when a payable is recognized can mean the difference between violating a debt covenant and complying with it.

Example

A company has current assets of $6.0 million and current liabilities of $4.5 million, a current ratio of 1.33. A debt covenant requires a minimum current ratio of 1.4, so the company is in violation. If it persuades a supplier to delay delivery of $1.0 million of inventory, current assets fall to $5.0 million and current liabilities fall to $3.5 million, so the ratio becomes 5.0 ÷ 3.5 = 1.43. The company now complies, though nothing about its real liquidity has improved.

Finally, remember the link between deferred revenue and later revenue. Increases in deferred revenue can signal future revenue recognition. But research suggests some firms with deferred revenue manage the timing of revenue recognition to manage their earnings.
