Why this unit matters

Executives are paid more in shares and options than in salary, and earnings per share is the number investors watch most closely. This unit connects the two. Share-based pay creates compensation expense and adds equity; the same awards, along with convertible securities, are potential common shares that can dilute earnings per share.

Three questions will guide you through the unit:

  • A company gives its CEO options worth $9 million but pays no cash. Why is there an expense, and why does it appear over four years instead of on the day the options are granted?
  • When a company sells shares in May and declares a stock dividend in August, why are the May shares weighted by time but the stock dividend shares are not?
  • Why would an investor want to see EPS calculated as if options were exercised and bonds were converted, when neither has happened?

What you will be able to do

When you finish this unit, you should be able to do the following.

Share-based compensation

  • Explain and record restricted stock awards and restricted stock units.
  • Explain and record stock options, including forfeitures, exercise, expiration, and their tax effects.
  • Apply the accounting for graded vesting and for performance and market conditions.
  • Determine whether an employee share purchase plan is compensatory and record it.

Basic earnings per share

  • Distinguish between a simple and a complex capital structure.
  • Compute the weighted-average number of common shares.
  • Explain how new shares, stock dividends and splits, and reacquired shares affect EPS.
  • Explain how preferred dividends affect the EPS numerator.

Diluted earnings per share and reporting

  • Include options, rights, warrants, and restricted stock using the treasury stock method.
  • Include convertible securities using the if-converted method.
  • Identify antidilutive securities and apply the order of inclusion.
  • Include contingently issuable shares.
  • Report EPS in the income statement and disclosure notes, and describe the main differences between U.S. GAAP and IFRS.

The big idea

Two ideas carry the whole unit.

  1. Share-based pay is measured once and spread over the service period. Measure the award at its fair value on the grant date, then expense it over the years the employee works to earn it. Equity awards are not remeasured; awards that can be paid in cash are liabilities and are remeasured.
  2. EPS is a fraction, and every rule adjusts the top or the bottom. The numerator is earnings available to common shareholders; the denominator is weighted-average common shares. Diluted EPS asks, “what if every security that could become a common share already had, as of the start of the year?” and keeps only the ones that lower EPS.

Remember

Diluted EPS is never higher than basic EPS. If including a security would raise EPS (or shrink a loss per share), it is antidilutive and is left out.

Rules of thumb

  • Changes in share price after the grant date never change the cost of an equity award.
  • Forfeiture estimate changes and performance probability changes are caught up in the current year.
  • A market condition (share-price target) does not affect whether compensation is recognized.
  • An ESPP discount above 5% is compensation.
  • Use the average market price for the treasury stock method.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

  • Adjusted earnings. Many companies exclude share-based compensation from “adjusted” or pro forma earnings. It is a real cost, paid in shares.
  • Option assumptions. Expected volatility and expected term drive option values. Compare them from year to year in the disclosure note.
  • Buybacks. Repurchases offset dilution from employee awards and raise EPS mechanically. EPS growth that consistently outruns net income growth deserves a closer look.
  • The gap between basic and diluted EPS measures how much ownership could shift to option holders and convertible security holders.
  • P/E and payout ratios. Price ÷ EPS and dividends per share ÷ EPS both depend on which EPS figure you use. Be consistent.

Check yourself

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. Options with a $6 fair value are granted for 400,000 shares and vest in four years. What is the annual compensation expense?

Show the answer

400,000 × $6 = $2,400,000 ÷ 4 = $600,000 a year, credited to paid-in capital—stock options. The share price after the grant date does not matter.

2. A company with 10,000,000 shares sells 2,400,000 more on October 1. What is the weighted-average number of shares?

Show the answer

10,000,000 + 2,400,000 × 3/12 = 10,600,000 shares.

3. The same company then declares a 2-for-1 split on December 1. Now what is the weighted average?

Show the answer

Both earlier amounts are doubled: (10,000,000 + 600,000) × 2 = 21,200,000 shares. The split shares are not weighted by time.

4. Options for 1,000,000 shares have an exercise price of $15. The average market price is $20. How many shares are added to diluted EPS?

Show the answer

Proceeds of $15,000,000 buy back 750,000 shares at $20, so 250,000 net shares are added.

5. Convertible bonds add $2,700,000 of after-tax interest and 1,800,000 shares. Basic EPS is $1.20. Are they dilutive?

Show the answer

The incremental effect is $2,700,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = $1.50, which is higher than $1.20. Including them would raise EPS, so they are antidilutive and excluded.
