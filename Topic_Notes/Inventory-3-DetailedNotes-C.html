A puzzle to start

Waynesville Hardware Distributors uses LIFO. Its balance sheet shows inventory of $252,000, and a note says the same goods would be $280,000 on a FIFO basis. A year ago the gap between the two was $35,000; now it is $28,000. Did using LIFO raise or lower this year's profit?

The gap between FIFO and LIFO (Waynesville Hardware Distributors)
ItemLast yearThis year
Inventory on a FIFO basis265,000280,000
Less: LIFO reserve (the gap)(35,000)(28,000)
Inventory reported on LIFO230,000252,000

It raised profit, by $7,000. The gap, called the LIFO reserve, shrank by $7,000, and a shrinking reserve means LIFO cost of goods sold was $7,000 lower than FIFO cost of goods sold this year. These notes show how LIFO companies report that gap, what happens when old LIFO layers are sold off, how analysts adjust for LIFO, and how companies make LIFO practical with pools and dollar-value LIFO.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept round so the accounting, not the arithmetic, is the focus.

The LIFO reserve

Many companies keep their day-to-day inventory records on FIFO or average cost and convert to LIFO only at year-end, for the financial statements and the tax return. They do this because unit-by-unit LIFO records are expensive to keep, because bonus and profit-sharing plans are often based on non-LIFO income, and because managers price products using current or average costs.

The difference between inventory under the internal method and inventory under LIFO is the LIFO reserve (also called the LIFO allowance or LIFO adjustment). Some companies record it as a contra account to inventory with a normal credit balance; others make the conversion outside the ledger. Either way, each year's change in the reserve is an adjustment to cost of goods sold.

The LIFO reserve rule

LIFO reserve = Inventory on the internal basis (FIFO) − Inventory on LIFO. An increase in the reserve increases cost of goods sold and lowers profit. A decrease in the reserve decreases cost of goods sold and raises profit.

Where the disclosure comes from

  • SEC Regulation S-X (Rule 5-02, item 6) requires public companies using LIFO to disclose the excess of replacement or current cost over the stated LIFO value, when material. That is the LIFO reserve in the notes.
  • Because of the LIFO conformity rule, these non-LIFO amounts may appear in the notes, but the face of the income statement must be on LIFO.

LIFO liquidations

LIFO keeps old costs on the balance sheet, sometimes costs from decades ago, in layers: the beginning layer plus a new layer for each year quantities grew. Supporters say this matches current costs with current revenues. That holds only while quantities are steady or growing.

When a company sells more units than it buys, it dips into old layers. Their old, low costs flow into cost of goods sold and are matched against current selling prices. This is a LIFO liquidation. When prices have been rising, a liquidation produces a one-time "paper" profit that will not repeat.

Murphy uses periodic LIFO. It began the year with 25,000 bags of feed that cost $12 each. During the year it bought 40,000 bags at $15 and sold 52,000. The income tax rate is 25%.

If the $27,000 is material, Murphy discloses that the LIFO liquidation increased net income by that amount, so readers don't mistake it for an improvement in operations.

Watch out: managing income with year-end purchases

Suppose Murphy's managers buy another 12,000 bags at $15 just before year-end. No old layer is touched, cost of goods sold becomes 52,000 × $15 = $780,000, and pretax income falls by $36,000 (saving $9,000 of tax this year). Skip the purchase and income is $36,000 higher. Under LIFO, a purchasing decision, not a sale, moves reported profit.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 330-10-S99-3 includes the SEC staff's view that the effect of a material LIFO liquidation on income should be disclosed.
  • ASC 270-10-45-6: in interim (quarterly) statements, a temporary liquidation of a LIFO base layer that is expected to be replaced by year-end is not recognized; cost of goods sold reflects the expected replacement cost.

Inventory ratios and comparing LIFO with FIFO

Managers want enough inventory to meet customer demand but as little as possible tied up in ordering and carrying costs. Analysts use three ratios to judge how well a company manages inventory and prices its products:

Inventory ratios
RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
Gross profit ratio(Net sales − Cost of goods sold) ÷ Net salesHow much of each sales dollar is left to cover other expenses and profit. A falling ratio can mean costs are rising faster than prices.
Inventory turnoverCost of goods sold ÷ Average inventoryHow many times the average inventory is sold during the year. A falling ratio can signal slow-moving or obsolete goods.
Average days in inventory365 ÷ Inventory turnoverHow many days it takes, on average, to sell the inventory.

LIFO changes both cost of goods sold and inventory, so ratios for a LIFO company and a FIFO company aren't directly comparable. The LIFO reserve disclosure lets an analyst convert the LIFO company to FIFO:

  • FIFO inventory = LIFO inventory + LIFO reserve.
  • FIFO cost of goods sold = LIFO cost of goods sold − the increase in the LIFO reserve during the year (or + a decrease).

Inventory and earnings quality

Earnings quality is how well this year's reported income reflects the company's ongoing, repeatable performance. Inventory gives analysts several warning signs:

  • Inventory growing faster than sales. Along with a slowing turnover, it can signal future production cuts, obsolete goods, or price cuts that will shrink margins.
  • LIFO liquidation profits. They are real accounting income but won't recur. That is why they must be disclosed when material.
  • Year-end purchases under periodic LIFO. Timing of purchases can push income up or down.
  • Write-downs and method changes. Both can shift income between periods. Write-downs to net realizable value are covered with the subsequent measurement of inventory.

Making LIFO practical: inventory pools

Applying LIFO to every individual item is called unit LIFO (or specific goods LIFO). It has two problems: it needs detailed records for every item, and it liquidates a layer whenever the quantity of any single item falls, even if total inventory is steady.

LIFO pools group similar items (all hardwood lumber, all window glass) and apply LIFO to the pool. All of a year's purchases for the pool are treated as bought at one average cost. If the pool's quantity grows, the increase becomes a new layer at that year's average cost.

Dollar-value LIFO

Dollar-value LIFO (DVL) takes pooling a step further. A DVL pool can hold a wide variety of goods that face the same cost pressures, and its layers are measured in dollars, not units. Most companies that report on LIFO use some form of DVL.

Cost indexes

The problem: if a pool's inventory grew from $400,000 to $441,000, how much of the growth is more goods and how much is higher prices? DVL removes the price change with a cost index:

Cost index

Cost index for a year = Cost at that year's prices ÷ Cost at base-year prices. The base year, the year DVL is adopted, has an index of 1.00. An index of 1.05 means costs are 5% above the base year. Companies may use an outside index (such as a producer price index) or, more often, one computed from their own costs.

Three steps

  1. Convert ending inventory to base-year costDivide ending inventory at year-end cost by the year's cost index. Comparing the result with last year's base-year cost shows whether quantity grew.
  2. Identify the layersIf base-year cost grew, the increase is a new layer for this year. If it fell, the decrease comes out of the most recent layers first (LIFO order).
  3. Restate each layer at its own year's indexMultiply each layer (at base-year cost) by the index of the year it was added, then add the layers. The total is ending inventory at DVL cost.

Why companies use dollar-value LIFO

  • Simpler records. No unit-by-unit tracking is needed; the company prices its year-end inventory at current costs and applies an index.
  • Fewer liquidations. Broad pools absorb shifts among individual items, so old layers are less likely to be sold off.
  • Product changes don't break the pool. A new model simply replaces the dollar value of the old one, so companies with annual model changes can still use LIFO.

Watch out: DVL is an approximation

DVL assumes everything added in a year was bought at that year's index. The layer's cost is an estimate of what the goods actually cost, not a record of it.

Common mistakes

  • Booking the whole reserve every year. The entry is the change in the reserve, not its ending balance.
  • Getting the direction of the reserve entry backward. An increase is a debit to cost of goods sold; a decrease is a credit to cost of goods sold.
  • Forgetting the tax on a liquidation. The disclosed effect on net income is the pretax amount × (1 − tax rate).
  • Comparing a LIFO company's ratios with a FIFO company's. Convert first, using the LIFO reserve.
  • Using the current index for every DVL layer. Each layer is restated at the index of the year it was added.
  • Rebuilding a liquidated DVL layer. Once a layer shrinks, later growth creates a new layer at the new year's index.

Vocabulary bridge: U.S. GAAP and IFRS

None of these notes applies under IFRS, because IAS 2 does not allow LIFO. A company moving to IFRS would restate its inventory to FIFO or average cost, and the LIFO reserve would disappear into inventory and equity.

Key terms

LIFO reserve
The difference between inventory on the internal basis (usually FIFO) and inventory on LIFO; a contra account to inventory when recorded.
LIFO layer
A quantity added to inventory in a given year and carried at that year's cost.
LIFO liquidation
Selling into old LIFO layers when quantities fall, which matches old costs with current revenues.
Gross profit ratio
Gross profit divided by net sales.
Inventory turnover ratio
Cost of goods sold divided by average inventory.
Average days in inventory
365 divided by the inventory turnover ratio.
Earnings quality
How well reported earnings reflect ongoing, repeatable performance.
Unit LIFO
LIFO applied to each individual item of inventory.
LIFO pool
A group of physically similar items treated as one item for LIFO purposes.
Dollar-value LIFO
LIFO applied to pools measured in dollars, using cost indexes to separate quantity changes from price changes.
Cost index
The ratio of a year's costs to base-year costs; the base year is 1.00.
Base year
The year a company adopts dollar-value LIFO.
