Statement of Cash Flows: Overview

Where cash came from, where it went, and why it differs from net income.

The big picture

The balance sheet and income statement are built on accrual accounting, so neither one tells you directly where the cash came from and where it went. The statement of cash flows fills that gap by explaining the change in cash for the period.

The three sections of the statement of cash flows
SectionWhat it coversTypical examples
Operating activitiesCash effects of transactions that enter net incomeCollections from customers, payments to suppliers and employees, interest, income taxes
Investing activitiesAcquiring and disposing of long-term assets and investments (other than cash equivalents)Buying or selling equipment, land, and investment securities; making and collecting loans
Financing activitiesRaising money from, and repaying, owners and lendersIssuing stock or bonds, borrowing, repaying debt, buying treasury stock, paying dividends

The one idea to hold onto

Every cash flow is explained by a change in some non-cash account. Net change in cash = change in all liabilities and equity accounts, minus change in all non-cash assets. That is why we can build the statement from two balance sheets and an income statement.

Two methods present the operating section. The direct method lists the major cash receipts and payments. The indirect method starts with net income and adjusts it to cash. Both give the same total, and investing and financing sections are identical under either method.

Both matter in this course

The FASB encourages the direct method, but nearly all companies use the indirect method. When the direct method is used, the company must also provide the indirect reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow. You will practice building a full direct-method statement and the reconciliation together.

Is it a cash flow?

Ask two questions. First, did cash (or a cash equivalent) actually change hands? If not, it is not on the statement body. Second, if so, was it an operating, investing, or financing item? Exchanging a note for equipment involves no cash, so it appears only in the noncash investing and financing disclosure.
