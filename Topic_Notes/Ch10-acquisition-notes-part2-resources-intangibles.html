Natural Resources and Asset Retirement Obligations

A natural resource is used up physically: a mineral deposit shrinks as it is mined. A building or machine, by contrast, stays physically intact while it produces. If a company buys a resource from someone else, cost is the price plus whatever is needed to get it ready. More often the company develops the resource itself, and cost can include four pieces:

  • Acquisition: amounts paid for the right to explore or to extract proven reserves.
  • Exploration: drilling, excavating, and other searching.
  • Development: costs after discovery but before production, such as shafts, wells, and tunnels.
  • Restoration: the cost of returning the land to an acceptable condition when extraction ends.

Heavy equipment used at the site is usually not part of the resource's cost. It is depreciated as equipment, though equipment that cannot be moved and has no other use is depreciated over no longer than the resource will last.

Asset retirement obligations (AROs)

Restoration is one example of an asset retirement obligation: a legal duty to dismantle, remove, or restore something when a long-lived asset is retired. The accounting has three moving parts:

  • Recognize a liability in the period the obligation arises, measured at fair value when it can be reasonably estimated.
  • Fair value is usually the present value of the expected cash outflow. When several outcomes are possible, weight each by its probability and discount the expected amount at the credit-adjusted risk-free rate.
  • Add the liability's amount to the cost of the related asset.

Sugar Cove paid $900,000 for the right to explore a 300-acre tract, spent $650,000 exploring, and spent $400,000 on access roads and a pit (development). It also bought $520,000 of excavation equipment. Its contract requires it to restore the land four years after extraction ends. Management sees three possible restoration outlays. Check yourself

Jarrett Creek Mining paid $8,000,000 for mineral rights and spent $2,500,000 on exploration and development. It must restore the land in five years. Management estimates the restoration cost at either $1,000,000 (30% probability) or $2,000,000 (70% probability). The credit-adjusted risk-free rate is 6% (present value of $1, n = 5, i = 6% is 0.74726). What is the initial cost of the mine?

Expected outflow = ($1,000,000 × 30%) + ($2,000,000 × 70%) = $1,700,000.
Present value = $1,700,000 × 0.74726 = $1,270,342.
Cost of the mine = $8,000,000 + $2,500,000 + $1,270,342 = $11,770,342.

Intangible Assets

Intangibles lack physical substance and usually represent exclusive rights. A company can buy them from someone else or develop them itself, and the accounting is very different:

  • Purchased: recorded at cost (price plus legal and other costs to get them ready), just like PP&E.
  • Developed internally: the development costs are generally expensed as incurred, not capitalized. Uncertain future benefits are the reason: it is hard to show which spending will pay off, and when.

Intangibles with a finite life are amortized over that life. Those with an indefinite life are not amortized. Both kinds are tested for impairment.

IntangibleLegal protectionCosts capitalized
PatentExclusive right to make or use a product or process, granted for 20 yearsPurchase price, legal and filing fees, and the cost of successfully defending it in court

Even when a company builds a patented product itself, the legal and filing fees to obtain the patent are capitalized. The research that produced the invention is not.

Example — Elkmont Biotech buys and defends a patent

Elkmont Biotech bought a patent for $350,000 and paid $22,000 in legal and filing fees to record the transfer. Two years later it spent $48,000 successfully defending the patent against an infringement claim.

Initial cost = $350,000 + $22,000 = $372,000.
The $48,000 defense cost is added to the patent account, bringing it to $420,000 to be amortized over the remaining life.

Goodwill

Goodwill is what a company is worth beyond the sum of its identifiable assets. It comes from things like reputation, loyal customers, a skilled workforce, and a good location, and it cannot be separated from the business. As a result, it is recorded only when one company buys another.

Goodwill = Price paid − (Fair value of identifiable assets − Fair value of liabilities assumed)

Goodwill is a residual: what is left after every identifiable asset and liability is valued. Companies can build their own goodwill through advertising, training, and good service, but they must expense those costs as they are incurred. That is why a balance sheet can understate the real assets of a company that grew its reputation internally, and why comparing it with an acquirer that bought its goodwill is tricky.

Check yourself

Haywood Sportswear paid $3,400,000 for all of Waterrock Outfitters' stock. On the purchase date Waterrock's assets had fair values of $820,000 (current assets) and $2,150,000 (property, plant, and equipment), and it owed $610,000 in liabilities. How much goodwill does Haywood record?

Identifiable net assets = $820,000 + $2,150,000 − $610,000 = $2,360,000.
Goodwill = $3,400,000 − $2,360,000 = $1,040,000.

Lump-Sum Purchases

Sometimes several assets are bought for one price. If the assets are identical, the answer is simple: eight identical trucks bought for $264,000 cost $33,000 each. If the assets are different (say land, a building, and equipment with different lives), the price must be divided among them, because each is depreciated or amortized differently.

The split uses relative fair values. Each asset's share of the total fair value (usually from an appraisal) is applied to the price actually paid.

Asset cost = Price paid × (Asset's fair value ÷ Total fair value of all assets)
Example — Brasstown Machining buys a plant for $3,300,000

The price covers the land, the building, installed equipment, a patent on a process the equipment uses, and a stock of inventory. An appraiser values the pieces as if bought separately:
