A puzzle to start

Jackson County Jams has 1,000,000 shares outstanding, trading at $30. It announces a two-for-one stock split. The next morning there are 2,000,000 shares, trading at about $15. Shareholders received no cash, the company paid out nothing, and total market value did not budge. So what changed, and what entry, if any, does the accountant record?

These notes cover the transactions that happen after shares are issued: buying them back, keeping and distributing earnings, and changing the number of shares. Some of them shrink the company (buybacks, cash dividends). Some only reshuffle equity accounts (stock dividends). One changes nothing but the share count (a stock split). The key question from Detailed Notes A still applies to each: how did this transaction change net assets?

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional, and amounts are kept round so the accounting stays in focus.

In the FASB Codification: where these rules live

  • ASC 505-30 covers treasury stock and the retirement of shares.
  • ASC 505-20 covers stock dividends and stock splits.
  • ASC 845-10-30 covers nonreciprocal transfers to owners, including property dividends, which are measured at fair value.
  • ASC 505-10-50 covers equity disclosures, including restrictions on retained earnings and dividends in arrears.

Why companies buy back their shares

Most large companies buy back their own shares, often in the billions of dollars a year. Common reasons:

  • Return cash to shareholders in a flexible way. Unlike a dividend increase, a buyback does not set an expectation for next year.
  • Offset dilution from shares issued to employees in stock option and stock award plans.
  • Raise earnings per share and return on equity by reducing the shares outstanding and the equity base.
  • Signal that management believes the shares are undervalued.
  • Defend against a takeover by reducing the shares available to a hostile buyer.

Whatever the reason, buying back shares pays cash to shareholders, so it reduces both assets and shareholders’ equity. The company can account for the shares in one of two ways: retire them or hold them as treasury stock.

Retiring shares

When shares are retired, reverse what was recorded when they were sold. Reduce the stock account at par and reduce paid-in capital—excess of par at the average amount originally received above par. Then compare the cash paid with that original issue price:

  1. Remove the original investment. Debit the stock account (shares × par) and paid-in capital—excess of par (shares × the average excess per share).
  2. Paid less than the original price? Credit the difference to paid-in capital—share repurchase. Some of the shareholders’ investment stays in the company.
  3. Paid more than the original price? Debit paid-in capital—share repurchase, but only up to its existing credit balance. Debit any rest to retained earnings. The extra cash is treated like a dividend.

Little Tennessee Lumber retires 100,000 shares

Before the buyback, Little Tennessee reports common stock of $2,000,000 (2,000,000 shares, $1 par), paid-in capital—excess of par of $30,000,000, paid-in capital—share repurchase of $50,000, and retained earnings of $40,000,000. The shares were originally sold for an average of $16 each: $1 of par plus $15 ($30,000,000 ÷ 2,000,000) of excess.

In Case 2, shareholders get back $400,000 more than they paid in. The first $50,000 uses up the earlier credit from share repurchases; the remaining $350,000 comes out of retained earnings. Paid-in capital—share repurchase can never go below zero.

If Little Tennessee later sells new shares, it records an ordinary sale: for 40,000 shares at $24, debit Cash $960,000, credit Common stock $40,000 and paid-in capital—excess of par $920,000.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 505-30-30-8: when shares are retired (or bought for retirement) at more than par or stated value, the excess may be charged to additional paid-in capital, as in the method above, or entirely to retained earnings. Some companies simply debit retained earnings for the whole excess over par.

Treasury stock

Most companies hold repurchased shares as treasury stock. The purchase and a later resale are treated as one transaction: the cost is parked in a contra-equity account, and any difference is assigned only when the shares are resold. This is the cost method, the one used in practice.

  • Purchase: debit Treasury stock for the cost. The stock account and paid-in capital—excess of par do not change.
  • Resale above cost: credit Treasury stock at cost and credit the excess to paid-in capital—share repurchase.
  • Resale below cost: debit the shortfall to paid-in capital—share repurchase, up to its credit balance, then to retained earnings.

Two rules that never bend

  • Retained earnings can be debited in a treasury stock transaction but never credited.
  • There is no gain or loss on the income statement. A company does not earn income by trading in its own shares; these are transactions with owners.

Treasury shares bought at different prices

If treasury shares were bought at different prices, the cost of shares resold is found with a cost flow assumption (FIFO, weighted average, or specific identification), just as with inventory.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 505-30-45-1: the cost of treasury shares is shown separately as a deduction from total equity (or the shares may be presented in other ways, such as at par with adjustments to paid-in capital).
  • ASC 505-30-30-10: gains on selling treasury stock are credited to additional paid-in capital, and losses are charged to additional paid-in capital from earlier treasury stock transactions and then to retained earnings.

Retained earnings

Retained earnings is the company’s accumulated net income less amounts distributed to shareholders. A debit balance is a deficit. Retained earnings is a claim on assets in general, not a pile of cash: a company can have a large retained earnings balance and very little cash.

  • Increased by net income.
  • Decreased by net losses, cash and property dividends, small stock dividends, some share buybacks and treasury stock resales, and certain corrections of errors and accounting changes.

Restrictions

A loan agreement or the board may restrict part of retained earnings, making it unavailable for dividends, for example to protect lenders or to save for an expansion. A restriction sets no cash aside. It is normally disclosed in the notes; a formal entry to an “appropriated retained earnings” account is rare.

Liquidating dividends

A dividend that exceeds retained earnings returns part of the shareholders’ own investment. The excess is a liquidating dividend and is debited to paid-in capital, not retained earnings.

Dividends on preferred shares

Preferred shareholders get their stated dividend before common shareholders get anything. If the preferred is cumulative, any unpaid amount becomes dividends in arrears. Arrears are not a liability, because nothing is owed until a dividend is declared, but they must be disclosed in the notes and paid before any common dividend.

Property dividends

A dividend paid with a noncash asset, most often shares of another company held as an investment, is a property dividend. It is recorded at the asset’s fair value on the declaration date. First adjust the asset to fair value and recognize a gain or loss; then record the dividend.

Stock dividends and stock splits

A stock dividend distributes additional shares of the company’s own stock to current shareholders, in proportion to what they own. No assets leave the company and no liability is created, so total equity does not change. Each shareholder owns the same percentage of the company, and the market price per share should fall in proportion to the new shares.

The accounting depends on the size of the distribution:

2-for-1 stock split

No entry. The common stock account stays at $1,000,000, but it now represents 2,000,000 shares at $0.50 par. Changing par in every record is a nuisance, which is why companies often effect a split in the form of a stock dividend instead.

Reverse stock split

A 1-for-5 reverse split would turn 1,000,000 shares at $1 par into 200,000 shares at $5 par, with no entry. Struggling companies use reverse splits to lift a low share price, for example to stay listed on an exchange.

Fractional shares

A shareholder with 85 shares is entitled to 8.5 new shares in a 10% stock dividend. Companies usually issue the whole shares and pay cash in lieu of the fraction: here, 8 shares and 0.5 × $30 = $15.

Why issue a stock dividend?

Companies sometimes declare a stock dividend to give the look of a dividend while keeping cash, or to move part of retained earnings into paid-in capital. A large distribution or split is usually meant to lower the price per share and make the stock more attractive to more investors. Requiring fair value for small stock dividends rests on an old view that investors believe they gained the value of the new shares; research shows share prices adjust.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 505-20-25-3 and ASC 505-20-30-3: a small stock dividend (one that does not materially reduce the share price, generally less than 20% to 25% of the shares outstanding) is recorded at the fair value of the shares issued, transferred from retained earnings.
  • ASC 505-20-25-2 and ASC 505-20-30-4: a larger distribution is a stock split; if it is effected in the form of a dividend, only the legally required amount (usually par) is transferred. The SEC treats distributions of 25% or more as splits.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

Return on shareholders’ equity measures how much income management earns on the owners’ book investment: net income ÷ average shareholders’ equity. When there is preferred stock, the return on common shareholders’ equity subtracts preferred dividends from net income and uses average common equity.

Cowee Mountain Media

Net income is $1,200,000 and preferred dividends are $200,000. Total equity was $9,000,000 at the start of the year and $11,000,000 at the end; average common equity was $8,000,000. There are 500,000 common shares outstanding, trading at $40.

Return measures
MeasureCalculationResult
Return on shareholders’ equity$1,200,000 ÷ [($9,000,000 + $11,000,000) ÷ 2]12.0%
Return on common shareholders’ equity($1,200,000 − $200,000) ÷ $8,000,00012.5%
Earnings per share($1,200,000 − $200,000) ÷ 500,000$2.00
Earnings-price ratio (inverse of P/E)$2.00 ÷ $405.0% (P/E of 20)

A 12% return on book equity looks strong, but investors paying $40 a share earn only 5% on the market value. Book values drift away from market values over time, so look at both.

  • Buybacks raise return on equity and earnings per share mechanically, because equity and shares shrink. Be wary when a company buys back shares during a slowdown: the higher EPS can mask weaker earnings growth, and the cash spent is no longer available to invest.
  • Dividend policy reflects the company’s stage. Mature companies tend to pay high dividends; growth companies reinvest.

IFRS

U.S. GAAP and IFRS are broadly similar for buybacks, dividends, and stock distributions. The main differences are terminology (share capital, share premium, reserves) and presentation, plus the classification of some preference shares as liabilities under IFRS (see Detailed Notes A).

Common mistakes

  • Recording a gain or loss on treasury stock. Differences go to paid-in capital—share repurchase or retained earnings, never the income statement.
  • Crediting retained earnings. A buyback or treasury resale can debit retained earnings but never credit it.
  • Retiring shares at par only. Remove both par and the average excess over par originally received.
  • Recording dividends in arrears as a liability. They are disclosed, not recorded, until declared.
  • Recording a property dividend at book value. Adjust the asset to fair value first and recognize the gain or loss.
  • Using par for a small stock dividend. Small stock dividends use the fair value of the new shares.
  • Making an entry for a stock split. A true split needs only a memo; par per share changes.

Key terms

Treasury stock
A company’s own shares that it has bought back and not retired; a contra-equity account recorded at cost.
Retired shares
Repurchased shares returned to the status of authorized but unissued.
Paid-in capital—share repurchase
Paid-in capital created when shares are retired for less than their original price or treasury shares are resold above cost.
Cost method
The usual approach to treasury stock: record the purchase at cost and assign differences when the shares are resold.
Retained earnings restriction
A portion of retained earnings made unavailable for dividends, usually disclosed in the notes.
Liquidating dividend
A dividend that exceeds retained earnings and so returns paid-in capital.
Declaration date, ex-dividend date, date of record, payment date
The four dates in the life of a dividend; entries are made only on the declaration and payment dates.
Dividends in arrears
Unpaid dividends on cumulative preferred stock from past years; disclosed, not recorded as a liability.
Property dividend
A dividend paid with a noncash asset, recorded at fair value on the declaration date.
Stock dividend
A distribution of the company’s own shares to shareholders; small ones are recorded at fair value.
Stock split
An increase in shares outstanding with a proportional cut in par value per share; no entry.
Stock split effected in the form of a stock dividend
A large distribution recorded by moving the par value of the new shares into the stock account, leaving par per share unchanged.
Reverse stock split
A decrease in shares outstanding with a proportional increase in par value per share.
Return on shareholders’ equity
Net income divided by average shareholders’ equity.
