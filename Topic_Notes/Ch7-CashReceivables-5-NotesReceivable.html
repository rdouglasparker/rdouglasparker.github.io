A question to start

Waynesville Tractor Co. sells a tractor and accepts a note promising $480,000 in two years, with no interest stated. Did it make a $480,000 sale? No. Waiting two years for cash is like lending money, so part of that $480,000 is really interest. The sale is worth only the present value of the $480,000.

These notes cover formal promises to pay, called notes receivable: how to compute interest, how to handle notes with interest built into the face amount, and how the effective interest method works for long-term notes.

Notes receivable basics

A note receivable is a formal credit arrangement, supported by a written promissory note, between a creditor (lender) and a debtor (borrower). Notes arise from loans to customers, employees, or others, and from sales of goods, other assets, or services with longer payment terms.

  • The face amount (principal) is paid at the maturity date.
  • An interest-bearing note states an interest rate. A noninterest-bearing note has interest built into the face amount.
  • Notes are current or noncurrent depending on when they will be collected.

The interest formula

Interest = Face amount × Annual rate × Fraction of the year. A six-month note uses 6/12; a 90-day note commonly uses 90/360 or 90/365, depending on the agreement.

Short-term interest-bearing notes

Short-term noninterest-bearing notes

A "noninterest-bearing" note is a misnomer. It does bear interest; the interest is simply discounted from the face amount. The difference between the face amount and the cash price of what was sold is the interest.

For notes due within a year, a seller may treat the financing component as insignificant and simply record revenue and a receivable at the face amount. If it treats the financing as significant, it records sales revenue at the cash price and recognizes the rest as interest over time, using a contra account, discount on notes receivable.

The effective rate is $24,000 ÷ $456,000 = 5.263% for six months, or about 10.53% a year. Total revenue is still $480,000: $456,000 of sales revenue and $24,000 of interest revenue.

Some companies use the net method instead: record the note at $456,000 and credit interest revenue as it is earned, with no discount account. The results are the same.

Long-term notes and the effective interest method

Long-term notes follow the same ideas, but the time value of money matters more, so the financing component is treated as significant. Sales revenue is the present value of the cash to be received, and interest is recognized with the effective interest method: each period, interest revenue = effective rate × carrying amount at the start of the period.

Waynesville Tractor Co.: a two-year noninterest-bearing note

On January 1, Year 1, Waynesville sells a custom tractor and accepts a two-year, $480,000 noninterest-bearing note due December 31, Year 2. A 9% rate is appropriate for this customer.

Waynesville earns $404,006 of sales revenue for the tractor and $75,994 of interest revenue for financing it. Interest grows from Year 1 to Year 2 because the same rate is applied to a larger carrying amount. The same method is used for long-term receivables and liabilities throughout the course.

Notes received only for cash

If a note is received solely in exchange for cash (for example, a $100,000 loan to an employee at a low or zero rate), the cash given is treated as the note's present value. The note is recorded at $100,000, even though the stated rate is unrealistically low, unless other rights or privileges are part of the deal.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 606-10-32-15 through ASC 606-10-32-20 require the transaction price to be adjusted for a significant financing component, so revenue reflects the cash selling price.
  • ASC 835-30 (Imputation of Interest) covers notes exchanged for goods or services with no or an unreasonable stated rate, and the effective interest method (ASC 835-30-35-2).

After the note is recorded

  • Credit losses. Notes receivable get an allowance under CECL, just like accounts receivable. Lenders often estimate it with discounted cash flows: compare the note's carrying amount with the present value of the cash they now expect to collect, discounted at the note's original effective rate. Banks report large "allowances for loan losses" against their loan portfolios.
  • Fair value. Companies disclose the fair value of notes receivable in the notes. They may also elect the fair value option and carry receivables at fair value, with changes in income.
  • Loan modifications for struggling borrowers. Your textbook's appendix describes "troubled debt restructuring" accounting. For creditors that use CECL, ASU 2022-02 eliminated that separate model: the expected losses are already in the allowance, and modifications for borrowers in financial difficulty are evaluated under the general loan-modification rules and disclosed.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

IFRS 9 treats discounts, returns, interest on notes, and allowances much like U.S. GAAP. Differences: IFRS restricts when the fair value option can be used, and it uses the expected credit loss model (12-month ECL unless credit quality falls significantly) rather than lifetime CECL. U.S. GAAP also requires more separate disclosure of receivables from customers, related parties, and others.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 326-20 applies the CECL allowance to notes and loans receivable.
  • ASC 825-10-50-10 requires fair value disclosure for financial instruments; ASC 825-10-25-1 allows the fair value option.
  • ASC 310-10-50 covers receivables disclosures, including loan modifications.

Common mistakes

  • Recording a long-term noninterest-bearing note's full face amount as sales revenue. Revenue is the present value.
  • Computing each year's interest on the face amount. Under the effective interest method, use the carrying amount at the start of the year.
  • Forgetting the year-end accrual when a note spans December 31.
  • Crediting interest revenue for the full interest at maturity after accruing part of it in the prior year. Credit Interest receivable for the accrued part.
  • Treating discount on notes receivable as a liability. It is a contra-asset that shrinks to zero as interest is earned.

Problem 2: Present value of a note

A company accepts a three-year, $300,000 noninterest-bearing note for land with no reliable cash price. The appropriate rate is 10%. At what amount is the sale recorded, and what is Year 1 interest revenue?

Show the answer

PV = $300,000 ÷ 1.10³ = $225,394. Year 1 interest = $225,394 × 10% = $22,539.

Problem 3: Effective rate

A six-month, $60,000 noninterest-bearing note is accepted for goods with a cash price of $57,000. What is the annual effective rate?

Show the answer

$3,000 ÷ $57,000 = 5.26% for six months, or about 10.53% a year.

Key terms

Note receivable
A formal written promise to pay, with specified payment terms.
Face amount (principal)
The amount due at maturity, excluding interest.
Interest-bearing note
A note with a stated interest rate paid in addition to the face amount.
Noninterest-bearing note
A note with interest built into the face amount rather than stated separately.
Discount on notes receivable
A contra-asset holding interest not yet earned; it is reduced as interest revenue is recognized.
Effective interest method
Recognizes interest each period as the effective rate times the carrying amount at the start of the period.
Carrying amount (amortized cost)
Face amount less any unamortized discount (and less any allowance).
Fair value option
An election to carry certain financial assets at fair value, with changes in income.
