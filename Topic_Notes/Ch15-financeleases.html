ACCT 351 · Leases — Chapter 15

Lease Classification & Finance/Sales-Type Leases

Reading Notes

Why Lease?

Leases have become the single largest source of external financing for U.S. companies. A handful of reasons explain the popularity:

1. Less upfront cash — many leases require nothing beyond the first payment, avoiding the closing costs and fees that come with a purchase.

2. Lower periodic payments — an installment purchase is priced off the asset's full value; a lease payment is priced off only the portion of value the asset is expected to use up during the lease term.

3. Easier disposal — returning a leased asset at the end of the term is simple; selling an owned asset can be costly and slow.

4. Protection from falling asset values — the lessor bears the risk (and any upside) of the asset's residual value.

5. Possible tax advantages — a lessor with more capacity to use depreciation deductions can pass some of that benefit back to the lessee through lower payments.

Substance over form: accounting for a lease depends on whether the arrangement is, in economic substance, an installment purchase or a temporary rental — regardless of what the contract is titled or who holds legal title. If the lessee ends up controlling substantially all the remaining benefits of the asset and takes on a purchase-like obligation, it's a finance lease (called a sales-type lease from the lessor's side). Otherwise, it's an operating lease.

The Five Classification Criteria

A lease is a finance lease (sales-type from the lessor) if any one of these five criteria is met. If none is met, it's an operating lease.

#Criterion
1Ownership of the asset transfers to the lessee by the end of the lease.
2The lease contains a purchase option the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise (a "bargain purchase option," or BPO).
3The lease term is for the "major part" of the asset's remaining economic life (a common benchmark: 75% or more).
4The present value of the lease payments equals or exceeds "substantially all" of the asset's fair value (a common benchmark: 90% or more).
5The asset is so specialized it will have no alternative use to the lessor once the lease ends.

Criteria 3 and 4 are the ones you'll calculate most often. Both use a discount rate: the lessor's rate implicit in the lease if the lessee knows it, otherwise the lessee's own incremental borrowing rate — what it would pay a bank to borrow and buy the asset outright.

Before the current lease standard, many companies deliberately structured leases to fail all five criteria, keeping both the asset and the liability off the balance sheet. Current GAAP requires lessees to record a right-of-use asset and a lease liability for essentially all leases — finance and operating — which is why the finance/operating distinction now mainly affects the income statement pattern rather than whether the lease shows up on the balance sheet at all.

Finance Leases Work Like Installment Purchases

A finance lease is accounted for the same way as buying the asset with a note payable: the lessee debits an asset and credits a liability for the present value of what it owes, then amortizes that liability with an effective-interest schedule exactly like a loan amortization table.

Right-of-use asset = Lease payable = Present value of the lease payments

Each lease payment is split into an interest piece (effective rate × outstanding balance) and a principal-reduction piece — identical mechanics to the installment notes from the previous chapter. Separately, the lessee amortizes the right-of-use asset, usually straight-line over the lease term (or over the asset's full useful life, if ownership transfers or a bargain purchase option is expected).

Running example — Sans Serif Publishers & First LeaseCorp

Sans Serif leases printing equipment for six annual payments of $100,000 (paid at the beginning of each year), a six-year term equal to the equipment's full useful life, and a 10% interest rate. First LeaseCorp paid $479,079 for the equipment — the same amount as the present value of the lease payments, so there's no selling profit involved (more on that below).

At least two of the five criteria are met (the lease term equals the full economic life, and the present value of payments equals the full fair value) — so this is a finance lease to Sans Serif and a sales-type lease to First LeaseCorp.

In a finance lease, interest expense and amortization expense are reported separately, and because interest is highest early in the lease (front-loading), total expense is higher in the early years and lower in the later years.

Sales-Type Leases with a Selling Profit

In the example above, First LeaseCorp is a financing intermediary — it bought the equipment for exactly what it's now financing, so its only income is interest revenue. But when the lessor is a manufacturer or dealer using the lease to sell its own product, the lessor typically earns a selling profit too.

Selling profit = Present value of lease payments (the "selling price") − Lessor's cost of the asset

The lessee's accounting is completely unaffected by whether the lessor has a selling profit — the lessee's entries look exactly the same either way. Only the lessor's opening entry changes.

Same contract, different lessor

Suppose Sans Serif instead leased directly from CompuDec Corporation (the manufacturer), which built the equipment for $300,000. The present value of the lease payments is still $479,079, so CompuDec earns a gross profit of $479,079 − $300,000 = $179,079.

Lease receivable479,079
Cost of goods sold (lessor's cost)300,000
Sales revenue479,079
Equipment (lessor's cost)300,000

CompuDec (lessor) at the beginning of the lease — this is the same structure as recording any product sale: one entry for revenue and receivable, another for cost of goods sold and inventory reduction.

Every entry after this opening entry — interest revenue, receivable amortization — is identical to a sales-type lease with no selling profit. The selling profit only shows up once, at inception.
