A puzzle to start

Lake Glenville Outfitters sold 5,500 backpacking stoves this year. Three accountants look at the same purchase records and report three different costs for those stoves: $49,000, $52,250, and $55,500. All three are following GAAP. How can one set of facts produce three right answers?

One year of stove sales, three costs (Lake Glenville Outfitters)
Cost flow methodCost of goods soldEnding inventoryTotal
First-in, first-out (FIFO)49,00027,00076,000
Average cost52,25023,75076,000
Last-in, first-out (LIFO)55,50020,50076,000

The stoves cost different amounts at different times during the year. When identical units are bought at different prices, the accountant has to assume which costs went out the door with the units sold and which costs are still on the shelf. Notice the last column: every method splits the same $76,000 of goods available for sale. The method changes only how the cost is divided, and so how much profit lands in this year.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept round so the accounting, not the arithmetic, is the focus.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

The cost flow methods come from Topic 330, Inventory. The Codification does not pick a method for you. It allows several and asks the company to choose the one that most clearly reflects its income, and then to disclose the choice. Boxes labeled "In the FASB Codification" paraphrase the guidance; read the paragraph itself when you need the exact wording.

The allocation problem

Every cost flow method answers the same question: how should the cost of goods available for sale be divided between the units sold and the units still on hand? All the examples in these notes use the same year of activity.

Assumed flow, not physical flow

FIFO, LIFO, and average cost are assumptions about the flow of costs. A company may use LIFO even though it physically ships its oldest stoves first. The methods need not match how the goods actually move.

Specific identification

When each unit can be tracked to its own invoice, the company can use specific identification: cost of goods sold is the actual cost of the actual units sold. It works for unique, expensive items sold in low volume, such as vehicles, jewelry, artwork, or custom-built products. For a store with thousands of identical items it is impractical, and it lets management choose which cost to expense by choosing which identical unit to ship.

Average cost

The average cost method treats the units sold and the units on hand as a mixture of all the units available. Each unit carries the same average cost, weighted by how many units were bought at each price. (A simple average of the unit prices would be wrong.)

Periodic average (weighted average)

Under a periodic system the average is figured once, at year-end:

Weighted-average unit cost

Cost of goods available for sale ÷ Units available for sale. For Lake Glenville: $76,000 ÷ 8,000 stoves = $9.50 per stove.

Periodic average cost
ItemUnitsUnit costTotal
Goods available for sale8,000$9.50$76,000
Less: ending inventory(2,500)9.50(23,750)
Cost of goods sold5,5009.50$52,250

Perpetual average (moving average)

Under a perpetual system the average must be known at every sale, so it is recomputed after each purchase. This moving average is the cost of the units on hand divided by the number of units on hand. Each sale is costed at the average in effect on the sale date.

The average changes from $8.00 to $9.20 to $10.00 as higher-cost stoves arrive. On May 8: ($8,000 + $18,000 + $20,000) ÷ (1,000 + 2,000 + 2,000) = $46,000 ÷ 5,000 = $9.20. On September 12: ($23,000 + $22,000) ÷ 4,500 = $10.00.

Watch out: periodic and perpetual average differ

Periodic average gave cost of goods sold of $52,250; moving average gave $51,000. The early sales under the moving average were costed before the expensive September stoves arrived. The two averages usually produce different numbers.

First-in, first-out (FIFO)

FIFO assumes the first units bought are the first units sold. Cost of goods sold carries the oldest costs, and ending inventory carries the newest costs. For goods that spoil or go out of style, FIFO also tends to match how the goods actually move.

Periodic FIFO

Ending inventory is the 2,500 most recent stoves: all 2,000 from September and 500 from May.

FIFO shortcut

Periodic FIFO and perpetual FIFO always give the same ending inventory and cost of goods sold. You can use whichever is quicker.

Last-in, first-out (LIFO)

LIFO assumes the last units bought are the first units sold. Cost of goods sold carries the newest costs, and ending inventory carries the oldest costs, sometimes costs from many years ago. Almost no business physically sells its goods this way, but GAAP allows the assumption.

Periodic LIFO

The 5,500 stoves sold are assumed to be the last 5,500 bought during the year. Ending inventory is whatever is left from the oldest layers.

Watch out: periodic and perpetual LIFO differ

Periodic LIFO gave cost of goods sold of $55,500; perpetual LIFO gave $54,500. With rising costs, periodic LIFO pulls the year's highest costs into cost of goods sold, so it usually produces the higher cost of goods sold. That is one reason companies that report on LIFO compute it on a periodic basis at year-end, even if they keep perpetual records on FIFO or average cost during the year.

Balance sheet or income statement?

With rising prices, FIFO puts current costs on the balance sheet (ending inventory is close to what it would cost to replace), while LIFO puts current costs on the income statement (cost of goods sold is close to current cost). No single method does both.

How companies choose a method

Over a company's whole life, total cost of goods sold equals total cost of goods bought, whatever the method. The method changes the timing of when costs hit income. Three considerations drive the choice.

1. Physical flow

A company may want its cost flow to resemble how its goods actually move: FIFO for perishable or fashion goods, average cost for goods that are mixed together (fuel in a tank, grain in a silo). This is the least important factor in practice, because no method is required to match physical flow.

2. Income taxes and the LIFO conformity rule

When prices are rising and inventory quantities are steady or growing, LIFO gives the highest cost of goods sold, so the lowest taxable income and the lowest current tax bill. The tax isn't avoided forever, only postponed, but money saved today is worth more than money paid later. That tax saving is the main reason companies choose LIFO.

Lake Glenville's tax saving from LIFO

Assume Lake Glenville's income tax rate is 25%.

LIFO compared with FIFO
ItemAmount
Cost of goods sold under LIFO55,500
Cost of goods sold under FIFO49,000
Lower pretax income under LIFO6,500
Lower income tax this year ($6,500 × 25%)1,625

Federal tax law adds a catch, the LIFO conformity rule: a company that uses LIFO on its tax return must also use LIFO in the financial statements it gives to shareholders and creditors. A LIFO company can't report low income to the IRS and high income to investors. It may, however, disclose supplemental non-LIFO amounts in the notes (see Detailed Notes C).

3. Reported income and matching

The tax saving comes at the price of lower reported net income, which can matter for the share price, for managers' bonuses tied to income, and for loan agreements with income-based covenants. Research suggests managers with income-based bonuses tend to choose methods that raise reported income. Supporters of LIFO also argue it matches current costs with current revenues better, because the most recent costs go to cost of goods sold.

Watch out: LIFO and year-end purchases

Under periodic LIFO, a large purchase late in the year goes straight into cost of goods sold. Management can raise or lower this year's income simply by deciding whether to buy more inventory before year-end. That is one reason analysts view LIFO earnings with some caution.

Mixing methods and staying consistent

  • A company need not use one method for everything. Many use LIFO for some inventories and FIFO or average cost for others, such as LIFO for domestic inventory and FIFO for foreign subsidiaries.
  • A company must disclose the method or methods it uses.
  • Once chosen, a method should be applied consistently. A switch is a change in accounting principle that must be justified as preferable and is reported under Topic 250.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 330-10-30-9 allows cost to be determined under any of several flow assumptions, such as FIFO, average, and LIFO.
  • ASC 330-10-30-10 and 30-11 say the goal is the method that most clearly reflects periodic income, and that the cost matched against a sale need not be the identified cost of the specific item sold.
  • ASC 330-10-50-1 requires disclosure of the basis for stating inventories, including the cost method.
  • ASC 250-10-45-2 allows a voluntary change in accounting principle, such as a change in inventory method, only if the new principle is preferable.

The LIFO conformity rule is a tax rule, found in Section 472(c) of the Internal Revenue Code, not in the FASB Codification.

LIFO and international standards

IFRS does not permit LIFO

Under IAS 2, Inventories, companies may use specific identification, FIFO, or weighted average cost, but not LIFO. Many U.S. multinationals therefore use LIFO for U.S. inventories and FIFO or average cost for foreign operations. Because of the LIFO conformity rule, moving U.S. GAAP to IFRS would force LIFO companies to give up LIFO for taxes too, and to pay tax on years of postponed income. That cost is one of the larger obstacles to adopting IFRS in the United States.

Common mistakes

  • Taking a simple average of unit prices. The average must be weighted by the units bought at each price.
  • Assuming periodic and perpetual always agree. They agree under FIFO only; average cost and LIFO usually differ.
  • Selling units that haven't arrived yet under perpetual LIFO. Each sale can only draw on units on hand at that date.
  • Mixing up which costs go where. FIFO: old costs to cost of goods sold, new costs to inventory. LIFO: the reverse.
  • Thinking the method must match physical flow. It doesn't; these are cost flow assumptions.
  • Saying LIFO avoids taxes. It postpones them while prices and quantities are rising.

Problem 3: Taxes and falling prices

(a) Using Problem 1 and a 25% tax rate, how much less income tax would Franklin pay this year under LIFO than under FIFO? (b) If Franklin's costs had been falling during the year, which method would give the lower tax bill?

Show the answer

(a) LIFO cost of goods sold is $32,100 − $28,700 = $3,400 higher, so pretax income is $3,400 lower and tax is $3,400 × 25% = $850 lower. (b) With falling costs, FIFO puts the older, higher costs into cost of goods sold, so FIFO would give the higher cost of goods sold and the lower tax bill.

Key terms

Cost flow assumption
An assumption about which costs leave with the units sold and which stay with the units on hand.
Specific identification
Assigning each unit its own actual cost; used for unique, high-value items.
Weighted-average unit cost
Cost of goods available for sale divided by units available for sale (periodic average).
Moving average
An average cost recomputed after each purchase in a perpetual system.
First-in, first-out (FIFO)
The assumption that the oldest units are sold first, leaving the newest costs in ending inventory.
Last-in, first-out (LIFO)
The assumption that the newest units are sold first, leaving the oldest costs in ending inventory.
LIFO layer
A quantity of inventory added in a given year and carried at that year's cost under LIFO.
LIFO conformity rule
The tax rule that a company using LIFO for taxes must also use it for external financial reporting.
