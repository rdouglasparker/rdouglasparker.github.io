Acquiring Assets Without Paying Cash Up Front

Four common situations put an asset on the books without an immediate cash payment: signing a note, issuing stock, receiving a donation, and trading in another nonmonetary asset. One rule governs all of them: record the asset at its fair value, using whichever is more clearly determinable, the fair value of what was given up or the fair value of what was received.

Paying later with a note

A note payable is valued at the present value of its future payments. When the note carries a realistic stated interest rate, present value equals face value, and the asset is recorded at that amount.

Case 1 — realistic stated rate

On January 2, 2026, Catawba Valley Kilns bought equipment priced at $72,000 by signing a three-year, 8% note. Interest is paid each December 31, and the principal is due at the end of year three.

A note with no stated interest

If a note says no interest but payment is years away, part of the face amount is really interest. The asset should be recorded at its cash-equivalent price, and the difference between that and the face amount goes into a contra-liability, discount on notes payable, which is amortized into interest expense over the note's life.

Case 2 — fair value of the note is the better evidence

On January 2, 2026, Pine Mountain Cabinetry bought a custom-built edge-banding line. The price was not published. Pine Mountain signed a noninterest-bearing note for $90,000 due December 31, 2028. A bank would have charged 9% on a loan of the same term.

Present value = $90,000 × 0.77218 = $69,496 (present value of $1, n = 3, i = 9%). Discount = $90,000 − $69,496 = $20,504.

Case 3 — fair value of the asset is known

On January 2, 2026, Tessentee Creek Bakery acquired a spiral mixer and signed a noninterest-bearing $125,000 note due December 31, 2028. The dealer's price list shows the mixer sells for $93,914 cash. No rate was stated.

Here the asset's fair value is the stronger evidence, so the mixer is recorded at $93,914 and the discount is $125,000 − $93,914 = $31,086.
The implicit rate comes from $93,914 ÷ $125,000 = 0.75131, which matches the present value factor for n = 3 at 10%. Interest each year is then 10% of the note's carrying amount.

Check yourself

On January 2, 2026, Lakeshore Dental bought a sterilization unit and signed a noninterest-bearing $150,000 note due in two years. The dealer's cash price is $126,000. At what amount is the unit recorded, and what is the discount?

The unit is recorded at the $126,000 cash price. The discount on notes payable is $150,000 − $126,000 = $24,000.

Paying with Stock and Receiving Donated Assets

Assets acquired for stock

This usually happens when a small business incorporates and its owners contribute assets in exchange for shares. If the stock is actively traded, its market value is the best measure of the exchange. If it is not (as with a new private corporation), the appraised value of the asset received is the better measure.

Example — stock for land

On March 31, 2026, Whitewater Falls Kayaks issued 12,500 shares of no-par common stock for a parcel of land. The stock was trading at $26 per share.

12,500 shares × $26 = $325,000. The land is recorded at $325,000 and common stock is credited for the same amount.
Land325,000
Common stock325,000
Check yourself

A founder contributed a patent to Appalachian Sensors in exchange for 40,000 shares of its actively traded common stock. The stock was trading at $31 per share. At what amount is the patent recorded?

40,000 × $31 = $1,240,000.

Donated assets

A donation is usually an incentive: a city or development authority might pay part of a facility's cost to attract an employer. The company records the asset at its fair value (market price or appraisal). This is not a departure from historical cost; it is as if the donor gave cash and the company used it to buy the asset. Generally accepted accounting principles give no specific rules for businesses, so practice follows the guidance for not-for-profit organizations: the contribution is recognized as revenue.

Example — a county helps pay for a building

Blue Ridge Composites agreed to relocate its distribution center to Henderson County. The county agreed to pay 25% of the $8,000,000 construction cost. Blue Ridge paid its share in cash when the building was finished.

Donation = $8,000,000 × 25% = $2,000,000. Blue Ridge pays the remaining $6,000,000.
Building8,000,000
Cash6,000,000
Revenue—donation of asset2,000,000
U.S. GAAP vs. IFRS on government grants: both value the donated asset at fair value, but IFRS does not allow the grant to be recorded as immediate revenue. A company may either (1) subtract the grant from the asset's cost, recording the building above at $6,000,000, or (2) record the building at $8,000,000 and credit a deferred income liability for $2,000,000 that is recognized in income systematically over the asset's useful life.

Exchanges of Nonmonetary Assets

Companies often acquire a new asset by handing over an old one, such as trading in a press for a newer model. Because the two assets rarely have equal value, cash usually changes hands to even things out. When the exchange has commercial substance and fair values can be determined, record it in four steps:

  1. Record the new asset at fair value: the fair value of what was given up plus any cash paid (minus any cash received). The fair value of what was received works equally well if it is clearer.
  2. Remove the old asset by crediting its cost and debiting its accumulated depreciation.
  3. Record the cash paid or received.
  4. Record the gain or loss: fair value of the old asset minus its book value. Fair value above book value is a gain; below is a loss.
Example — a gain (Shoal Creek Printing trades presses)

Shoal Creek traded in a press with a cost of $640,000 and accumulated depreciation of $500,000 (book value $140,000). The old press had a fair value of $185,000. Shoal Creek also paid $310,000 cash for the new press.

New press = $185,000 + $310,000 = $495,000.
Gain = $185,000 fair value − $140,000 book value = $45,000.

When fair value cannot be determined

If neither asset's fair value can be reliably measured, fair value cannot support the new asset's recorded amount. The new asset is recorded at the book value of the asset given plus cash paid, and no gain or loss is recognized. In the first example above, that would be $140,000 + $310,000 = $450,000.

Equipment—new450,000
Accumulated depreciation—old500,000
Equipment—old640,000
Cash310,000

When the exchange lacks commercial substance

An exchange has commercial substance when it is expected to change the company's future cash flows, for example by trading for a newer model that raises output or cuts costs. The rule exists to stop companies from swapping essentially identical assets just to book a gain.

  • Gain situation: fair value cannot be used to record the gain. The new asset is recorded at the old asset's book value plus cash paid (minus cash received), and no gain is recognized.
  • Loss situation: the loss is recognized in full, and the new asset is recorded using fair value, just as in the commercial-substance case.
Example — identical land swapped, no commercial substance

Linville Gorge Vineyards traded a tract of land (book value $1,800,000, fair value $3,200,000) for a nearly identical tract owned by another grower and paid $350,000 cash.

New land = $1,800,000 + $350,000 = $2,150,000. The $1,400,000 unrealized gain is not recognized.
Land—new2,150,000
Land—old1,800,000
Cash350,000

Receiving cash in an exchange that lacks commercial substance

A gain may be partly recognized when the company receives cash, because part of the deal is then a sale. The recognized gain is:

Recognized gain = (FV given − BV given) × (Cash received ÷ Total fair value received)

If the cash received is at least 25% of the total fair value received, the entire gain is recognized. The new asset's amount is the book value given, minus cash received, plus the gain recognized.

Example — land swap with cash received

A company trades land (book value $1,800,000, fair value $3,200,000) for different land worth $2,800,000 and receives $400,000 in cash. The exchange lacks commercial substance.

Cash received is $400,000 ÷ $3,200,000 = 12.5% of total fair value received, less than 25%, so only part of the gain is recognized.
Recognized gain = ($3,200,000 − $1,800,000) × 12.5% = $175,000.
New land = $1,800,000 − $400,000 + $175,000 = $1,575,000.

Carolina Courier traded in a delivery van (book value $18,000, fair value $21,000) for a new van and paid $26,000 cash. The exchange has commercial substance. At what amount is the new van recorded, and what gain is recognized?

New van = $21,000 + $26,000 = $47,000.
Gain = $21,000 − $18,000 = $3,000.

Decision Makers' Perspective

Deciding whether to acquire

Buying long-lived assets is among management's biggest decisions, often called capital budgeting. Managers forecast the net cash the asset will generate and compare it with what it costs. Under the net present value approach, the asset is worth buying when the present value of those future net cash flows is greater than the initial acquisition cost.

Measuring how well the assets are used

Analysts want to know how much revenue a company squeezes out of its PP&E. The fixed-asset turnover ratio answers that question.

Fixed-asset turnover = Net sales ÷ Average fixed assets (net carrying value)

Average fixed assets is the beginning and ending carrying value added together and divided by two. A higher ratio means more sales per dollar invested in fixed assets. As with other turnover ratios, compare it with competitors, an industry average, or the same company's history.
