A puzzle to start

Great Smokies Fabrication needs a new press brake but is short on cash. The seller offers a deal: take the machine today and pay $60,000 in two years, with no interest charged. Is the machine worth $60,000? Of course not: money due in two years is worth less than money in hand. The machine’s cost is the cash-equivalent price, and the difference is interest.

Part A assumed the company paid cash. Often it does not. Assets are acquired by signing notes, issuing shares, receiving gifts, or trading in an old asset. In every case the question is the same: what is the fair value of what was given up (or of the asset received), measured as if cash had changed hands?

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

Initial measurement of PP&E is in Topic 360-10-30. Interest on receivables and payables is in Topic 835-30. Exchanges are in Topic 845, Nonmonetary Transactions. Gifts and donations are in Topic 958-605. Under IFRS, government grants are in IAS 20.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 835-30: a note exchanged for property is recorded at the present value of its payments when the stated rate is absent or unreasonable; the difference is interest.
  • ASC 845-10-30: an exchange of nonmonetary assets is generally measured at fair value, with exceptions when fair value is not determinable or the exchange lacks commercial substance.
  • ASC 845-10-25-6: if the monetary consideration in an exchange is significant (25% or more of the exchange’s fair value), the exchange is treated as monetary and the whole gain is recognized.
  • ASC 958-605: a donated asset is recorded at fair value, and the donee recognizes revenue.

Noninterest-bearing notes

When a note carries no stated interest (or an unreasonably low rate), the interest is still there; it is hidden in the price. Accounting makes it visible by recording the asset at the present value of the note’s payments, using the interest rate the company would pay to borrow for a similar term. The difference between the face amount and the present value is a discount that becomes interest expense over the life of the note using the effective interest method.

Two ways to write the entry

The gross method records the note at face value and the discount in a contra-liability account, Discount on note payable. The net method records the note directly at its present value. Both give the same net liability and the same interest expense; the notes below use the gross method.

When the rate is not given: the implicit rate

Sometimes the cash price is known but the rate is not. Then the rate is the one that makes the present value of the note equal the cash price. This is the implicit rate.

Nantahala Outfitters: finding the implicit rate

Nantahala Outfitters buys a delivery van with a $100,000 note due in four years. The same van sells for $68,301 cash. What rate is built into the note?

Present value factor = $68,301 ÷ $100,000 = 0.6830. In the present value of $1 table for 4 periods, that factor is 10%, so the implicit rate is 10%. The van is recorded at $68,301 and the $31,699 difference is interest to be recognized over four years.

Purchase of the van

Journal entry: Purchase of the van
AccountDebitCredit
Vehicles68,301
Discount on note payable31,699
Note payable100,000

Do not record the face amount as the cost

Recording the van at $100,000 overstates the asset and understates interest expense. The asset’s cost never includes the financing charge.

Assets acquired with shares

A company may pay for an asset by issuing its own shares. Cost is the fair value of what was given up or of the asset received, whichever is more clearly evident. If the shares trade actively, the market price is usually the best evidence. If the shares are newly issued and thinly traded, an appraisal of the asset is better.

Cullowhee Biotech: shares for equipment

Cullowhee Biotech issues 6,000 shares of $1 par common stock for lab equipment. The shares trade at $22.

Fair value of shares = 6,000 × $22 = $132,000.

Donated assets and government grants

Sometimes a city or county gives a company land, or a building, to persuade it to locate there. The company has not paid anything, but it has received something valuable, and it has agreed (explicitly or not) to bring jobs and activity. U.S. GAAP treats the asset as revenue received at fair value.

IFRS: government grants

Under IFRS (IAS 20), a grant related to an asset is not simply recorded as revenue when received. A company may choose one of two approaches:

  • Deduct the grant from the asset’s cost. Depreciation is then lower each year.
  • Record the grant as deferred income (a liability) and recognize it in income over the asset’s life, matching it to the depreciation.

For example, a $90,000 grant toward a $450,000 machine gives a cost of $360,000 under the first approach, or a $450,000 machine with $90,000 of deferred income under the second.

Nonmonetary exchanges: the four steps

A nonmonetary asset exchange swaps one productive asset for another, often with cash to even things out. The usual case is a trade-in. The general rule is to measure the new asset at fair value, remove the old asset at book value, record any cash, and recognize the resulting gain or loss.

  1. Record the new asset at fair value Use the fair value of what was given up, or of what was received, whichever is more clearly evident.
  2. Remove the book value of the old asset Credit the old asset’s cost and debit its accumulated depreciation.
  3. Record cash paid or received Cash makes up the difference in fair values.
  4. Record the gain or loss Gain or loss = fair value of the old asset − its book value.

Tuckasegee Printing: a trade-in at a gain

Tuckasegee Printing trades an old press (cost $300,000, accumulated depreciation $220,000, so book value $80,000) and pays $150,000 cash for a new one. The old press has a fair value of $95,000.

A shortcut check

Gain or loss also equals the recorded amount of the asset received minus the book value of everything given. Here: $245,000 − ($80,000 + $150,000) = $15,000 gain.

Two exceptions: no fair value, and no commercial substance

Fair value is the rule, but two situations move the new asset to book value and prevent a gain.

Fair value cannot be determined

If neither asset’s fair value can be reasonably determined (rare), the new asset is recorded at the book value of the old asset plus cash paid. No gain or loss is recognized.

Tuckasegee Printing: no fair value available

New press = book value $80,000 + cash $150,000 = $230,000.

The exchange lacks commercial substance

An exchange has commercial substance when future cash flows will change as a result of it. Trading old equipment for faster equipment changes cash flows. Trading a tract of land for an identical tract does not. If there is no commercial substance, a gain is not recognized, because the company has not changed its economic position; it is simply swapping like for like. Otherwise a company could create income by trading assets back and forth.

Pisgah Land Company: swapping similar tracts

Pisgah Land trades a tract with a book value of $1,200,000 and a fair value of $2,000,000 for a nearly identical tract, and pays $100,000 cash. The exchange lacks commercial substance.

Exchange without commercial substance

Journal entry: Exchange without commercial substance
AccountDebitCredit
Land (new)1,300,000
Land (old)1,200,000
Cash100,000

The new land is recorded at $1,200,000 + $100,000 = $1,300,000. The unrecognized $800,000 gain is embedded in a lower book value for the new land.

Cash received without commercial substance

When cash is received, part of the transaction is a sale, and that part of the gain is recognized. The recognized gain is the total gain times cash received ÷ (cash received + fair value of the asset received), which equals cash divided by the fair value of the asset given.

Pisgah Land Company: cash received

Pisgah Land trades the same tract (book value $1,200,000, fair value $2,000,000) and receives $90,000 cash. The fair value of the land received is $1,910,000.

Share = $90,000 ÷ ($90,000 + $1,910,000) = 4.5%. Gain recognized = ($2,000,000 − $1,200,000) × 4.5% = $36,000. New land = $1,200,000 + $36,000 − $90,000 = $1,146,000.

Two special cases

Losses are always recognized, even without commercial substance, because conservatism does not allow carrying an asset above its fair value. And if the cash is 25% or more of the exchange’s fair value, the exchange is treated as monetary and the entire gain is recognized as if it had commercial substance.

Fixed-asset turnover

Analysts want to know how effectively a company uses its property, plant, and equipment to produce sales. The fixed-asset turnover ratio divides net sales by average net property, plant, and equipment. A higher ratio means each dollar of PP&E generates more sales, so the company is using its assets efficiently.

Highlands Bike Works

Net sales are $4,800,000. Net PP&E was $1,150,000 at the beginning of the year and $1,250,000 at the end.

Average net PP&E = ($1,150,000 + $1,250,000) ÷ 2 = $1,200,000. Turnover = $4,800,000 ÷ $1,200,000 = 4.0 times.

Compare the ratio with prior years and with competitors in the same industry. Capital-intensive industries naturally have lower turnover than retailers.

Common mistakes

  • Recording an asset bought with a zero-interest note at its face amount. Use the present value.
  • Computing interest on the face amount. Interest is the carrying amount (face minus discount) times the effective rate.
  • Forgetting that cash is part of what was given up when finding the cost of the new asset in a trade-in.
  • Recognizing a gain on an exchange that lacks commercial substance. Use book value plus cash paid.
  • Treating a loss like a gain. Losses are recognized even without commercial substance.
  • Using the wrong gain fraction when cash is received. The numerator is cash received; the denominator is the total consideration received (cash plus asset).

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: Noninterest-bearing note

Balsam Mountain Foods buys a freezer by signing a $90,000 noninterest-bearing note due in three years. Borrowing costs 10%. At what amount is the freezer recorded?

Show the answer

PV = $90,000 × 0.75131 = $67,618. The discount is $22,382. Year 1 interest = 10% × $67,618 = $6,762.

Problem 2: Implicit rate

A truck costs $41,500 cash or a $60,000 note due in 5 years with no interest. What is the implicit rate?

Show the answer

Factor = 41,500 ÷ 60,000 = 0.6917. In the PV table (n = 5) this is 8%. The truck is recorded at $41,500.

Problem 3: Trade-in with a gain

Old equipment: cost $250,000, accumulated depreciation $180,000, fair value $85,000. The company pays $40,000 cash for new equipment. Record the exchange.

Show the answer

New equipment = $85,000 + $40,000 = $125,000. Book value = $70,000, so gain = $85,000 − $70,000 = $15,000. Debit Equipment (new) $125,000, Accumulated depreciation $180,000; credit Equipment (old) $250,000, Cash $40,000, Gain $15,000.

Problem 4: No commercial substance

Land with a book value of $600,000 and fair value of $900,000 is traded for similar land, and $50,000 cash is received (fair value of land received: $850,000). The exchange lacks commercial substance. What gain is recognized and at what amount is the new land recorded?

Show the answer

Cash is $50,000 of $900,000 (5.6%, under 25%). Gain = $300,000 × $50,000 ÷ ($50,000 + $850,000) = $16,667. New land = $600,000 + $16,667 − $50,000 = $566,667.

Key terms

Cash-equivalent price
The price the asset would have sold for in an immediate cash sale.
Discount on note payable
A contra-liability equal to the face amount minus the present value; becomes interest expense.
Implicit interest rate
The rate that equates the present value of a note’s payments to the asset’s cash price.
Gross method
Records a note at its face amount with a separate discount account.
Net method
Records a note directly at its present value.
Nonmonetary exchange
A swap of productive assets, often with some cash.
Commercial substance
Exists when an exchange is expected to change the company’s future cash flows.
Fixed-asset turnover
Net sales divided by average net property, plant, and equipment.
