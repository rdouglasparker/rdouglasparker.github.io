A puzzle to start

Two companies each report net income of $62 million. Cullowhee Creek Financial has about 30 million common shares; a rival has 60 million. Which earned more for each owner? And if Cullowhee Creek sold new shares in May, issued a stock dividend in August, and bought back shares in November, how many shares should the $62 million be divided by?

Earnings per share (EPS) is the single accounting number that gets the most attention from investors, analysts, and the financial press. It condenses a company’s performance into one figure that can be compared across companies of very different sizes. Because comparability is the whole point, the rules for computing EPS are detailed and, in places, deliberately uniform even where another choice could also have been defended.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. One company, Cullowhee Creek Financial, is used throughout so you can see how each new complication changes the same calculation. Per-share amounts are rounded to the cent.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

EPS is governed by Topic 260, Earnings per Share. The requirements apply to public companies; private companies are not required to report EPS. Most of what you need is in two sections of subtopic 260-10: 45, Other Presentation Matters (how to compute and present EPS) and 50, Disclosure.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-10 defines basic EPS as income available to common shareholders divided by the weighted-average number of common shares outstanding during the period.
  • ASC 260-10-45-2 requires basic and diluted EPS on the face of the income statement for public companies with complex capital structures.

Simple and complex capital structures

Some securities are not common shares today but could become common shares later: stock options, warrants, restricted stock, convertible bonds, convertible preferred stock, and contingently issuable shares. These are potential common shares. If they became common shares, earnings would be spread over more shares, so they could dilute (reduce) EPS.

Basic EPS in one line

Basic EPS = (Net income − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted-average common shares outstanding. Basic EPS ignores potential common shares completely. These notes build the numerator and denominator one piece at a time; Detailed Notes C adds the effect of potential common shares to get diluted EPS.

The denominator: weighted-average common shares

The basic setting is simple. Cullowhee Creek Financial had 20,000,000 common shares outstanding all year and earned $62,000,000, so EPS is $62,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000 = $3.10. Things get interesting when the number of shares changes during the year.

Shares sold during the year

New shares are time-weighted by the fraction of the year they were outstanding. The reason: cash from the sale is available to generate income only after the shares are sold, so the shares in the denominator should match the period the income in the numerator was earned.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: new shares

Cullowhee Creek had 20,000,000 shares outstanding on January 1 and sold 6,000,000 new shares on May 1. Net income was $62,000,000.

Stock dividends and stock splits

A stock dividend or split adds shares without bringing in any new resources. The same pie is cut into more slices. So the new shares are not time-weighted. Instead, the shares outstanding before the distribution are retroactively restated as if the distribution had happened at the beginning of the year. A 25% stock dividend multiplies earlier shares by 1.25; a 2-for-1 split multiplies them by 2.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: a 25% stock dividend

Add a 25% stock dividend distributed on August 1. Both the January 1 shares and the May 1 shares were outstanding before the distribution, so both are multiplied by 1.25.

Weighted-average shares with a stock dividend

Basic EPS = $62,000,000 ÷ 30,000,000 = $2.07.

This drop is not dilution. Each shareholder simply holds more, less valuable shares; no one’s share of earnings changed. The restatement also applies to prior years shown for comparison. If last year’s EPS was $47,000,000 ÷ 19,000,000 = $2.47, it is restated in this year’s comparative income statement as $47,000,000 ÷ (19,000,000 × 1.25) = $1.98, so both years are measured in the same “new” shares.

Shares reacquired during the year

Shares bought back, whether retired or held as treasury stock, are time-weighted for the fraction of the year they were not outstanding and subtracted.

Cullowhee Creek Financial: treasury shares

Add a purchase of 3,000,000 treasury shares on November 1. They were bought after the August 1 stock dividend, so they are already post-dividend shares and are not multiplied by 1.25.

The timing rule for stock distributions

A stock dividend or split increases every share transaction that happened before it (beginning shares, shares sold, shares reacquired). Share transactions after the distribution are already in “new” shares and are not adjusted. A distribution after year-end but before the statements are issued is also applied retroactively.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-55-12 and related guidance restate the weighted-average shares retroactively for stock dividends, stock splits, and reverse splits, including those that occur after the period ends but before the financial statements are issued, for all periods presented.

The numerator: earnings available to common shareholders

The denominator counts only common shares, so the numerator should be only the earnings that belong to common shareholders. When preferred shareholders are entitled to a dividend first, that dividend is subtracted from net income.

Add 1,000,000 shares of 6%, $50 par, cumulative, nonconvertible preferred stock outstanding all year. The annual preferred dividend is 1,000,000 × $50 × 6% = $3,000,000.

Cullowhee Creek still has a simple capital structure, because none of its securities could become common shares. It reports a single EPS figure: $2.00.

Cumulative preferred: subtract the dividend even if none was declared

Cumulative dividends that are not declared this year accumulate and will be paid before common shareholders get anything in the future. So this year’s cumulative dividend is subtracted whether or not it was declared. Paying last year’s arrears this year does not reduce this year’s numerator again.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 260-10-45-11 deducts dividends declared on preferred stock, and dividends accumulated for the period on cumulative preferred stock (whether or not earned), from income to arrive at income available to common shareholders.

A convenient layout is the one used throughout these notes: list the beginning shares, then each change in date order, multiply each by its fraction of the year and any later stock distribution factor, and add the column.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

  • Price-earnings (P/E) ratio. Market price per share ÷ EPS. It shows how much the market is willing to pay for a dollar of current earnings. A low ratio may reflect temporarily high earnings rather than low expectations, and a high ratio may reflect temporarily depressed earnings.
  • Dividend payout ratio. Dividends per common share ÷ EPS. Low payouts are common in growth industries that reinvest; high payouts are common in mature industries.
  • Buybacks and the denominator. Buying back shares lowers the weighted-average shares and raises EPS even if net income does not change. A company whose EPS growth routinely exceeds its net income growth may be using buybacks to manage EPS.
  • Pro forma EPS. Companies sometimes report “pro forma” or “adjusted” EPS that leaves out expenses they consider unusual. These figures always look better than GAAP EPS. Find out exactly what was excluded.

Common mistakes

  • Time-weighting a stock dividend or split. Restate the earlier shares instead, as if the distribution happened at the start of the year.
  • Adjusting shares bought or sold after a stock dividend. Only transactions before the distribution are multiplied by the factor.
  • Weighting treasury shares for the time they were held. Weight them for the time they were not outstanding, and subtract.
  • Ignoring undeclared cumulative preferred dividends. Subtract the current year’s cumulative dividend whether declared or not.
  • Subtracting dividends in arrears paid this year. They were already subtracted in the years they accumulated.
  • Forgetting to restate prior-year EPS for a stock dividend or split in the comparative statements.

Problem 1: A full year of share changes

Scotts Creek Logistics had 12,000,000 common shares outstanding on January 1. It sold 3,000,000 shares on April 1, distributed a 2-for-1 stock split on June 1, and bought 1,200,000 treasury shares on September 1. Net income was $45,000,000. There is no preferred stock. Compute basic EPS.

Problem 2: Preferred dividends

Caney Fork Builders earned $9,000,000. It has 200,000 shares of 7%, $100 par preferred stock outstanding and 4,000,000 weighted-average common shares. No dividends were declared this year. Compute basic EPS if the preferred is (a) cumulative and (b) noncumulative.

Show the answer

Annual preferred dividend = 200,000 × $100 × 7% = $1,400,000. (a) Cumulative: ($9,000,000 − $1,400,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = $1.90. (b) Noncumulative, none declared: $9,000,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = $2.25.

Problem 3: Restating last year

Last year Savannah Ridge Foods reported EPS of $3.30 ($33,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 shares). This year it distributed a 10% stock dividend. What prior-year EPS appears in this year’s comparative income statement?

Show the answer

$33,000,000 ÷ (10,000,000 × 1.10) = $33,000,000 ÷ 11,000,000 = $3.00.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

IAS 33, Earnings per Share, and Topic 260 are the result of a joint effort by the IASB and the FASB, so basic EPS is computed the same way under both. The few remaining differences involve diluted EPS and are covered in Detailed Notes C.

Key terms

Earnings per share (EPS)
Earnings available to common shareholders for each common share outstanding.
Simple capital structure
A capital structure with no potential common shares; only basic EPS is reported.
Complex capital structure
A capital structure with potential common shares; both basic and diluted EPS are reported.
Potential common shares
Securities that could become common shares: options, warrants, rights, restricted stock, convertible securities, and contingently issuable shares.
Dilution
A reduction in EPS from the assumed issuance of additional common shares.
Basic EPS
Earnings available to common shareholders ÷ weighted-average common shares outstanding; ignores potential common shares.
Weighted-average common shares
The number of common shares outstanding, with each change weighted by the fraction of the period it was in effect.
Retroactive restatement
Treating a stock dividend or split as if it occurred at the start of the earliest period presented.
Earnings available to common shareholders
Net income minus preferred dividends (declared, or accumulated for the period if cumulative).
Price-earnings ratio
Market price per share ÷ earnings per share.
Dividend payout ratio
Dividends per common share ÷ earnings per share.
