Reading the Codification references

These notes point to the guidance in ASC Topic 740, Income Taxes, in the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, the authoritative source of U.S. GAAP. Boxes labeled “In the FASB Codification” paraphrase the guidance. For exact wording, read the cited paragraph.

A citation runs from topic to subtopic to section to paragraph. For example, ASC 740-10-35-4 is Topic 740 (Income Taxes), Subtopic 10 (Overall), Section 35 (Subsequent measurement), Paragraph 4. Common sections are 20 (glossary), 25 (recognition), 30 (initial measurement), 35 (subsequent measurement), 45 (other presentation matters), 50 (disclosure), and 55 (implementation guidance and illustrations). Detailed Notes A has a longer guide.

Permanent differences

Not every gap between book income and taxable income closes by itself. A permanent difference comes from an item that, under current tax law, will never be reported on the tax return the way it is on the income statement, or never at all. The tax-free interest on a municipal bond is the classic case. It appears as revenue on the income statement, but it never appears in taxable income, now or later.

The contrast with a temporary difference is the heart of this topic:

Temporary differences compared with permanent differences
ItemTemporary differencePermanent difference
Does it reverse?Yes. The same total ends up on both reports over time.No. The item is on one report only.
Effect on future taxesCreates future taxable or deductible amounts.None.
Deferred tax asset or liability?Yes.No.
Effect on the effective tax rateNone over the life of the item.Moves the effective rate away from the statutory rate.

Check the rules in your problem

Which items are permanent depends on the tax law in force. Provisions come and go, so rely on what a problem tells you about whether an item is taxable or deductible.

In the FASB Codification

  • The Codification builds its recognition model around temporary differences (ASC 740-10-20, ASC 740-10-25-23). A company records deferred taxes for all temporary differences and carryforwards, subject to a few specific exceptions (ASC 740-10-25-29, ASC 740-10-25-3). Items that will never produce taxable or deductible amounts in a later year fall outside that model. Practice calls them permanent differences.
  • They show up in the rate reconciliation that companies disclose (ASC 740-10-50-12), where items such as tax-exempt income and nondeductible expenses explain why the effective rate differs from the statutory rate.
  • A related special rule: ASC 740-30-25-18 contains an exception that can let a company skip deferred taxes on some undistributed earnings of foreign subsidiaries that it plans to reinvest abroad indefinitely.

How to account for them

Permanent differences are easier than temporary ones. Because the item never reverses, there is nothing to defer, so no deferred tax asset or liability is recorded. The procedure is:

  1. Start from pretax accounting income.
  2. Remove permanent differences when you compute taxable income. Subtract revenue that will never be taxed. Add back expenses that will never be deductible.
  3. Multiply taxable income by the tax rate. That is tax payable, and (with no temporary differences) it is also income tax expense.

The name can confuse. A permanent difference is a difference between taxable income and pretax accounting income. It is not a difference between tax expense and tax payable. Those two are equal as far as permanent differences are concerned.

Permanent and temporary differences together

Real companies have both kinds in the same year. Handle them in one pass: take pretax income, add back or subtract the permanent differences (they are gone for good), and then adjust for temporary differences (they get a deferred tax entry). Only the temporary differences feed the deferred tax balances.

Balsam Mountain Metalworks

The effective tax rate

The statutory rate is the rate written into law. The effective tax rate is what the company actually bears:

Effective tax rate = income tax expense ÷ pretax accounting income

Pretax accounting income is the denominator, so permanent differences move the effective rate. A permanent difference that lowers taxable income, such as tax-free interest, pulls the effective rate below the statutory rate. A permanent difference that raises taxable income, such as a nondeductible fine, pushes it above. Temporary differences do not change the effective rate over time, because deferred taxes make expense line up with book income.

Public companies must publish a table like these, called the rate reconciliation, which starts at the statutory rate and walks to the effective rate item by item. It shows readers which permanent differences, tax credits, and other items matter most. A company that earns income abroad in low-tax places, for example, will usually show a lower effective rate. If it later decides to bring those earnings home and pay the extra tax, the effective rate rises that year, even though pretax income does not change.

In the FASB Codification: rate reconciliation

  • ASC 740-10-50-12 through ASC 740-10-50-14 require a reconciliation between reported income tax expense and the expense that would result from applying the statutory federal rate to pretax income from continuing operations. Public business entities report it in detail. Other entities describe the nature of significant reconciling items.
  • Recent change. FASB Accounting Standards Update (ASU) 2023-09, issued in December 2023, amended the requirement for public business entities. They now present the reconciliation as a table in both dollars and percentages, using prescribed categories (for example, state and local taxes, tax credits, changes in valuation allowances, and nontaxable or nondeductible items), and they disclose income taxes paid by jurisdiction. The update applies to public business entities for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2024, and to other entities for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2025.
  • The permanent differences in these notes, such as tax-exempt interest and nondeductible fines, belong in the “nontaxable or nondeductible items” part of such a reconciliation.

The two small reconciliations in this section use the simple format. A real public-company table now follows the prescribed categories.

Which tax rate to use

To measure a deferred tax liability or asset, multiply the temporary difference by the enacted tax rate that will apply in the year or years it reverses. Accountants could try to forecast future rates, but standard setters chose to wait until a rate change is actually law. That choice favors reliability over relevance: a law that has passed is a fact, while a proposed one is a guess.

In the FASB Codification: which rate to use

  • ASC 740-10-30-2: current and deferred taxes are measured under the tax law as enacted. Possible future changes in tax laws or rates are not factored in.
  • ASC 740-10-30-5(b) and ASC 740-10-30-5(c): the deferred tax liability and the deferred tax asset are found by applying the applicable tax rate to the totals. That rate is the enacted rate expected to apply when the temporary differences reverse.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

IFRS (IAS 12, paragraphs 47 through 49) measures deferred taxes using rates that are enacted or substantively enacted by the end of the reporting period. In some jurisdictions an announcement by the government has the effect of enactment, and IFRS then uses the announced rate. U.S. GAAP uses only enacted rates. IFRS also uses an average rate when different rates apply to different levels of taxable income.

When enacted rates differ from year to year

Sometimes a law already on the books schedules different rates for different future years. Then multiply each year’s reversing amount by that year’s rate, and add the results.

When tax rates change

The case above involves rates that were already scheduled. A different situation arises when lawmakers change the rate that will apply in the future. Deferred tax accounts exist to show what will be paid or saved later. When the law changes how much that is, the accounts must be restated. The rule:

Key idea

When a new rate is enacted, remeasure the existing deferred tax assets and liabilities at the new rate. The whole adjustment goes through income tax expense in the period of enactment. It does not wait for the differences to reverse.

Tax payable for the year of enactment is unaffected, because the current year’s return is figured at the rate in force for that year. Only the deferred balances change, so the plug in the journal entry is the entire effect.

In the FASB Codification: changes in tax laws or rates

  • ASC 740-10-25-47: a change in tax laws or rates enters the financial statements on the date it is enacted.
  • ASC 740-10-35-4: existing deferred tax balances are remeasured for the new law or rate, and management should also reconsider whether the valuation allowance is still right.
  • ASC 740-10-45-15: the effect is reported in income from continuing operations for the period that contains the enactment date, even when the deferred tax was first recorded elsewhere, such as in other comprehensive income.
  • ASC 740-10-50-9: the adjustment is disclosed as its own component of income tax expense.

The enactment date matters. For U.S. federal legislation, the enactment date is the date the President signs the bill. A company records the effect in the reporting period that contains that date, even if the new rate does not take effect until later. Two companies with different year-ends can therefore record the same law change in different fiscal years.

A rate cut and a deferred tax liability

Western Highlands Brewing Company has future taxable amounts of $800,000. Under a combined rate of 35% its deferred tax liability is $280,000. New legislation lowers the future combined rate to 25%, so the liability should now be $200,000.

The deductions will save less tax than expected, so the asset shrinks by $50,000, and net income falls by the same amount.

Effect of a change in the future tax rate on income
ItemRate decreasesRate increases
Company with a net deferred tax liabilityLiability falls; net income rises.Liability grows; net income falls.
Company with a net deferred tax assetAsset falls; net income falls.Asset grows; net income rises.

This is why a lower tax rate, which sounds like good news, can hurt reported earnings in the year it is enacted. When the U.S. federal corporate rate dropped from 35% to 21% beginning in 2018, companies with large net deferred tax assets, such as banks with big loss carryforwards, recorded big charges, while companies with large net deferred tax liabilities, such as capital-intensive firms, recorded big gains. Neither group had earned or lost anything operationally. They simply revalued tax balances.

Several temporary differences at once

Having many temporary differences adds bookkeeping but no new principles. Sort every temporary difference into one of two groups, then total each group:

  • Differences that leave future taxable amounts: total them and multiply by the enacted rate to get the deferred tax liability.
  • Differences that leave future deductible amounts: total them and multiply by the enacted rate to get the deferred tax asset.

Keep the two totals separate. They produce two different accounts, and the four-step process then runs as before. If different rates are scheduled for different years, total each group year by year, apply that year’s rate, and add up the taxes.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

Both frameworks account for deferred taxes in a similar way, but reported deferred taxes still differ often, because many non-tax accounting differences flow through to deferred taxes. For example, IFRS records a loss contingency at a lower probability threshold than U.S. GAAP. A loss accrued under IFRS but not under U.S. GAAP creates a deferred tax asset under IFRS only.

Common mistakes

  • Recording deferred tax on a permanent difference. If the item will never be taxed or deducted, there is nothing to defer.
  • Adjusting in the wrong direction. Subtract revenue that will never be taxed. Add back expenses that will never be deductible.
  • Saying expense is 25% of pretax income when permanent differences exist. It is 25% of pretax income after permanent differences are removed.
  • Using a rate that has not been enacted. Use the enacted rates only, even if everyone expects a change.
  • Spreading a rate change over future years. The effect is recognized in full, in the year of enactment.
  • Mixing up the effect of a rate cut on a liability and an asset. A cut helps a net liability position (income rises) and hurts a net asset position (income falls).
  • Netting differences before multiplying. Keep future taxable and future deductible amounts in separate totals so the liability and the asset are separately measured.

Key terms

Permanent difference
A difference between pretax accounting income and taxable income caused by an item that will never be taxed or never be deductible. It has no deferred tax effect.
Statutory tax rate
The tax rate written into law.
Effective tax rate
Income tax expense divided by pretax accounting income.
Rate reconciliation
A disclosure that starts from the statutory rate and shows each item that moves the company to its effective rate.
Enacted tax rate
A rate that has been signed into law and is scheduled to apply. Deferred taxes are measured using enacted rates.
Remeasurement
Restating deferred tax assets and liabilities at a new enacted rate; the effect goes through income tax expense in the year of enactment.
Net deferred tax liability (asset) position
The total of a company’s deferred tax liabilities less its deferred tax assets (or the reverse); the direction determines whether a rate cut helps or hurts income.
Future taxable amounts
Amounts that will increase taxable income when temporary differences reverse; multiplied by the rate they give the deferred tax liability.
Future deductible amounts
Amounts that will reduce taxable income when temporary differences reverse; multiplied by the rate they give the deferred tax asset.
