Investments in Debt Securities

Reading Notes

What a Debt Investment Looks Like

A bond (or note, or Treasury security) is a promise: the issuer will pay the holder a stated amount of interest on specified dates and return the principal (also called the face amount or maturity value) on the maturity date. A company that buys another entity's debt is lending money, so the question for the investor is how to account for a purchase, interest, changes in value while it holds the security, and a sale or maturity.

Four events drive the accounting for every debt investment: (1) purchase, (2) receiving interest, (3) holding the investment while its fair value changes, and (4) selling it or collecting the principal at maturity. Events 1 and 2 are accounted for the same way no matter what. Events 3 and 4 depend on how the investment is classified.

Pricing the Investment and Recording the Purchase

An investor pays what the future cash flows are worth today, using the market interest rate for debt of similar risk and maturity, not the rate printed on the bond.

Price = PV of the periodic interest payments + PV of the principal, both discounted at the market rate

When interest is paid semiannually, use half the annual rate and twice the number of years.

Stated rate > market rate → investors pay more than face value (a premium).

Stated rate < market rate → investors pay less than face value (a discount).

Recording Interest Revenue: The Effective Interest Method

Each period, the investor receives a fixed amount of cash but earns a return on the amount it has invested. Interest revenue is based on the market rate at the date of purchase and the investment's current amortized cost (face amount less unamortized discount, or plus unamortized premium).

Interest revenue = Market rate (per period) × Beginning amortized cost
Cash received = Stated rate (per period) × Face amount
Amortization = Interest revenue − Cash received

For Catamount's first period: 5% × $474,624 = $23,731 of revenue, but only $20,000 of cash. The $3,731 difference is a piece of the discount that has now been earned.

Three Classifications of Debt Investments

The company classifies each debt investment based on what it intends to do with it. The classification determines how unrealized holding gains and losses (changes in fair value of securities that haven't been sold) are reported.

Why do fair values change? A bond's fair value is the present value of its remaining cash flows at the current market rate. When market rates rise, fair value falls; when market rates fall, fair value rises. The investor's amortization schedule does not change; it stays based on the rate at purchase.

Amortized cost at that date is $478,355, so there is an unrealized holding gain of $489,025 − $478,355 = $10,670. The three classifications treat that gain three different ways.

Held-to-Maturity (HTM)

If the investor has the positive intent and ability to hold a debt security until it matures, sale before maturity isn't a real alternative, so day-to-day fair value changes are not very relevant. HTM investments stay at amortized cost and fair value changes are ignored (only disclosed in the notes).

A security can't be HTM if the investor might sell it in response to changes in market prices or interest rates, or to meet liquidity needs.

The "tainting" rule: selling an HTM security for reasons other than a narrow list of unforeseen events calls the company's original intent into question. The company can be required to reclassify all its HTM securities as AFS and avoid the HTM category for two years.

If an HTM investment is sold early

It's like selling any other asset: compare the cash received with the carrying amount (amortized cost). In the running example, suppose Catamount sells on January 5, 2027, for $498,000 (ignoring a few days of accrued interest). The investment account is $500,000 and the unamortized discount is $21,645:

Credit losses

HTM is the one place where something resembling an unrealized loss is recognized. Under the Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) model, the investor estimates the interest and principal it does not expect to collect, records a credit loss in net income, and reduces the carrying amount with an allowance for credit losses, just like bad debt expense and the allowance for uncollectible accounts.

Trading Securities

Trading securities are bought and sold for short-term profit (holding periods are measured in days or hours, not years), so they're reported at fair value and the changes flow through net income as they occur. That makes management's success at timing purchases and sales visible in the income statement.

The investment account keeps its amortized cost. A valuation account, Fair value adjustment, is adjusted each period so that cost plus (or minus) the adjustment equals fair value.

Required fair value adjustment = Fair value − Amortized cost
Entry = Required balance − Existing balance in the adjustment account

When a trading security is sold

First, bring the adjustment up to the date of sale (here, from $489,025 to the $498,000 sale price, an additional gain of $8,975). Then remove the accounts. Because every change in value has already hit net income, no additional gain or loss is recorded on the sale.

Available-for-Sale (AFS)

AFS is the middle ground: the investor isn't actively trading, but the security is available to sell if cash is needed or the market is favorable. It's reported at fair value like a trading security, but unrealized holding gains and losses go to other comprehensive income (OCI) rather than net income.

Net income vs. OCI. Comprehensive income = net income + OCI. Net income is closed to retained earnings; OCI is closed to accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI). Think of AOCI as a "holding tank" in shareholders' equity: unrealized gains and losses accumulate there while the investment is held, and are emptied out when it's sold.

The rationale is that AFS investments are held for multiple periods, so gains and losses may offset over time; running them through net income each period would make earnings look more volatile than they really are.

Impairment

If an AFS security's fair value is below amortized cost, ask what management expects to do. If management intends to sell, or more likely than not will have to sell before fair value recovers, write the security down to fair value and recognize the loss in net income. Otherwise, estimate credit losses and record them through an allowance (as with HTM); any remaining decline stays in OCI as an unrealized loss.

Income taxes

Retained earnings contains after-tax amounts, so for AOCI to be comparable, unrealized gains and losses in OCI are reported net of tax, which typically creates deferred tax assets and liabilities.

Transfers Between Classifications

The classification is reassessed at each reporting date. A security that is reclassified is transferred at its fair value on the transfer date, and any unrealized gain or loss is handled consistently with the new classification.

Effect of reclassifying a debt investment
Transfer from → toTreatment of the unrealized gain or loss at transfer
HTM or AFS → TradingInclude the total unrealized gain or loss in current net income, as if it all occurred now.
Trading → HTM or AFSInclude in net income only the unrealized gain or loss that arose in the current period before the transfer (earlier amounts already went through net income).
HTM → AFSNo effect on net income. Report the total unrealized gain or loss in AOCI.
AFS → HTMNo effect on net income. Leave the existing AOCI balance in place and amortize it to net income over the security's remaining life; the fair value becomes the new amortized cost.

Reclassifications are unusual, and the notes must describe the circumstances.

The Fair Value Option

GAAP lets a company elect to account for most financial assets (including HTM- and AFS-type debt investments) at fair value, with unrealized gains and losses in net income, which is exactly how trading securities are treated. Key features:

  • The election is made when the investment is purchased and is irrevocable.
  • It can be applied to some securities and not to others, but the company must explain a partial election in the notes.
  • Unlike true trading securities, cash flows from FVO investments usually are investing activities, because they aren't held for near-term sale.
  • One reason for the option: it simplifies hedging. If a company hedges an asset with a liability, electing fair value for both lets the offsetting changes show up in earnings together without complex hedge-accounting rules.

Individual securities are classified as current or noncurrent based on when they will mature or be sold. A company doesn't have to show the three categories separately on the face of the balance sheet if the notes provide the information.

The notes also disclose, for each period: aggregate fair value, gross realized and unrealized gains and losses, the change in net unrealized gains and losses, and amortized cost by major security type. They also identify the level of the fair value hierarchy (Levels 1, 2, or 3) for each measurement; Level 3 estimates, the most subjective, require the most disclosure.

GAAP vs. IFRS

Different Classification Categories

IFRS has no HTM or AFS categories. Debt investments are classified as amortized cost (like HTM), fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI) (like AFS, but with different impairment rules), or fair value through profit or loss (FVPL) (like trading). Classification depends on (1) whether the contractual cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest, and (2) the business model: collect cash flows only, collect and sell, or sell for profit.

Transfers Are Much Rarer

Under IFRS, an investment moves between categories only when the company changes its business model for it.

Tighter Fair Value Option

IFRS allows the fair value option only in specific circumstances, such as avoiding an "accounting mismatch" in a hedge. U.S. GAAP's option isn't limited to those situations. U.S. GAAP also permits specialized accounting for industries like broker-dealers and insurers; IFRS does not.
