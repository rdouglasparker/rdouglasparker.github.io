A puzzle to start

On December 31, the staff at Hazelwood Paddle Supply count every kayak and paddle in the warehouse and total them at $412,000. The controller says the balance sheet should show $442,500. Nobody miscounted. So where does the extra $30,500 come from?

Why the warehouse count is not the inventory balance (Hazelwood Paddle Supply)

Inventory Hazelwood owns at December 31442,500

Inventory is not "what is on our shelves." It is what we own and intend to sell, measured at what it cost to get it ready for sale. Before anyone can pick FIFO or LIFO, the accountant has to answer three questions: Which units belong in inventory? What costs belong in each unit? And how does the accounting system keep track of both? These notes answer those three questions.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept round so the accounting, not the arithmetic, is the focus. Account titles follow common practice, but companies use many different labels for the same inventory accounts.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

Most of the rules in these notes come from Topic 330, Inventory, in the FASB Accounting Standards Codification (ASC). Questions about when goods change hands between a seller and a buyer (shipping terms, consignments, and returns) are answered by Topic 606, Revenue from Contracts with Customers, because the seller's sale and the buyer's purchase are the same event. Boxes labeled "In the FASB Codification" point you to the guidance. They paraphrase it, so read the paragraph itself when you need the exact wording.

What inventory is, and the types of inventory

Inventory is the goods a company (1) holds for sale in the normal course of business, (2) is producing for future sale, or (3) will use up in producing goods for sale. The same item can be inventory for one company and equipment for another. A laptop on the shelf at an electronics store is inventory; the laptop the store's bookkeeper uses is equipment.

Inventory's expense twin is cost of goods sold (COGS). The balance sheet reports the cost of goods still on hand; the income statement reports the cost of goods sold this period. For retailers, wholesalers, and manufacturers, inventory is often the largest current asset and COGS the largest expense, so small errors in either one move reported profit.

In the FASB Codification

  • The glossary at ASC 330-10-20 defines inventory as tangible items held for sale in the ordinary course of business, in the process of production for such sale, or to be consumed in producing goods or services for sale.
  • ASC 330-10-30-1 makes cost the primary basis for measuring inventory.

Merchandising companies

Retailers and wholesalers buy goods that are already finished and resell them. Their inventory is called merchandise inventory, and its cost is the purchase price plus whatever else it takes to get the goods to the store and ready to sell.

Manufacturing companies

A manufacturer makes what it sells, so it carries three kinds of inventory:

  4. Sell the productThe cost moves from finished goods to cost of goods sold.

Perpetual and periodic inventory systems

A company can track inventory in one of two ways. The difference is when the inventory account and cost of goods sold are updated.

Perpetual system

A perpetual system updates the inventory account for every purchase, sale, and return, and records cost of goods sold at the moment of each sale. Barcode scanners and RFID tags make this cheap enough that nearly all large companies use it. Companies still count inventory, at least once a year or by sampling, to catch theft, breakage, and recording errors.

Periodic system

A periodic system does not update inventory during the year. Purchases go to a temporary Purchases account, and nothing is recorded for cost when a sale is made. At year-end a physical count sets ending inventory, and cost of goods sold is the amount that must have left:

The cost of goods sold equation

Beginning inventory + Net purchases − Ending inventory = Cost of goods sold. Beginning inventory plus net purchases is the cost of goods available for sale. Every dollar of it ends up in one of two places: still on hand (ending inventory) or sold (cost of goods sold).

Watch out: shrinkage

If Tuckaseigee's perpetual records had shown $184,000 but the count found $180,000, the $4,000 difference is inventory shrinkage. The company writes the inventory down to the count (debit cost of goods sold or a shrinkage expense, credit inventory). A periodic system can't see shrinkage at all: the missing goods are simply counted as sold.

Which units belong in inventory

Usually the answer is "the goods in the building." Three situations break that rule. In each one, ask: who controls the goods, and who carries the risk of owning them?

Goods in transit

At year-end, the company needs a clean cutoff: goods on a truck between seller and buyer belong to whoever holds title under the shipping terms.

Shipping terms and ownership of goods in transit
TermsTitle passes to the buyerAt year-end, goods in transit belong toWho usually pays freight
F.o.b. shipping pointWhen the seller hands the goods to the carrierThe buyerThe buyer (freight-in)
F.o.b. destinationWhen the goods arrive at the buyer's locationThe sellerThe seller (freight-out)

Back to the puzzle: Hazelwood's $26,000 of kayaks shipped f.o.b. shipping point belonged to Hazelwood the moment the supplier's truck pulled away, so they go in Hazelwood's inventory. The $14,000 of kayaks Hazelwood shipped f.o.b. destination stay in Hazelwood's inventory until the customer receives them; Hazelwood also waits until delivery to record the sale.

Goods on consignment

In a consignment, the owner of the goods (the consignor) ships them to another company (the consignee), which tries to sell them for a commission. The consignee has the goods, but the consignor keeps title, sets the price, and takes back anything unsold. So the goods stay in the consignor's inventory until the consignee sells them to a customer.

Blue Ridge Pottery Works and Dillsboro Craft Gallery

In November, Blue Ridge Pottery Works ships pottery that cost $48,000 to Dillsboro Craft Gallery on consignment and pays $1,200 to have it delivered. The gallery keeps a 15% commission on what it sells and returns anything unsold after six months. By December 31 the gallery has sold half of the pottery to customers for $40,000.

Blue Ridge Pottery's accounting at December 31
ItemAmountHow it is figured
Cost of pottery shipped, including freight to the gallery49,200$48,000 + $1,200; freight to get goods to the point of sale is a cost of inventory
Sales revenue40,000Recognized when the gallery sells to customers
Commission expense6,000$40,000 × 15%; a selling expense, not a cost of inventory
Cost of goods sold24,600Half of $49,200
Ending inventory (held by the gallery)24,600The unsold half stays on Blue Ridge's books

The gallery records none of the pottery as its own inventory. It records only its commission revenue.

Goods expected to be returned

When customers have a right of return, the seller estimates returns at the time of sale. It records a refund liability for the cash it expects to give back and, because the returned goods will come back to the shelf, an asset for the cost of the inventory it expects to recover. That asset is reported with inventory.

Sylva Ridge Apparel

Sylva Ridge sells jackets for $100,000 that cost $60,000. Based on experience it expects 5% of the jackets to come back in resalable condition.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 606-10-25-30 lists indicators that control has passed to a customer, such as legal title and physical possession. Shipping terms decide when that happens for goods in transit.
  • ASC 606-10-55-79 and 55-80 describe consignment arrangements: the consignor does not recognize revenue when it delivers goods to the consignee.
  • ASC 606-10-55-22 through 55-29 cover sales with a right of return, including the asset for the right to recover products from customers.

Which costs belong in inventory

The cost of inventory is everything necessary to get the goods into condition and location for sale (or, for raw materials, ready for use in production). Costs that attach to inventory are product costs: they wait on the balance sheet and become cost of goods sold when the goods are sold. Everything else is a period cost, expensed when incurred.

Freight-out on goods shipped to customersExpenseA cost of selling, not of acquiring. Reported in cost of goods sold or as a selling expense; a company that leaves it out of cost of goods sold discloses the amount and where it is reported.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 330-10-30-1: the cost of inventory is the total of the expenditures and charges incurred, directly or indirectly, to bring an item to its existing condition and location.
  • ASC 330-10-30-7: abnormal idle-facility expense, freight, handling costs, and wasted materials (spoilage) are current-period charges.
  • ASC 606-10-25-18B lets a seller treat shipping and handling that occurs after the customer gains control as a cost of fulfilling the sale rather than as a separate service.

Freight-in vs. freight-out

Ask which direction the truck is going. Freight on goods coming in is part of what the goods cost. Freight on goods going out to customers is a cost of selling them.

Purchase returns and purchase discounts

Purchase returns

When the buyer sends goods back, it records a purchase return. In a perpetual system the return reduces Inventory directly. In a periodic system it goes to a temporary Purchase returns account (a contra account to Purchases), which is subtracted in figuring net purchases.

Purchase discounts

Suppliers often offer a cash discount for quick payment. Terms of 2/10, n/30 mean a 2% discount if the buyer pays within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days. The discount is a reduction in the cost of the inventory. The buyer can record it under one of two methods:

  • Gross method: record the purchase at the full invoice price and reduce inventory only if the discount is taken.
  • Net method: record the purchase at the invoice price less the discount, on the expectation that the discount will be taken. A discount missed is recorded in Purchase discounts lost, which is usually reported as an expense (often like interest).

Under the gross method, inventory ends at $29,640 ($30,000 − $360 discount taken). Under the net method it is $29,400, and the missed $240 is an expense. The net method treats a missed discount as a cost of paying late, not as part of what the goods cost.

Why companies almost always take the discount

Skipping a 2/10, n/30 discount means paying 2% to keep the money for 20 more days. That is about 2% ÷ 98% × (365 ÷ 20) ≈ 37% a year. Few companies can earn that much on the cash, so most pay within the discount period. That is also why the net method, which flags missed discounts, appeals to management.

Common mistakes

  • Counting only what is in the building. Add goods bought f.o.b. shipping point that are in transit and goods out on consignment; remove goods held for others on consignment.
  • Putting consigned goods in the consignee's inventory. The consignor owns them until they are sold.
  • Adding freight-out to inventory. Only freight-in is a cost of inventory; freight-out is a cost of selling.
  • Capitalizing abnormal costs. Abnormal spoilage, idle capacity, and excess handling are expensed.
  • Recording cost of goods sold at the time of sale in a periodic system. Periodic systems compute it at year-end from the equation.
  • Forgetting the discount under the net method. The purchase is recorded net from day one; a missed discount is an expense, not extra inventory.

Key terms

Inventory
Goods held for sale, in production for sale, or to be used in producing goods for sale.
Cost of goods sold
The expense for the cost of inventory sold during the period.
Raw materials, work in process, finished goods
A manufacturer's three kinds of inventory: parts not yet used, products not yet finished, and products ready to sell.
Perpetual inventory system
A system that updates inventory and records cost of goods sold with every transaction.
Periodic inventory system
A system that sets inventory by a year-end count and computes cost of goods sold from the cost of goods sold equation.
Cost of goods available for sale
Beginning inventory plus net purchases.
Net purchases
Purchases plus freight-in, less purchase returns and purchase discounts.
F.o.b. shipping point / f.o.b. destination
Shipping terms under which title passes to the buyer when goods are shipped / when they arrive.
Consignment
An arrangement in which the consignor keeps title to goods that a consignee holds and tries to sell for a commission.
Product cost / period cost
A cost included in inventory and expensed when the goods are sold / a cost expensed when incurred.
Freight-in / freight-out
Shipping on goods coming in (a cost of inventory) / on goods going to customers (a cost of selling).
Gross method / net method
Recording purchases at the full invoice price / at the price less the available cash discount.
Purchase discounts lost
Under the net method, the cost of failing to pay within the discount period.
Inventory shrinkage
Inventory lost to theft, damage, or error, found by comparing records with a count.
