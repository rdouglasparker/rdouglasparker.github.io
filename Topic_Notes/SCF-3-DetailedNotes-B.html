The indirect method

The indirect method begins with net income and works backward to cash from operations. It always has two kinds of adjustments: items that are in net income but are not operating cash flows (noncash expenses, gains, and losses), and changes in operating working capital accounts.

The continuing example, indirect method

Cullowhee Creek Trading Company sells outdoor and camping gear to shops in the Blue Ridge. All amounts are in thousands. Sales are $1,000 and cost of goods sold is $600. Salaries expense is $120, insurance expense is $20, depreciation expense is $40, interest expense is $10 (no interest payable at either date), and income tax expense is $75. The company sold land with a book value of $35 for $50, a gain of $15. During the year, accounts receivable rose $30, inventory rose $20, accounts payable rose $15, salaries payable fell $10, prepaid insurance rose $5, and income taxes payable fell $5. It bought equipment for $90 cash, borrowed $80 on long-term notes, sold stock for $20, and paid dividends of $50. Cash was $70 at the start of the year.

The total of $120 matches the direct method. If the two ever differ, an error was made.

Direct plus reconciliation

A company that chooses the direct method must also present a reconciliation of net income to net cash from operating activities, with the same adjustments shown above. So the full direct statement of the example has the six cash lines (totaling $120), followed by the schedule that starts with $150 of net income and ends at $120. Both must agree.

Why each adjustment has its sign

Logic of the adjustments
ItemWhy it is adjusted
DepreciationReduced net income but used no cash, so add it back
Gain on sale of landIncluded in net income, but the whole $50 is in investing, so subtract the gain to avoid double counting
Loss on sale of an assetReduced net income without using operating cash, so add it back
Increase in receivablesRevenue recognized but not collected, so subtract
Increase in payablesExpense recognized but not yet paid, so add

Special items in operating activities

Several less common items follow the same logic: find the part of net income that is not an operating cash flow, then adjust.

Special items and how they are handled

Amortization of a bond discountAdd (interest expense exceeds cash interest)Cash interest is operating; principal is financing
Amortization of a bond premiumSubtract (interest expense is less than cash interest)Cash interest is operating; principal is financing

Conversion of bonds to stockNo adjustmentNoncash disclosure only
Inventory write-down, patent amortization, other noncash chargesAdd backNone

Early bond retirement

Fontana Lake Resorts retires $200 face value of bonds with a carrying amount of $196 by paying $204 cash. The loss is $8. The investing and operating sections do not include the payment. The $204 outflow goes in financing, and the $8 loss is added back in the operating reconciliation because it reduced net income without being an operating cash outflow.

Noncash activities, U.S. GAAP, and IFRS

A noncash investing and financing activity is a significant transaction that affects the long-term asset, liability, or equity structure without using cash. Report it in a schedule or note, not in the statement body.

Differences between U.S. GAAP and IFRS

Consistency

Under IFRS the company chooses a classification for each of these items and must apply it consistently from period to period. A reader comparing a U.S. GAAP company to an IFRS company should adjust for these choices.

Using the statement

Operating cash flow is a quality-of-earnings check. If net income is high and operating cash flow is low, ask why: growing receivables, inventory build-up, or aggressive revenue recognition are common explanations.

Common cash flow measures
MeasureFormulaWhat it tells you
Free cash flowNet cash from operating activities − capital expendituresCash left to pay dividends, repay debt, or buy back stock after maintaining and expanding operations
Operating cash flow to current liabilitiesOperating cash flow ÷ average current liabilitiesAbility to meet short-term obligations from operations
Cash flow to net incomeOperating cash flow ÷ net incomeWhether earnings are backed by cash
Capital expenditure ratioOperating cash flow ÷ capital expendituresWhether operations fund investment

In the continuing example, free cash flow is $120 − $90 = $30, and operating cash flow is $120 ÷ $150 = 0.80 of net income.

Presentation rule

Cash flow per share may not be reported in the statement of cash flows. Ratios and free cash flow are analyst tools, not part of the statement.

Practice: the indirect method and special items

Try each problem, then open the answer.

Problem 1

Pisgah View Furniture reports net income of $85 (in thousands), depreciation of $30, and a loss on sale of equipment of $8. Accounts receivable rose $12, inventory fell $6, accounts payable fell $4, and accrued wages rose $3. Find net cash from operating activities.

Show the answer

85 + 30 + 8 − 12 + 6 − 4 + 3 = $116.

Problem 2

Whitewater Falls Sports has net income of $210 (in thousands). It amortized $5 of bond discount, recorded $12 of equity-method income and received $4 of dividends from that investee, and its deferred tax liability increased $9. Starting from net income, find the adjusted amount (ignore working capital).

Show the answer

210 + 5 − 12 + 4 + 9 = $216. Equity-method income not received in cash is subtracted, and the dividends received are added back.

Problem 3

A company retires bonds with a $300 face value and a $297 carrying amount by paying $291 cash. Determine the gain or loss, the section where the cash payment appears, and how the indirect method reflects it.

Show the answer

Carrying amount $297 − cash $291 = a $6 gain. The $291 outflow is in financing. The indirect method subtracts the $6 gain from net income.

Problem 4

Using the continuing example, a company has operating cash flow of $120, capital expenditures of $90, and net income of $150. Compute free cash flow and cash flow to net income, and explain which is more informative about earnings quality.

Show the answer

Free cash flow = 120 − 90 = $30. Cash flow to net income = 120 ÷ 150 = 0.80. The second ratio is more directly about earnings quality: only 80 cents of each dollar of earnings was collected as operating cash.

Key terms

Cash equivalent
A short-term, highly liquid investment that is readily convertible to cash with little risk of loss, generally within three months of acquisition.
Operating activities
Cash effects of transactions that enter into the determination of net income.
Investing activities
Acquiring and disposing of long-term assets and of investments other than cash equivalents.
Financing activities
Raising and repaying cash from owners and creditors.
Direct method
Reporting operating cash flows as gross receipts and payments by category.
Indirect method
Reconciling net income to operating cash flow by adjusting for noncash items and working capital changes.
Noncash investing and financing activity
A significant transaction without cash that changes long-term assets, liabilities, or equity, disclosed separately.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures.
