A puzzle to start

Balsam Peak Brewing sells 200,000 new shares of its common stock to investors for $25 a share and collects $5,000,000. Yet its balance sheet does not show a single $5,000,000 line. It shows $200,000 of “common stock” and $4,800,000 of “paid-in capital—excess of par.” Why would one investment be split into two numbers, and what do the shareholders actually own?

One sale of shares, two balance sheet lines (Balsam Peak Brewing)
ItemAmount
Cash received (200,000 shares × $25)5,000,000
Common stock (200,000 shares × $1 par)200,000
Paid-in capital—excess of par (the rest)4,800,000

The split is a leftover from old state corporation laws built around a share’s par value, which you will meet later in these notes. The economics are simple: shareholders invested $5,000,000, and equity rose by $5,000,000. Most of shareholders’ equity accounting is about recording each owner transaction in the right equity account, while always asking one question: how did this transaction change net assets?

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept round so the accounting, not the arithmetic, is the focus. Account titles follow common practice, but companies use many different labels for the same equity accounts.

What shareholders’ equity is

Shareholders’ equity is a residual: what is left of the assets after the creditors’ claims are subtracted. Another name for that residual is net assets.

The equation behind every entry

Assets − Liabilities = Shareholders’ equity. When you record an equity transaction, ask how it changed net assets. Selling shares for cash raises net assets and equity. Paying a cash dividend lowers both. Some transactions only move amounts from one equity account to another and leave the total unchanged.

Owners’ interests come from two basic sources:

  • Invested capital: amounts shareholders put in when they buy shares from the company, reported as paid-in capital.
  • Earned capital: profits the company has earned for its shareholders and kept, reported as retained earnings.

Many names for the same thing

Shareholders’ equity is also called stockholders’ equity, shareowners’ equity, or simply equity. Paid-in capital—excess of par is often called additional paid-in capital or capital in excess of par. Retained earnings is sometimes called reinvested earnings. Focus on what an account represents, not its exact label.

Comprehensive income and AOCI

Comprehensive income is the total change in equity during a period from all sources other than transactions with owners. Owner transactions, such as selling shares, buying them back, and paying dividends, are left out. Comprehensive income has two parts:

  • Net income, reported in the income statement.
  • Other comprehensive income (OCI): certain gains and losses that change equity but are kept out of net income.

The usual OCI items

  1. Unrealized holding gains and losses on available-for-sale investments in debt securities.
  2. Pension and other postretirement plan gains and losses, and prior service cost from plan amendments.
  3. Deferred gains and losses on derivatives designated as cash flow hedges.
  4. Foreign currency translation adjustments from converting a foreign subsidiary’s statements into dollars.

Each OCI item is reported net of its income tax effect (or before tax, with the tax disclosed).

A flow and a balance

OCI works just like net income. Net income for the period flows into retained earnings, the running total. OCI for the period flows into accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI), the running total reported in shareholders’ equity.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 220-10-45 allows comprehensive income to be presented either in one continuous statement (income statement and OCI together) or in two consecutive statements, and requires the total of comprehensive income to be shown.
  • The same subtopic requires the accumulated balance of each OCI component to be reported in equity, separate from retained earnings and paid-in capital.

The corporate form of organization

A business can be a sole proprietorship, a partnership, or a corporation. Assets and liabilities are accounted for the same way in all three; what differs is the accounting for the owners’ interest. A proprietorship or partnership keeps a single capital account for each owner. A corporation uses the several equity accounts in these notes.

A corporation is a separate legal entity, distinct from its owners. It is created under the laws of a state, which issues its charter based on the articles of incorporation. Many states base their corporation laws on the Model Business Corporation Act.

Why organize as a corporation?
FeatureWhat it means
Limited liabilityShareholders can lose what they invested, but creditors cannot reach their personal assets.
Ease of raising capitalOwnership is divided into shares that can be sold to many investors and traded easily, so the company can raise large amounts of equity.
ContinuityThe company continues when shareholders sell their shares or die.
Double taxation (a disadvantage)The corporation pays income tax on its profits, and shareholders pay income tax again on dividends from those after-tax profits.
Regulation (a disadvantage)Corporations, especially publicly traded ones, face more reporting requirements and oversight, such as SEC filings.

Types of corporations and hybrids

  • Not-for-profit corporations: charities, many hospitals and universities.
  • For-profit corporations: publicly held (shares traded on an exchange or over the counter) or privately held (shares owned by a small group and not publicly traded).
  • Hybrids that combine limited liability with the tax treatment of a partnership: S corporations (limited in the number and type of shareholders), limited liability companies (LLCs), and limited liability partnerships (LLPs).

Authorized, issued, and outstanding shares

The charter authorizes a maximum number of shares. Shares sold to shareholders are issued. Issued shares still held by shareholders are outstanding. Shares the company has bought back and holds as treasury stock are issued but not outstanding.

Common and preferred shares

Rights of common shareholders

Unless an agreement says otherwise, common shareholders have the right to:

  1. Vote on matters that come before shareholders, including the election of directors (usually one vote per share).
  2. Share in profits when the board declares dividends, in proportion to shares owned.
  3. Share in assets if the company is liquidated, after creditors and preferred shareholders are paid.

Some companies also grant a preemptive right: the right to buy a proportionate share of any new shares issued, so an owner can keep the same percentage of the company. Most companies exclude it.

Features of preferred shares

Preferred shares usually have two preferences over common shares: a dividend preference (a stated dividend, as a percent of par or dollars per share, paid before any common dividend) and a liquidation preference. Preferred shareholders usually do not vote. Other features vary:

Preferred stock features
FeatureWhat it means
CumulativeAny stated dividend not paid in a year becomes dividends in arrears, which must be paid before common shareholders get anything. Noncumulative preferred loses an unpaid year for good.
ParticipatingPreferred shareholders can receive dividends beyond the stated amount, sharing with common. Rare today; most preferred is nonparticipating.
ConvertibleThe holder may exchange preferred shares for a set number of common shares.
Redeemable or callableThe shares can be bought back at a set price, at the holder’s option (redeemable) or the company’s option (callable).

The labels “common” and “preferred” guarantee nothing by themselves. The rights of each class come from the contract with shareholders, which must be summarized in the notes.

Equity or debt?

Some preferred shares look a lot like bonds: a fixed dividend, cumulative, and a required redemption date. When shares are mandatorily redeemable, meaning the company must buy them back for cash at a fixed or determinable date, they are reported as a liability, not equity, and their dividends are reported as interest expense.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 480-10-25-4: a mandatorily redeemable financial instrument is classified as a liability unless the redemption is required only when the company liquidates or terminates.
  • ASC 505-10-50-3: a company explains, in summary form, the pertinent rights and privileges of its outstanding securities, such as dividend and liquidation preferences, participation rights, call prices and dates, conversion terms, and voting rights.

IFRS

Under IFRS (IAS 32), the test for a liability is whether the issuer can be required to deliver cash or another financial asset. As a result, many preference shares that U.S. GAAP reports as equity are reported as liabilities under IFRS, with their dividends reported as interest expense.

Par value, no-par, and stated value

Par value is an amount printed in the charter for each share. Long ago it was meant to be the real value of a share, and early laws treated it as legal capital, a cushion of net assets that could not be paid out to shareholders. Selling shares below par created a liability for the buyer, so companies began choosing tiny par values, often a penny or less. Today par value has almost no economic meaning, and the Model Business Corporation Act has dropped the idea, but most companies still have par value shares outstanding.

For accounting, par value matters only because it decides how the proceeds of a sale are split:

Shares issued for noncash consideration

A company may issue shares for land, equipment, or services. Record the transaction at fair value: the fair value of the shares or of what was received, whichever is more clearly evident. For a company whose shares trade actively, the quoted market price of the shares is often the best evidence.

Glenville Granite

Glenville issues 40,000 shares of $2 par common stock for a quarry site. No reliable price is available for the land, but Glenville’s shares trade at $30. Later, Glenville issues 2,000 shares to an attorney for legal work, when the shares again trade at $30.

More than one security for a single price

Occasionally a company sells two securities, such as common and preferred shares, as a package for one price. The price is allocated by fair value:

  • Both fair values known (proportional method): allocate the total price in proportion to the relative fair values.
  • Only one fair value known (incremental method): record the known security at its fair value and assign the rest of the price to the other.

Share issue costs

Selling shares requires legal, accounting, underwriting, and printing services. These share issue costs are not expenses. They reduce the net proceeds, so they reduce the credit to paid-in capital—excess of par.

Scott Creek Solar

Scott Creek sells 500,000 shares of $0.10 par common stock to the public at $12 a share and pays $240,000 of underwriting and legal fees.

Public offering

In the FASB Codification

  • Shares issued to nonemployees for goods or services are measured at fair value under the share-based payment guidance in ASC 718, which also covers shares issued to employees.
  • For public companies, SEC guidance codified at ASC 340-10-S99-1 treats specific costs of an equity offering as a reduction of the proceeds, not as an expense.

Common mistakes

  • Recording the stock account at the selling price. Only par (or stated value) goes to the stock account; the rest is additional paid-in capital.
  • Recording a noncash asset at par value. Use the fair value of the shares or of the asset, whichever is more clearly evident.
  • Expensing share issue costs. They reduce paid-in capital—excess of par, not income.
  • Putting OCI in retained earnings. OCI accumulates in AOCI; only net income goes to retained earnings.
  • Counting treasury shares as outstanding. They are issued but not outstanding, and they get no dividends or votes.
  • Treating every preferred share as equity. Mandatorily redeemable shares are liabilities.

Problem 3: Net income, OCI, or neither?

Black Rock Bakery reports net income of $510,000. During the year it also (a) paid $90,000 of cash dividends, (b) had an after-tax unrealized loss of $15,000 on available-for-sale debt investments, (c) issued shares for $300,000, and (d) had an after-tax pension gain of $8,000. Compute other comprehensive income and comprehensive income.

Show the answer

Dividends (a) and the share issue (c) are transactions with owners, so they are excluded. OCI = −$15,000 + $8,000 = −$7,000 (a net loss). Comprehensive income = $510,000 − $7,000 = $503,000. Retained earnings rises by $510,000 − $90,000 = $420,000; AOCI falls by $7,000; paid-in capital rises by $300,000.

Key terms

Shareholders’ equity
The owners’ residual interest in a corporation’s assets: assets minus liabilities.
Paid-in capital
Amounts invested by shareholders, including the stock accounts and additional paid-in capital.
Additional paid-in capital
Paid-in capital other than the stock accounts; most commonly, paid-in capital—excess of par.
Retained earnings
Cumulative net income less dividends and certain other distributions. A debit balance is a deficit.
Comprehensive income
The change in equity from all sources other than owners: net income plus other comprehensive income.
Other comprehensive income (OCI)
Gains and losses that change equity but are excluded from net income.
Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)
The running total of OCI, reported in shareholders’ equity.
Statement of shareholders’ equity
A statement that explains the change in each equity account during the period.
Authorized, issued, and outstanding shares
The maximum the charter allows; the shares sold; and the issued shares still held by shareholders.
Preferred stock
Shares with a preference over common shares to dividends and to assets in liquidation.
Cumulative preferred
Preferred shares whose unpaid dividends (dividends in arrears) must be paid before any common dividend.
Par value
An amount per share set in the charter; it decides how much of the proceeds goes to the stock account.
Legal capital
An old legal idea, usually equal to total par value, of net assets that could not be paid out to shareholders.
Share issue costs
Costs of selling shares; they reduce the proceeds and so reduce additional paid-in capital.
Mandatorily redeemable shares
Shares the company is required to buy back for cash; reported as a liability.
