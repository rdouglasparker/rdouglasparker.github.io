ACCT 351 · Leases — Chapter 15

Lease Classification & Finance/Sales-Type Leases

Reading Notes

Why Companies Lease

Leasing has become one of the most common ways for companies to obtain the use of an asset. Several operational, financial, and tax incentives explain why:

1. Less up-front cash. Buying an asset can trigger loan origination fees, closing costs, brokerage fees, and taxes. Many leases begin with nothing more than the first periodic payment. Newer or higher-credit-risk companies may find leasing is their only way to acquire an asset.

2. Lower periodic payments. Installment payments are based on the asset's full fair value. Lease payments are often tied only to the portion of value expected to decline during the lease, and the lease period can be shorter than the asset's life.

3. Flexibility and lower disposal cost. Returning a leased asset takes little effort or cost. Selling an owned asset can be difficult (thin markets for unique assets) and expensive (a real estate agent may charge up to 6%).

4. Protection against declining asset values. The lessee avoids the risk that the asset's value falls, though it also gives up any increase in value.

5. Possible tax advantages. A company with little taxable income gets little benefit from depreciation deductions. By letting the lessor keep ownership (and the deductions), the lessee can often negotiate lower lease payments.

Classifying a Lease: Substance over Form

How a lease is accounted for depends on whether it more naturally resembles the purchase of an asset with debt financing or a rental agreement. Sometimes that's obvious: a 10-year noncancelable lease of a computer with a 10-year life, with title passing to the lessee at the end, is clearly a purchase in disguise. A 10-day rental of the same computer is clearly a rental.

LesseeLessor
Finance leaseSales-type lease (without selling profit, or with selling profit)
Operating leaseOperating lease
Substance over form: from an accounting perspective, legal ownership is irrelevant to classification. The lease is classified by its economic substance, even when that conflicts with its legal form.

A finance lease is not literally identical to buying the asset (a lessee can't sell a leased asset, for example). But it is economically similar to a purchase because its terms (a) normally let the lessee direct the use of the asset so that it receives substantially all of the remaining benefits, and (b) create obligations similar to those of financing a purchase. An operating lease lacks those characteristics.

Between the two extremes is a gray area: a lease that doesn't transfer title but covers six years of an 8-year life, or one where the present value of the payments is nearly equal to the asset's value. Because pure judgment leads to inconsistent practice, the FASB provides five criteria to guide the decision.

The Five Classification Criteria

A lease is a finance lease (a sales-type lease to the lessor) if one or more of these five criteria is met. If none is met, it's an operating lease.

#CriterionCommon guideline / what to remember
1Ownership transfers to the lessee.Self-evident: if title passes during or at the end of the lease, the ownership attributes have transferred.
2Purchase option the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise.Often called a bargain purchase option (BPO). The exercise price is low enough, relative to expected fair value, that exercise is reasonably certain.
3Lease term is for the "major part" of the asset's remaining economic life.One reasonable approach: 75% or more of the remaining economic life.
4Present value of lease payments equals or exceeds "substantially all" of the asset's fair value.One reasonable approach: 90% or more of fair value.
5Asset is so specialized it has no alternative use to the lessor at the end of the lease.Only the lessee can derive the risks and rewards of ownership, and the lessor earns its return only through this lease.

Working through the details

Criterion 2 — a bargain purchase option. Suppose a food truck is leased for $18,000 per year for four years, with an option at the end to buy it for $6,000. If the truck is expected to be worth about $15,000 in four years, $6,000 is a bargain price, so exercise is reasonably certain and the criterion is met. The logic mirrors criterion 1: we expect title to transfer.

Criterion 3 — the major part of economic life. The 75% figure is only one approach, not a precise indicator. Three implementation issues come up:

  • Uncertain lease term. If the lease has renewal options or can be canceled after a noncancelable period, you must judge whether a change in the term is reasonably certain. Periods covered by a renewal option are not included in the lease term if a bargain purchase option is present, because the term can't extend beyond the date a purchase option is exercised.
  • Estimating economic life. Economic life is the remaining time the asset is expected to be economically usable with normal maintenance. It's an estimate, which creates room to arrive at a figure that keeps this criterion from being met.
  • Leases near the end of an asset's life. If the lease begins at or near the end of the asset's economic life, criterion 3 is not used, because most of the risks and rewards of ownership occurred before then. (One reasonable approach treats the final 25% of economic life as "at or near the end.")

Criterion 4 — present value of payments. If the lease payments have a present value that represents substantially all of the asset's fair value, the lessee has effectively paid enough to have purchased it. The payments counted for classification are those the lessee is required to make in connection with the lease, including any residual value of the asset that the lessee guarantees (covered later in the chapter).

Only one criterion has to be met. A lease that fails four of the five tests and passes one is still a finance/sales-type lease. To be an operating lease, a lease must fail all five.

Why classification still matters. Before the current lease guidance, a lessee that could structure a lease as an operating lease kept both the asset and the liability off its balance sheet, helping it avoid limits in debt covenants (such as a maximum debt-to-equity ratio). Today, GAAP requires lessees to record an asset and a liability for all but short-term leases, and curtailing off-balance-sheet financing was the main reason for the change.

Managers are still motivated to structure leases as operating leases because of the more favorable income statement effects. Tactics such as making payments depend on some future event (variable payments aren't included in the lease liability) are common, so analysts should watch for them when comparing companies' profits.

A Finance Lease Is Like an Installment Purchase

You already know how to account for an installment note. A finance lease follows the same logic, because in substance it is an installment purchase. Compare two versions of the same deal: Trailhead Printing either buys a $245,866 digital press from Summit Machinery Corporation with a three-year installment note, or leases it under a three-year lease. Either way there are six semiannual payments of $50,000 at 12% annual interest (6% per period), with payments at the end of each period.

Finance Lease / Sales-Type Lease: No Selling Profit

The running example — Balsam Ridge Bakery & Mountain Trust Leasing

On January 1, Year 1, Balsam Ridge Bakery leases a commercial baking oven from Mountain Trust Leasing. Mountain Trust Leasing bought the equipment from Ridgeline Ovens Corporation for $360,376.

  • Five annual payments of $85,000: the first on January 1 (the beginning of the lease), then on each December 31 for the next four years.
  • The five-year lease term equals the oven's estimated useful life.
  • Mountain Trust Leasing routinely acquires commercial kitchen equipment to lease to other food-service businesses.
  • The interest rate in these financing arrangements is 9%.

This is more realistic than the Trailhead Printing example because the payments are made at the beginning of each period (an annuity due).

Step 4 — The lessee amortizes the right-of-use asset

Like other noncurrent assets, the right-of-use asset provides economic benefit over the lease term, so the lessee amortizes it, usually straight-line, unless its pattern of use is different. (Output measures such as units produced, or input measures such as hours used, may sometimes reflect the asset's consumption better.)

Amortization expense ($360,376 ÷ 5 years)72,075

Recorded each December 31 for the five years of the lease term. This is a separate entry from the interest/principal entries above.

Which period? The lessee normally amortizes the right-of-use asset over the lease term. But if ownership transfers or a purchase option is reasonably certain to be exercised (criterion 1 or 2 is met), the lessee will keep the asset beyond the lease term, so it amortizes over the asset's useful life instead.

The lessor's role. Mountain Trust Leasing is a financial intermediary: it acquires the asset and leases it, so its only income from the lease is interest revenue over the lease term ($64,624 in total). Some lessors, though, use leasing as an alternative way to sell their products. That's next.

Sales-Type Lease with a Selling Profit

When the lessor is a manufacturer or retailer using a lease as a means of "selling" its products, it earns a selling profit in addition to interest revenue. Selling profit exists when the fair value of the asset (usually the present value of the lease payments, or "selling price") exceeds the asset's cost or carrying value.

Selling profit = Sales revenue (PV of lease payments) − Cost of goods sold (lessor's cost)
Modify the running example

All facts are the same, except Balsam Ridge leases the equipment directly from Ridgeline Ovens Corporation, the manufacturer, instead of through Mountain Trust Leasing. Ridgeline's cost of the equipment was $270,000. The present value of the payments is still $360,376, so Ridgeline's gross profit is $360,376 − $270,000 = $90,376.

Beginning of the Lease

It's an ordinary sale, recorded in two pieces. Suppose a customer buys an $850 commercial mixer from a restaurant-supply store on the store's credit account, and the store paid $520 wholesale. The store debits Accounts receivable and credits Sales revenue for $850, then debits Cost of goods sold and credits Inventory for $520. Ridgeline's entry has exactly the same structure, with a lease receivable in place of the account receivable and equipment in place of inventory.

Everything after day one is unchanged. Except for the entry at the beginning of the lease that includes the selling profit, all of the lessor's entries (first payment, interest revenue, receivable reduction) are precisely the same as for a sales-type lease without selling profit. The receivable is $360,376 either way, so the same schedule applies.

The lessee is unaffected. Balsam Ridge's entries are exactly the same whether its lessor earns a profit or not.

A selling loss is possible. If the asset's carrying value exceeds its fair value, the lessor records a selling loss instead.

Reporting. Sales-type leases must be separately distinguished from nonlease sales in the financial statements. For example, Dell Technologies records revenue from equipment sold under sales-type leases at the present value of the lease payments at inception, plus financing income recognized at effective rates over the lease term.
