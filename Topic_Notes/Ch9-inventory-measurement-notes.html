ACCT 351 · Inventory Measurement

Measuring and Estimating Inventory

Reading Notes

Why Inventory Needs a Second Look After Purchase

Companies record inventory at cost when it's purchased or produced. But cost isn't always the right number to report at the end of a period. Damage, obsolescence, falling prices, or a shift in demand can mean the inventory on hand is no longer worth what was paid for it.

Inventory write-down: when the expected benefit of unsold inventory has fallen below its cost, an end-of-period adjusting entry reduces both the reported amount of inventory and net income. This keeps a company from reporting inventory at more than the cash it can actually generate.

There are two acceptable measurement approaches for this write-down, and which one applies depends on the cost flow method a company uses:

ApproachUsed by companies that use
Lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV)FIFO, average cost, or any method other than LIFO or the retail inventory method
Lower of cost or market (LCM)LIFO or the retail inventory method

Both approaches share the same financial statement effect — reduce reported inventory, reduce net income — and the same conservative purpose. They differ only in how the write-down amount is measured.

Lower of Cost or Net Realizable Value (LCNRV)

Net realizable value (NRV) is the estimated selling price of inventory in the ordinary course of business, reduced by reasonably predictable costs of completion, disposal, and transportation (sales commissions, shipping, etc.). Think of it as the net cash a company expects to collect from selling the item.
NRV = Selling price − Costs to sell

After comparing cost to NRV, inventory is reported at whichever is lower:

  • If NRV is lower than cost, an adjusting entry writes inventory down to NRV, which becomes the new carrying value.
  • If cost is lower than NRV, no entry is needed — inventory is already recorded correctly.

Applying LCNRV at different levels

LCNRV can be applied (a) to individual items, (b) to major categories, or (c) to the entire inventory. Applying it to groups instead of item-by-item lets increases in some items offset decreases in others, which always produces a higher reported inventory value.

Example — five inventory items

Items A and B form one category; items C, D, and E form another.

By total inventory: $415,000 (total cost, since it's lower than total NRV of $431,000).

Recording the write-down

Write-downs are usually included as part of cost of goods sold, since they're treated as a normal cost of holding inventory. If the write-down is substantial and unusual, it's recorded in a separate loss account instead and disclosed.

Cost of goods sold20,000
Inventory20,000

Writing inventory down from $415,000 (cost) to $395,000 (NRV, individual-item basis).

Once inventory is written down, the reduced amount becomes the new cost basis. If NRV later recovers before the item is sold, U.S. GAAP does not allow writing the value back up. (Under IFRS, a recovery is reversed if an inventory write-down is no longer appropriate — one of the few differences between the two standards on this topic.)

Lower of Cost or Market (LCM)

Companies using LIFO or the retail inventory method use a different benchmark than NRV: market, which means current replacement cost — what it would cost to buy or reproduce the item today — subject to two limits:

Ceiling: market cannot be greater than NRV.

Floor: market cannot be less than NRV minus a normal profit margin.

In other words, replacement cost is compared to the ceiling and floor, and whichever value falls in the middle becomes "market":

  • If replacement cost is below the floor, the floor becomes market.
  • If replacement cost is above the ceiling, the ceiling becomes market.
  • If replacement cost falls between the ceiling and floor, replacement cost itself is market.

Market is then compared to cost, and inventory is reported at the lower of the two.

Like LCNRV, LCM can be applied to individual items, categories, or total inventory, and grouping items together produces a higher reported value than an item-by-item approach.

Estimating Inventory Without a Physical Count

Sometimes counting every unit of inventory is impossible or impractical — think of a warehouse fire, a nationwide retail chain, or the need for a quick interim financial statement. Two techniques let a company estimate ending inventory and cost of goods sold without a physical count.

Both techniques rely on the same relationship: beginning inventory + net purchases = cost of goods available for sale, and goods available for sale − cost of goods sold = ending inventory. Normally, a physical count gives us ending inventory and we derive cost of goods sold. When estimating, we do the reverse: estimate cost of goods sold first, then subtract it from goods available for sale to get ending inventory.

The Gross Profit Method

The gross profit (or gross margin) method is useful for:

  • Estimating inventory lost, destroyed, or stolen (for an insurance claim, for example)
  • Preparing interim financial statements without the cost of a full physical count
  • Auditors testing the reasonableness of a client's reported inventory
  • Budgeting and forecasting

The method relies on a historical gross profit ratio — the relationship between net sales, cost of goods sold, and gross profit — to back into an estimate of cost of goods sold from known net sales.

Estimated COGS = Net sales × (1 − Gross profit ratio)
Example — inventory destroyed by fire

Beginning inventory $600,000; net purchases $1,500,000; net sales $2,000,000 prior to the fire; historical gross profit ratio 40%.

Beginning inventory$600,000
Plus: Net purchases1,500,000
Goods available for sale2,100,000
Less: Estimated cost of goods sold [$2,000,000 × (1 − 0.40)](1,200,000)
Estimated ending inventory (destroyed)$900,000
A gross profit ratio can also be expressed as a markup on cost instead of a percentage of sales. A 66⅔% markup on cost converts to a 40% gross profit ratio: markup% ÷ (1 + markup%) = gross profit ratio. Always check which way a percentage is stated before applying it.

This method gives only an estimate, and its accuracy depends entirely on the reliability of the gross profit ratio used. It doesn't explicitly account for theft or spoilage (it assumes anything not sold is still on hand), and different products with different markups can distort a single blended ratio — grouping inventory into pools with similar margins improves accuracy. Because of these limitations, the gross profit method is not acceptable for annual financial statements, though it's fine for interim reporting and the other uses listed above.

The Retail Inventory Method

High-volume retailers selling many different items at low unit prices — think large chain stores — use the retail inventory method instead. Rather than tracking the cost of each item, the company tracks purchases at both cost and current selling (retail) prices, then uses the relationship between the two to convert ending inventory at retail back to an estimate of cost.

Beginning inventory (at retail) + net purchases (at retail) − net sales = ending inventory at retail. Multiplying that by a cost-to-retail percentage (goods available for sale at cost ÷ goods available for sale at retail) converts it to an estimate of cost.
Example — Home Improvement Stores, June
CostRetail
Beginning inventory$60,000$100,000
Plus: Net purchases287,200460,000
Goods available for sale347,200560,000
Less: Net sales(400,000)
Estimated ending inventory at retail$160,000
Cost-to-retail percentage = $347,200 ÷ $560,000 = 62%.
Estimated ending inventory at cost = $160,000 × 62% = $99,200.
Estimated cost of goods sold = $347,200 − $99,200 = $248,000.

The retail method tends to be more accurate than the gross profit method because it's based on a current cost-to-retail relationship rather than a historical gross profit ratio. It also lets a company approximate different cost flow assumptions (average cost or LIFO) within the estimate, and can be used for the same purposes as the gross profit method — plus, unlike the gross profit method, it's acceptable for annual financial reporting.

Retail terminology: tracking price changes

Selling prices rarely stay fixed once inventory is on the shelf. The retail method needs to track these movements to determine ending inventory at retail:

TermMeaning
Initial markupOriginal amount of markup from cost to selling price
Additional markupIncrease in selling price after the initial markup
Markup cancellationElimination of an additional markup (not below the original price)
MarkdownDecrease in selling price below the original selling price
Markdown cancellationElimination of a markdown (not above the original price)

The net effect of additional markups and markup cancellations is the net markup; the net effect of markdowns and markdown cancellations is the net markdown. Both must be included when determining ending inventory at retail.

Retail Method Cost Flow Variations

Approximating average cost

To approximate average cost, the cost-to-retail percentage is based on all goods available for sale — both net markups and net markdowns are included in the retail-column denominator before dividing.

Example — Home Improvement Stores, July (average cost)
CostRetail
Beginning inventory$99,200$160,000
Plus: Net purchases305,280470,000
Plus: Net markups10,000
Less: Net markdowns(8,000)
Goods available for sale404,480632,000
Less: Net sales(434,000)
Estimated ending inventory at retail$198,000
Cost-to-retail percentage = $404,480 ÷ $632,000 = 64%.
Estimated ending inventory at cost = $198,000 × 64% = $126,720.
Estimated cost of goods sold = $404,480 − $126,720 = $277,760.

Approximating the lower of average cost or market — the conventional retail method

Companies using the retail method report inventory at the lower of cost or market. The conventional retail method approximates this by excluding net markdowns from the cost-to-retail percentage calculation (markups are still included). Markdowns are subtracted from goods available for sale only after the percentage has been calculated.

The logic: a markdown is evidence that obsolescence, overstocking, or falling prices has reduced the utility of the merchandise. Excluding it from the percentage recognizes that decline immediately, which produces a lower cost-to-retail percentage — and therefore a lower, more conservative estimate of ending inventory — whenever markdowns exist.

Example — same data, conventional method
CostRetail
Beginning inventory$99,200$160,000
Plus: Net purchases305,280470,000
Plus: Net markups10,000
Goods available for sale (excluding markdowns)404,480640,000
Less: Net markdowns(8,000)
Goods available for sale (including markdowns)632,000
Less: Net sales(434,000)
Estimated ending inventory at retail$198,000
Cost-to-retail percentage = $404,480 ÷ $640,000 = 63.2% (lower than the 64% average-cost percentage, because markdowns were excluded from the denominator).
Estimated ending inventory at cost = $198,000 × 63.2% = $125,136.
Estimated cost of goods sold = $404,480 − $125,136 = $279,344.

Note that ending inventory at retail ($198,000) is identical under every cost flow variation — it always reflects current retail prices. Only the cost-to-retail percentage applied to it changes.
