A puzzle to start

Catamount Peak Outfitters reported inventory of $1,150,000 on last year’s balance sheet. This year’s annual report shows last year’s inventory as $1,490,000. Nothing was found in a warehouse, and nobody miscounted. What happened?

The company switched its inventory costing method. If last year’s number had been left alone, an investor comparing the two years would be comparing one method to another. Instead, the company went back and recast last year as if the new method had been used all along. That recasting is the most common way to report an accounting change, but it is not the only way. This unit sorts every kind of change into a small number of buckets and tells you how each bucket is reported.

The continuing example

Catamount Peak Outfitters began operations in Year 1 and sells outdoor gear to shops across the Blue Ridge. At the beginning of Year 3 it switches from LIFO to FIFO for both financial reporting and tax. Revenues, expenses, and the effect of the new method are in the tables ahead. The tax rate is 25% in every year, and the company pays $100,000 of dividends each year.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

Accounting changes and error corrections have their own Topic: Topic 250, Accounting Changes and Error Corrections. The reporting rules are in ASC 250-10-45 and the disclosure rules are in ASC 250-10-50. Topic 250 is a companion to every other Topic. Other Topics (such as inventory, property, plant, and equipment, and investments) say what a method is, and Topic 250 says how to report a switch to it.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 250-10-45-1 A company may change an accounting principle only if the change is required by a new standard or the company can justify the new principle as preferable.
  • ASC 250-10-45-5 Changes in principle are reported by retrospective application to all prior periods presented, unless that is impracticable.
  • ASC 250-10-45-17 A change in estimate is accounted for in the current period and future periods. Prior periods are not restated.
  • ASC 250-10-45-18 A change in estimate effected by a change in principle, such as a change in depreciation method, is accounted for as a change in estimate.
  • ASC 250-10-50 Disclosure requirements for each kind of change: the nature, the justification, and the effect on each financial statement line item and on per share amounts.

Three types of accounting changes

Every accounting change falls into one of three categories. Deciding which one you have is the first and most important step, because the category tells you how to report it.

The three types of accounting changes
TypeWhat happensExamples
Change in accounting principleThe company switches from one generally accepted principle to another.Switch inventory costing from LIFO to FIFO; adopt a new accounting standard; change from the cost method to the equity method for an investment
Change in accounting estimateThe company revises an estimate because of new information or experience.Revise the useful life or residual value of equipment; change the warranty percentage; revise the periods an intangible asset will benefit; change actuarial assumptions
Change in reporting entityThe financial statements become those of a different reporting entity.Begin issuing consolidated statements in place of individual statements; change which subsidiaries are included in the consolidated group

Two items look like accounting changes but are not. Correcting an error is accounted for much like a change, and we cover it in Detailed Notes B. A new event or transaction that did not exist before is not a change either. For example, adopting a postretirement benefit plan for the first time is just a new transaction to account for.

Watch out

A change in depreciation method is a hybrid. It is a change in principle (you move from one acceptable method to another) and a change in estimate (you are changing your view of how benefits will arrive). It is reported as a change in estimate, which means prospectively. Section 6 explains why.

Three ways to report a change

There are three ways to report a change, and each type of change is matched to one of them.

  1. Retrospective approach. Revise the prior-period financial statements that are presented again for comparison, so they look as if the new method had always been used. Adjust the account balances with a journal entry, and adjust the beginning retained earnings of the earliest year presented for the cumulative effect of earlier years.
  2. Modified retrospective approach. Apply a new standard only to the current (adoption) period, and adjust the beginning retained earnings of the adoption period for the cumulative effect of prior periods. The numbers for prior periods are not revised. The FASB sometimes allows this when it issues a new standard.
  3. Prospective approach. Do not revise anything from the past. No cumulative effect, no restated statements. The new method or estimate simply affects the current year and later years.

Remember

The retrospective approach gives comparability: every year shown in the statements is on the same basis. The price is that numbers investors saw before get replaced. The prospective approach is easier and leaves the past alone, but years on different bases sit side by side.

Changes in accounting principle

Accounting is not an exact science. Management chooses among acceptable methods: FIFO, LIFO, or average cost; straight-line or accelerated depreciation; when to adopt a new standard. Because comparability and consistency make information more useful, methods should generally be followed from year to year. Even so, a company may change, either because a new standard requires it or because the new method is preferable, and the company must be able to explain why.

Why companies change methods

  • New standard. The FASB issues a standard that requires a new method.
  • Economic conditions. In the 1970s, rapid inflation prompted hundreds of companies to switch from FIFO to LIFO.
  • Industry practice. A company adopts the method its competitors use, or adapts to new technology.

Decision makers’ perspective: motivation for accounting choices

It would be nice if every choice were made purely to give users the best information. Research suggests other motives matter too: bonus plans tied to net income, debt covenants that restrict dividends or require minimum ratios, union negotiations, tax savings, and the desire to smooth earnings. A switch is not always in the direction of higher income. LIFO, for example, is popular because it lowers taxable income when prices rise.

Analysts should read every change with two questions in mind. What does the change do to comparability? And does the company’s stated reason hold up, or is there a hidden agenda? When choices are driven by something other than providing useful information, earnings quality suffers: reported earnings become a poorer predictor of future earnings. Some companies have been accused of “cookie jar” accounting, using unrealistic estimates and strategic choices to smooth earnings and meet analysts’ expectations.

The retrospective approach: most changes in principle

Most voluntary changes in principle are reported retrospectively. Reporting a change this way involves four things:

  1. Revise the comparative financial statements. Every year presented is recast as if the new method had been used all along.
  2. Adjust the accounts for the change. A journal entry brings the account balances to what they would have been under the new method, as of the beginning of the year of the change.
  3. Adjust retained earnings. The cumulative effect on years before the earliest year presented goes into the beginning retained earnings of that earliest year.
  4. Disclose. A note explains why the new method is preferable and the effect of the change on every item affected.

Step 5: Disclosure note

In the first set of statements after the change, a note explains the change and why it is preferable (for example, “FIFO better reflects the current cost of inventory on the balance sheet and improves comparability with industry peers”), states that prior periods were retrospectively adjusted, reports the amount of the retained earnings adjustment, and gives the effect on each line item and on per share amounts.

The two cumulative amounts

For a retrospective change, you need only two numbers from the years before the change: the cumulative effect through the end of last year (for the journal entry) and the cumulative effect through the year before the earliest year presented (for the equity statement adjustment). Everything else is recasting the statements one year at a time.

Variations on the retrospective approach

When the new method lowers income: a deferred tax asset

Suppose instead that Catamount Peak switched from FIFO to average cost, and the cumulative effect through the end of Year 2 was to lower inventory by $80 (in thousands). The new method decreases accounting income, but taxable income is unchanged. That creates a temporary difference that will reverse as the inventory is sold, when taxable income will be less than accounting income: a future deductible amount. The company records a deferred tax asset.

Beginning of the year of change: FIFO to average cost ($ in thousands)

Journal entry: Beginning of the year of change: FIFO to average cost ($ in thousands)
AccountDebitCredit
Retained earnings (decrease in net income, after tax)60

If the tax law did not require the recapture in the LIFO-to-FIFO example, the credit would also have gone to a deferred tax liability rather than to income tax payable. Higher accounting income with no change in taxable income is a future taxable amount. Which account you use depends on whether the tax method changed along with the financial reporting method.

The modified retrospective approach

When the FASB issues a new standard, it usually specifies how companies must transition. Commonly it allows full retrospective application, and often it allows the modified retrospective approach too. Under the modified approach, the new standard is applied only to the adoption period and beginning retained earnings of that period is adjusted for the cumulative effect of earlier periods. Prior-period amounts that appear in the comparative statements are left as originally reported, so the company must disclose that the periods are not comparable.

When a change in principle is reported prospectively

Sometimes it is not practicable or not appropriate to go back. There are two situations.

Retrospective application is impracticable

Revising the past requires knowing what the past balances would have been. Suppose a company switches from FIFO to LIFO. LIFO inventory is made of “layers” added in earlier years at the costs of those years. A company that used FIFO never tracked those layers, so its old records usually cannot support a recast. In that case the company applies LIFO prospectively: the beginning inventory of the year of change (measured under the old method) becomes the base-year inventory for all future LIFO calculations. No journal entry is needed, and a note explains why retrospective application was impracticable.

There are also partial cases. If the company has the information to recast some prior years, it applies the new method retrospectively from the earliest year it can:

How much can be recast?
What the company can determineWhat it reports
Period-specific effects for all years, and the cumulative effect before the earliest year presentedFull retrospective application
Period-specific effects for some years, but not allApply the new method retrospectively as of the beginning of the earliest year practicable; adjust beginning retained earnings of that year
Period-specific effects for no prior year, or the cumulative effect cannot be determinedApply the new method prospectively from the earliest date practicable; beginning balances carry forward on the old basis; no retained earnings adjustment

Glenville Lake Brewing: FIFO to LIFO

Glenville Lake Brewing adopts LIFO at the beginning of Year 5. Its December 31, Year 4 inventory was $480,000 under FIFO, and it has no records that would support LIFO layers for earlier years.

No retrospective adjustment is possible, so there is no journal entry for the change. The $480,000 becomes the opening LIFO layer. Year 5 purchases and sales are accounted for under LIFO, and Year 5 and later statements use LIFO. Year 4 and earlier statements are not revised.

A standard requires prospective application

Some changes are prospective because authoritative literature says so. For example, when an investor’s ownership falls from 25% to 12% and the equity method no longer applies, the investor simply stops using the equity method. The carrying amount of the investment at that date becomes the new cost basis for the investment in equity securities. When ownership rises from 12% to 25% and the equity method now applies, the earlier balance (including any unrealized holding gains or losses) is the starting balance for the equity method. Nothing is restated either way.

Changes in accounting estimate

Accounting depends on estimates: useful lives and residual values, uncollectible receivables, warranty costs, the periods an intangible asset benefits, pension assumptions. Estimates are routinely different from what actually happens, so revising them is a normal consequence of getting better information, not a mistake.

A change in estimate is reported prospectively. Nothing is restated and no cumulative effect is reported. The new estimate is used in the year of the change and later years. If the effect is material, a note describes the effect on income from continuing operations, net income, and per share amounts of the current period.

Revising a percentage: warranty expense

Tuckasegee Trail Gear has estimated warranty cost at 2% of sales. After reviewing its claims experience, management concludes that 3% is more realistic. Sales for Year 3 are $6,000,000. There are 500,000 shares outstanding and the tax rate is 25%. No account balances are adjusted.

Revising a life or a residual value

For a depreciable or amortizable asset, take the undepreciated cost (book value) at the date of the change, subtract the new residual value, and spread the result over the new remaining life.

Settling a contingency for a different amount

If a company accrued a loss contingency (say $200,000) and later settles for $350,000, the extra $150,000 is a change in estimate. The company records the additional loss in the period of settlement. Prior years are not revised, because the earlier accrual was the best estimate available at the time.

Changing depreciation, amortization, and depletion methods

Why is a switch from one depreciation method to another treated as a change in estimate rather than a change in principle? Because companies change methods when their expectations about how the benefits will arrive change. A company that chose an accelerated method because it expected greater benefits early, and later sees benefits arriving evenly, switches to straight-line. The change reflects a new estimate of the benefit pattern.

Even though it is accounted for as a change in estimate, the company must still justify the new method as preferable, just as for any change in principle. Most companies that change methods apply the new method only to assets placed in service after the change, which is simply prospective.

Watch out

When you cannot tell whether a change is a change in principle or a change in estimate (for instance, a company begins to capitalize small tools it used to expense), treat it as a change in estimate. The same is true when both occur together.

Change in reporting entity

A reporting entity can be a single company or a group of companies that issues one set of statements. A change in reporting entity occurs when (1) a company presents consolidated statements in place of the statements of individual companies, or (2) the specific companies in the consolidated or combined group change. Some changes are the result of new rules. For example, a manufacturer may be required to consolidate its financing subsidiary.

A change in reporting entity is reported retrospectively: the prior-period statements shown for comparison are recast as if the new entity had existed in those periods. A note describes the nature of the change and the reason, and gives the effect on net income, income from continuing operations, and per share amounts for each period presented.

The exception

The more common reason a group changes is an acquisition. When one company buys another, the acquirer includes the acquiree from the date of acquisition and does not restate its prior statements. That makes year-to-year comparisons harder, so an acquirer discloses pro forma revenue, income, and earnings per share as if the acquisition had occurred at the start of the comparative period.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

U.S. GAAP and IFRS are largely converged on accounting changes: voluntary changes in policy are applied retrospectively, estimates are changed prospectively, and a change must be justified as providing more reliable and relevant information. IFRS has no concept of a “change in estimate effected by a change in principle.” Under IFRS (IAS 8), a company decides whether a change is a policy change or an estimate change, and when it is hard to tell, treats it as an estimate. The main remaining difference is for error corrections, covered in Detailed Notes B.

Common mistakes

  • Restating prior years for a change in estimate. Estimates change prospectively. Nothing is restated.
  • Treating a change in depreciation method as a change in principle. It is reported prospectively, as a change in estimate.
  • Forgetting the tax effect of the cumulative entry. Multiply the pretax cumulative difference by the tax rate; the balance goes to retained earnings. Use income tax payable when the tax method changed with the book method, and deferred taxes when it did not.
  • Adjusting the wrong retained earnings balance. The journal entry covers the cumulative effect through the end of last year. The equity statement adjusts the beginning of the earliest year shown. Those are different amounts.
  • Using the original remaining life. After a change in life, spread the undepreciated cost less the new residual value over the remaining life, not the total life.
  • Forgetting the justification. Any change in principle, including a depreciation method change, must be justified as preferable.

Practice with answers

Problem 1

Fieri Mountain Foods switches from FIFO to average cost at the beginning of Year 3 for both book and tax. Inventory at the end of Year 2 was $560,000 under FIFO and would have been $500,000 under average cost. The tax rate is 25%. Prepare the journal entry.

Show the answer

The new method lowers income, so inventory decreases by $60,000, a deferred tax asset of $60,000 × 25% = $15,000 arises, and retained earnings falls by the remaining $45,000.

Beginning of Year 3

Journal entry: Beginning of Year 3
AccountDebitCredit
Retained earnings45,000
Deferred tax asset15,000
Inventory60,000

Prior years in the comparative statements are also revised, and beginning retained earnings of the earliest year presented is adjusted for the cumulative effect before that year.

Problem 2

A company bought a machine for $330,000 at the beginning of Year 1 with no residual value and a 10-year life, and used SYD. At the beginning of Year 4 it changes to straight-line. What is Year 4 depreciation? Is anything restated?

Show the answer

SYD depreciation: Year 1 = 10/55 × $330,000 = $60,000; Year 2 = 9/55 × $330,000 = $54,000; Year 3 = 8/55 × $330,000 = $48,000. Accumulated: $162,000. Undepreciated cost: $168,000 over the remaining 7 years = $24,000 a year. Nothing is restated (change in estimate). The company must justify the new method as preferable.

Problem 3

Greenleaf Gap Supply accrued a $200,000 loss on a lawsuit last year. This year it settles for $350,000. What is the entry, and are prior statements revised?

Show the answer

Record a loss of $150,000 (debit litigation loss $150,000 and debit litigation liability $200,000 if the liability is still on the books; credit cash $350,000). It is a change in estimate: prior statements are not revised, and no retained earnings adjustment is made.

Problem 4

Waynesville Wire Works used the FIFO method and now switches to LIFO. Records for the years before last year are not available, but the company has all the information it needs to apply LIFO for last year. What does it report?

Show the answer

It cannot determine the cumulative effect before last year, but it can recast last year. It applies LIFO retrospectively as of the beginning of last year: last year’s statements are revised, and prior balances are carried forward on the old basis with no retained earnings adjustment for earlier years. A note explains why full retrospective application was impracticable.

Key terms

Change in accounting principle
A switch from one generally accepted principle to another.
Change in accounting estimate
A revision of an estimate because of new information or experience.
Change in reporting entity
A change that causes the statements to be those of a different entity, such as a change in the consolidated group.
Retrospective approach
Revising prior-period statements as if the new method had always been used, with a cumulative effect adjustment to retained earnings.
Modified retrospective approach
Applying a new standard only to the adoption period and adjusting beginning retained earnings for the cumulative effect.
Prospective approach
Applying the change to the current and future periods only.
Impracticable
Not feasible after reasonable effort, usually because the information needed to revise the past is unavailable.
Preferable
A new method must be justified as better than the old one.
Earnings quality
The ability of reported earnings to predict future earnings.
