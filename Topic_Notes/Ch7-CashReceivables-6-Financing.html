A question to start

A company is owed $400,000 by customers who will pay in 30 to 60 days, but it needs cash this week. It can wait, borrow against the receivables, or sell them. Two of those choices bring in the same cash, yet one adds a liability to the balance sheet and the other doesn't. Which one, and who decides?

These notes cover financing with receivables: when a transfer is a loan and when it is a sale, how to record each, and how analysts read a company's receivables.

Two ways to account for a transfer

Every arrangement for getting cash from receivables is accounted for in one of two ways. The transferor is the company that held the receivables; the transferee is the bank or finance company.

Secured borrowing versus sale
QuestionSecured borrowingSale of receivables
What happens to the receivables?Stay on the transferor's balance sheet as collateralRemoved (derecognized) from the balance sheet
What is credited for the cash received?A liability (note payable)The receivables
Gain or loss at transfer?No; fees and interest are expensesYes, usually a loss
Transferee recordsA note receivableThe receivables, at fair value

Either way the transferor debits Cash. The question is whether the credit goes to a liability or to the receivable.

Secured borrowing: pledging and assigning

When a company pledges its receivables, the whole receivables balance serves as collateral for a loan, and the company keeps collecting from customers. No special entry is needed beyond recording the loan; the arrangement is disclosed. Many asset-based lines of credit let a company borrow up to a set percentage of eligible receivables.

When a company assigns specific receivables, collections from those accounts go toward repaying the loan. The lender usually advances less than the receivables assigned (a cushion for bad debts) and charges a finance fee plus interest.

Dillsboro Pottery assigns receivables

On November 1, Dillsboro Pottery borrows $300,000 from Pisgah Finance and signs a note at 9% interest, payable monthly. It assigns $380,000 of specific receivables as collateral. Pisgah charges a finance fee of 2% of the receivables assigned. During November, Dillsboro collects $240,000 of the assigned receivables and remits that amount, plus November interest, on November 30.

Selling receivables: factoring

In factoring, a company sells its receivables to a financial institution (the factor), which usually collects from customers and charges a fee. Credit card sales are a familiar form of factoring. A key term is recourse:

  • Without recourse: the factor cannot come back to the seller if customers don't pay, so the factor bears the bad-debt risk.
  • With recourse: the seller must reimburse the factor for uncollectible accounts. The seller keeps the bad-debt risk, so the factor typically charges a lower fee.

Factors commonly advance only part of the receivables (say 85% to 92%) and hold back the rest until collections cover the advance and the fee. The seller then has a beneficial interest in the held-back amount, recorded as a receivable from factor at its fair value less the fee.

Franklin Gemstone Distributors factors receivables without recourse

In December, Franklin transfers receivables with a book value of $400,000 to Smoky Mountain Factors without recourse. The factor pays 88% immediately ($352,000) and holds back 12% ($48,000). Its fee is 3% of the receivables ($12,000), taken from the holdback. Franklin estimates the fair value of the held-back amount at $44,000, a bit less than $48,000 because the last accounts collected tend to be reduced by returns and allowances. The transfer meets the conditions for a sale.

December: Record the sale of receivables

Journal entry: December: Record the sale of receivables
AccountDebitCredit
Cash ($400,000 × 88%)352,000
Loss on sale of receivables (to balance)16,000
Receivable from factor ($44,000 − $12,000 fee)32,000
Accounts receivable (book value sold)400,000

The $16,000 loss equals the $12,000 fee plus the $4,000 write-down of the holdback to fair value ($48,000 − $44,000).

If the factor instead withheld its fee from the cash advance, Franklin would debit Cash $340,000 and Receivable from factor $44,000; the loss would still be $16,000.

The same sale with recourse

Now assume Franklin sells the receivables with recourse and estimates the fair value of its recourse obligation at $4,000.

December: Record the sale with recourse

Journal entry: December: Record the sale with recourse
AccountDebitCredit
Cash352,000
Loss on sale of receivables (to balance)20,000
Receivable from factor32,000
Recourse liability4,000
Accounts receivable400,000

The recourse liability increases the loss by $4,000. If every receivable is eventually collected, Franklin removes the liability and records a gain.

Securitization

In a securitization, a company transfers a pool of receivables (trade receivables, credit card balances, or loans) to a special purpose entity (SPE), usually a trust or subsidiary. The SPE sells securities backed by the receivables to investors. Because the SPE is typically "bankruptcy remote," investors are protected, and the company can reach a large pool of investors at favorable rates. The company often keeps servicing the receivables for a fee.

Discounting a note receivable

Transferring a note receivable to a bank is called discounting. The bank pays the note's maturity value less a discount, its fee for advancing the cash. Like other transfers, it can be a sale or a secured borrowing.

  1. Accrue interest earned up to the date the note is discounted.
  2. Compute the maturity value: face amount plus all interest to maturity.
  3. Subtract the bank's discount: maturity value × the bank's discount rate × time remaining. The result is the cash proceeds.

Cherokee Creek Lumber discounts a note

On October 1, Year 1, Cherokee Creek Lumber sells land and accepts a $250,000, 12-month, 8% note; principal and interest are due September 30, Year 2. It accrues $5,000 of interest at December 31, Year 1 ($250,000 × 8% × 3/12). On March 31, Year 2, it discounts the note at Western Carolina Bank, which charges a 10% discount rate. The transfer is without recourse and qualifies as a sale.

Sale or secured borrowing: who decides?

Companies usually prefer sale treatment, because it makes them look less leveraged and more liquid.

Why transferors prefer sale treatment
EffectSaleSecured borrowingWhy sale looks better
Receivables removed from assets?YesNoLower total assets, higher return on assets
Liability for the cash received?NoYesLower debt and debt-to-equity
Where is the cash in the statement of cash flows?May be operatingFinancingCan raise operating cash flow
Gain at transfer?More likelyLess likelyCan raise income

So GAAP sets the test: the transfer is a sale only if the transferor surrenders control, which requires all three conditions. If any one fails, it is a secured borrowing.

  1. Isolation. The transferred assets are beyond the reach of the transferor and its creditors, even in bankruptcy.
  2. Right to pledge or exchange. Each transferee can pledge or sell the assets it received.
  3. No effective control. The transferor does not keep effective control, for example through an agreement that is likely to return the assets to it.

Recourse alone does not prevent sale treatment; it adds a recourse liability. Transfers of only part of a receivable must also qualify as a participating interest (a proportionate share of the cash flows), or they are secured borrowings.

A cautionary tale: Repo 105

Before its 2008 bankruptcy, Lehman Brothers transferred financial assets to banks near each quarter-end, called the transfers sales, and used the cash to pay down debt, making it look far less leveraged. It agreed to buy the assets back the next quarter. In substance these were loans. The transactions were poorly disclosed, and when they came to light the reaction was severe. The FASB later tightened the guidance to close the loophole.

Because sale accounting can hide risk, companies that sell receivables but keep some involvement must disclose the transfer, their continuing involvement, ongoing risks, how fair values were estimated, cash flows with the transferee, and the quality of the transferred assets (for example, amounts past due).

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

U.S. GAAP asks one main question: was control surrendered? IFRS 9 asks first whether substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership were transferred (sale) or kept (borrowing). Only if neither is true does it look at control. The two frameworks often agree but can differ for the same transaction.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 860-10-40-5 lists the three conditions for surrender of control that are required for sale accounting.
  • ASC 860-10-40-6A defines a participating interest for partial transfers.
  • ASC 860-20 covers transfers accounted for as sales, with assets obtained and liabilities incurred measured at fair value.
  • ASC 860-10-50 sets the disclosure requirements for transfers with continuing involvement.

Reading receivables like an analyst

A company's investment in receivables depends on its sales level, what it sells, and its credit and collection policies. Looser credit terms can lift sales but tie up more cash in receivables and raise bad debts; cash discounts speed collection but cost money.

  • Receivables turnover = Net sales ÷ Average accounts receivable (net)
  • Average collection period = 365 days ÷ Receivables turnover

Mountain Gear sells lower-priced goods to retailers on short terms; Blue Ridge sells expensive equipment to hospitals that take longer to pay. Comparisons are meaningful only within an industry and against the company's own history and credit terms. If receivables grow faster than sales, turnover falls, which may signal unhappy customers, overly generous terms to chase sales, or rising bad debts.

Earnings quality

Watch for (1) allowances built up in good years and released in bad years, (2) aggressive fair value estimates in securitizations that create gains, and (3) receivable sales reported in operating cash flows, which can make operating cash flow look stronger. Many sophisticated investors and rating agencies treat receivable sales as borrowings when assessing risk.

Common mistakes

  • Removing assigned or pledged receivables from the balance sheet. A secured borrowing keeps them and adds a liability.
  • Recording the full holdback as the receivable from factor. Use its fair value, less the factor's fee.
  • Treating recourse as automatically making the transfer a borrowing. If control is surrendered, it is still a sale; record a recourse liability.
  • Computing the bank's discount on the face amount. Use the maturity value and the time remaining.
  • Forgetting to accrue interest up to the date a note is discounted.

Net sales are $6,300,000; net receivables were $700,000 at the start and $560,000 at the end of the year. Compute turnover and the average collection period.

Show the answer

Key terms

Transferor / transferee
The company giving up receivables / the institution receiving them.
Secured borrowing
A transfer treated as a loan; receivables stay on the books as collateral and a liability is recorded.
Pledging
Using the entire receivables balance as collateral for a loan.
Assigning
Using specific receivables as collateral, with their collections applied to the loan.
Factoring
Selling receivables to a financial institution that collects them for a fee.
Recourse
The seller's obligation to reimburse the buyer for uncollectible receivables.
Receivable from factor
The seller's right to the held-back amount, measured at fair value less the fee.
Securitization
Transferring a pool of receivables to a special purpose entity that issues securities backed by them.
Discounting
Transferring a note receivable to a bank for its maturity value less a discount.
Receivables turnover
Net sales divided by average net receivables.
Average collection period
365 divided by receivables turnover; the approximate days receivables are outstanding.
