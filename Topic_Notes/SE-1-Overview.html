Why this unit matters

Every share a company sells, every share it buys back, and every dividend it pays changes what the owners hold. This unit explains how those owner transactions are recorded and reported, and why some of them change total equity while others only move amounts around inside it.

Three questions will guide you through the unit:

  • When a company sells shares for $25 each, why does only a small slice land in the “common stock” account?
  • When a company buys back its own shares for more than it sold them for, where does the extra cost go, and why is there never a gain or loss?
  • Why does a two-for-one stock split need no journal entry, while a 10% stock dividend moves millions out of retained earnings?

The nature of shareholders’ equity and issuing shares

  • Describe the components of shareholders’ equity and explain how they relate to each other.
  • Describe comprehensive income and its components, and explain how AOCI is reported.
  • Explain the corporate form of organization and the rights of common and preferred shares.
  • Record the issuance of shares for cash, for noncash consideration, and as a package of securities.

Buybacks, retained earnings, and dividends

  • Distinguish between accounting for retired shares and for treasury shares, and record both.
  • Describe retained earnings and distinguish it from paid-in capital.
  • Record cash and property dividends and allocate dividends between preferred and common shares.
  • Explain stock dividends and stock splits and record them.
  • Describe the main differences between U.S. GAAP and IFRS for shareholders’ equity.

The big idea

Shareholders’ equity is what is left of the assets after creditors are paid: assets − liabilities, or net assets. It comes from two sources: what owners invested (paid-in capital) and what the company earned and kept (retained earnings). Two further pieces adjust it: gains and losses that bypass net income (accumulated other comprehensive income) and shares bought back (treasury stock).

Every transaction in this unit can be analyzed with the same three questions:

  1. Did net assets change? Cash in from selling shares raises equity; cash out for dividends and buybacks lowers it. Stock dividends and splits move no assets.
  2. Is it a transaction with owners? Owner transactions never create income statement gains or losses. They go straight to equity accounts.
  3. Which equity accounts move? Invested capital goes to the stock accounts and additional paid-in capital; distributions of earnings come out of retained earnings.

Remember

A company cannot earn income by trading in its own shares. Differences on buybacks and treasury stock resales go to paid-in capital or retained earnings, never to the income statement, and retained earnings can be reduced by these transactions but never increased.

Rules of thumb

  • Noncash consideration is recorded at the fair value of the shares or of what was received, whichever is more clearly evident.
  • A package of securities is allocated by relative fair values (proportional), or by the known value with the rest to the other (incremental).
  • Mandatorily redeemable preferred shares are liabilities.
  • Dividends in arrears on cumulative preferred are disclosed, not recorded, and must be paid before common dividends.
  • Comprehensive income = net income + other comprehensive income. OCI accumulates in AOCI, not retained earnings.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

  • Return on equity. Net income ÷ average shareholders’ equity. With preferred stock, use net income less preferred dividends ÷ average common equity. Compare with the earnings-price ratio, which uses market value instead of book value.
  • Buybacks. They raise earnings per share and return on equity mechanically by shrinking shares and equity. A buyback during an earnings slowdown can make growth look better than it is.
  • Book value versus market value. Equity on the balance sheet reflects amounts invested and earned, not what the company is worth. The gap widens as a company ages.
  • Dividend policy. High payouts are typical of mature companies; growth companies usually reinvest. A stock dividend in place of a cash dividend gives shareholders nothing of value.

Check yourself

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. A company sells 50,000 shares of $2 par common stock for $30 a share. What is recorded in each equity account?

Show the answer

Cash rises by $1,500,000. Common stock is credited for 50,000 × $2 = $100,000, and paid-in capital—excess of par for the remaining $1,400,000.

2. Is a $40,000 after-tax unrealized gain on available-for-sale debt investments part of net income?

Show the answer

No. It is other comprehensive income. It is reported in comprehensive income for the period and added to accumulated other comprehensive income in equity, not to retained earnings.

3. A company buys treasury shares for $20 and later resells them for $26. Is there a gain?

Show the answer

No. The $6 per share excess is credited to paid-in capital—share repurchase. Transactions in a company’s own shares never produce income statement gains or losses.

4. Cumulative preferred dividends of $50,000 were skipped last year. Is there a liability?

Show the answer

No. Dividends in arrears are disclosed in the notes. A liability exists only when the board declares a dividend, and then the arrears must be paid before common shareholders receive anything.

5. How do a 10% stock dividend and a 2-for-1 stock split differ in their accounting?

Show the answer

The 10% stock dividend moves the fair value of the new shares from retained earnings to paid-in capital. The stock split requires no entry; the par value per share is cut in half and the number of shares doubles. Neither changes total equity.
