Time Value of Money

Reading Notes — Present Value Concepts

A note on financial calculators: Examples below show keystrokes for a standard financial calculator (BA II Plus / similar). The five time-value keys are N (number of periods), I/Y (interest rate per period, entered as a whole number like 10, not 0.10), PV, PMT, and FV. Enter any three known values and solve (CPT) for the fourth. Calculators follow the cash flow sign convention — money you pay out is entered as negative, money you receive is positive — which is why a computed PV or FV often displays as a negative number even though the dollar amount is correct.

Present Value of a Single Amount

Future value asks: "If I invest money today, how much will it grow to later?" Present value flips that question around: "How much would I need today to end up with a specific amount later?"

Definition: The present value (PV) of a single amount is the amount of money today that is equivalent to a given amount to be received or paid in the future, given a specified interest rate.

Because present value is the mirror image of future value, the formula is the same relationship — just solved in the other direction:

FV = I(1 + i)ⁿ   →   PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)ⁿ

Instead of dividing by hand, you can use Table 2 (Present Value of $1), which gives you a factor to multiply by the future amount. The interest rate used in these calculations is often called the discount rate, and the process of finding a present value is called discounting.

Two patterns worth memorizing

1. The higher the interest rate, the lower the present value. A higher rate means money grows faster, so you'd need less of it today to reach the same future goal.

2. The further into the future the amount is, the lower the present value. The longer you have to wait, the less that future dollar is worth today.

You will receive $10,000 in 4 years. If your discount rate is 9%, what is that $10,000 worth today?

What Is an Annuity?

Definition: An annuity is a series of equal-sized cash flows occurring at equal intervals of time.

Think car payments, rent, bond interest payments, or retirement withdrawals — anything where the same dollar amount shows up on a regular schedule. There are two flavors, and the difference comes down to when in the period the payment happens:

Ordinary annuity

Payments occur at the end of each period.

Today ---- $ ---- $ ---- $ (end of period 3) end yr1 end yr2 end yr3

Annuity due

Payments occur at the beginning of each period (the first payment happens immediately).

Today $ ---- $ ---- $ (last payment at beginning of period 3) begin yr1 begin yr2 begin yr3
Example 1

You pay $500 rent on the first of every month for a year. Is this an ordinary annuity or an annuity due?

Annuity due — payment happens at the start of each period (the month), not the end.
Example 2

A bond pays $10,000 of interest every June 30 and December 31, with the bond issued on June 30 and the first payment not occurring until six months later (December 31). Is this an ordinary annuity or an annuity due?

Ordinary annuity — each payment is made at the end of the six-month period it covers, not the beginning.

Why it matters: whether cash flows are an ordinary annuity or an annuity due changes which present value (and future value) table/factor you use — annuity due payments are worth more today because you receive/pay them sooner.

Present Value of Annuities

Since most real-world financial instruments (bonds, leases, loans, pensions) involve a series of equal payments rather than one lump sum, present value of an annuity is the calculation you'll use most often in this course.

Present Value of an Ordinary Annuity (PVA)

The present value of a series of equal cash flows, with the first payment at the end of the first period.

You could find this by discounting each payment separately using Table 2 and adding them up — but Table 4 (Present Value of an Ordinary Annuity of $1) already does that summing for you. Just multiply the annuity amount by the Table 4 factor.

PVA = Annuity payment × PVA factor (Table 4)
Example

You'll pay $10,000 at the end of each year for 3 years for a graduate program, and your time value of money is 10%. What is the cost today?

Present Value of an Annuity Due (PVAD)

The present value of a series of equal cash flows, with the first payment immediate (beginning of the period).

Because the first payment isn't discounted at all (it happens today), a PVAD is always larger than a PVA of the same size and length. You can find the PVAD factor from Table 6, or take a shortcut: the PVAD factor equals the PVA factor for one fewer period, plus 1.0.

PVAD factor (n periods) = PVA factor (n − 1 periods) + 1.0
Example

Same graduate program as above — $10,000 per year for 3 years — but now the first payment is due today instead of at year-end. Time value of money is still 10%.

Present Value of a Deferred Annuity

The present value of a series of equal cash flows where the first payment doesn't start until more than one period after the agreement begins.

There's no single "deferred annuity" table. Instead, find the present value of each individual payment separately (using Table 2, the PV of $1 for each specific future period) and add them together.

Example

You'll receive three payments of $10,000, but the first one isn't until 3 years from now, then years 4 and 5. Time value of money is 10%. What's the value today?

A deferred annuity always takes two calculator steps: first value it as an ordinary annuity as of one period before the first payment, then discount that single lump sum back to today.

Quick comparison

PVA: equal payments, first one in one period.

PVAD: equal payments, first one right now.

Deferred annuity: equal payments, but a waiting period (more than one period) before the first one — no shortcut table, so discount each payment individually and sum.
