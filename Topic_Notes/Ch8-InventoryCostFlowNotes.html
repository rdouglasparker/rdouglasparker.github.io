Inventory Cost Flow Assumptions

Reading Notes — FIFO, LIFO, Average Cost, and Choosing a Method

FIFO, LIFO, and Average Cost

When identical units are purchased at different prices throughout the year, a company needs some way to decide which costs belong in Cost of Goods Sold and which stay in Ending Inventory. That's what a cost flow assumption does — and importantly, it doesn't have to match how the units actually moved physically off the shelf.

Goods available for sale = beginning inventory + purchases. This total dollar amount gets split between two places: Cost of Goods Sold and Ending Inventory. Every method starts from this same total — they just divide it up differently.

First-In, First-Out (FIFO)

Assumes the oldest costs are sold first. Ending inventory is made up of the most recently purchased costs. FIFO gives the same answer whether a company uses a perpetual or periodic system — the oldest costs are the oldest costs no matter when during the year you check.

Last-In, First-Out (LIFO)

Assumes the most recently purchased costs are sold first. Ending inventory is left holding the oldest costs. Unlike FIFO, LIFO can give different answers under a perpetual system versus a periodic system — a perpetual system applies "last in" at the moment of each individual sale, while a periodic system waits until year-end and treats the whole year's purchases as one pool.

Average Cost

Instead of tracking specific cost layers, this method spreads the total cost evenly across all units.

Weighted-average unit cost = Cost of goods available for sale ÷ Total units available

Under a periodic system, this average is calculated once, at the end of the period (weighted-average). Under a perpetual system, a new average is recalculated after every purchase, and that new average costs the units sold until the next purchase (moving-average).

Example 1 — FIFO, LIFO, and Average Cost (Periodic)

Whiteside Mountain Supply Co. has the following activity for its one product during the period:

UnitsUnit CostTotal Cost
Beginning inventory500$10.00$5,000
Purchase 1300$12.00$3,600
Purchase 2400$13.00$5,200
Goods available for sale1,200$13,800

900 units were sold, leaving 300 units on hand. Calculate ending inventory and cost of goods sold under FIFO, LIFO, and weighted-average.

FIFO: the 300 units on hand are assumed to be the most recently purchased — all from Purchase 2.
Ending inventory = 300 × $13.00 = $3,900. COGS = $13,800 − $3,900 = $9,900.

LIFO: the 300 units on hand are assumed to be the oldest — all from beginning inventory.
Ending inventory = 300 × $10.00 = $3,000. COGS = $13,800 − $3,000 = $10,800.

Weighted-average: unit cost = $13,800 ÷ 1,200 = $11.50.
Ending inventory = 300 × $11.50 = $3,450. COGS = 900 × $11.50 = $10,350.

Notice the pattern: since unit costs were rising all year, FIFO produced the lowest COGS, LIFO the highest, and average cost fell in between. That pattern is worth remembering — it's the whole reason the method chosen matters.

Example 2 — Perpetual LIFO vs. Periodic LIFO

A small boutique tracks one item. Beginning inventory is 100 units at $5.00. It sells 60 units, then purchases 80 units at $6.00, then sells another 70 units. (180 units available, 130 sold, 50 remain.)

Perpetual LIFO applies "last in, first out" at the moment of each sale:

  • Sale of 60: only layer available is the beginning 100 @ $5.00, so all 60 come from there → $300. (40 units @ $5.00 remain.)
  • Purchase of 80 @ $6.00 is added.
  • Sale of 70: the most recent layer (80 @ $6.00) is used first → 70 × $6.00 = $420. (10 units @ $6.00 remain, plus the 40 @ $5.00.)

Perpetual COGS = $300 + $420 = $720. Perpetual ending inventory = (40 × $5.00) + (10 × $6.00) = $260.

Periodic LIFO ignores the timing and treats the whole period's purchases as one pool, applied only at year-end: 130 units sold are assumed to come from the most recent total purchases first — all 80 units @ $6.00, plus 50 more from the beginning layer @ $5.00.

Periodic COGS = (80 × $6.00) + (50 × $5.00) = $480 + $250 = $730. Periodic ending inventory = 50 × $5.00 = $250.

Same facts, two different answers ($720 vs. $730) — purely because of when LIFO gets applied, not because of any error. This is the one case in this chapter where the choice of perpetual vs. periodic actually changes a dollar amount, not just the bookkeeping mechanics.

Choosing an Inventory Method

If every method starts from the same total cost of goods available for sale, why would a company prefer one over another? Three considerations drive the choice.

Physical flow

A company is not required to pick a method that matches how goods actually move off the shelf. FIFO happens to mirror physical flow for perishable goods (grocers really do try to sell the oldest milk first). Average cost fits commingled goods like fuel or grain, where individual units can't be told apart. Very few companies actually sell inventory in a true last-in-first-out pattern — LIFO is a reporting choice, not a physical description.

Income taxes

In a period of rising costs, LIFO assigns the newest (highest) costs to Cost of Goods Sold, which produces a lower reported taxable income than FIFO would — meaning lower taxes paid right now. This isn't a permanent tax savings, just a deferral: the tax will eventually catch up if inventory quantities or costs later decline. Still, deferring a tax payment has real value because of the time value of money. This is the single biggest reason companies choose LIFO. In a period of falling costs, the effect flips — LIFO would produce the higher taxable income, so the tax incentive disappears.

Matching

LIFO's supporters argue it does a better job matching current costs against current revenues on the income statement, since the most recently purchased (most current) costs flow to Cost of Goods Sold. Critics point out this comes at the cost of an outdated, unrealistic balance sheet, since ending inventory is left holding old, sometimes decades-old cost layers.

Direction of the effect depends on which way costs are moving

Cost TrendHigher COGS / Lower IncomeLower COGS / Higher Income
Rising costsLIFOFIFO
Falling costsFIFOLIFO
Example 1 — Rising Costs

Using the Whiteside Mountain Supply Co. data from Example 1 above (costs rose from $10 to $12 to $13 during the period), how much lower would taxable income be under LIFO than under FIFO?

LIFO COGS ($10,800) − FIFO COGS ($9,900) = $900 lower income under LIFO. Because costs were rising, LIFO pushed more cost into COGS, shrinking reported income (and taxable income) by $900 compared to FIFO.
Example 2 — Falling Costs

Now suppose Whiteside Mountain Supply Co.'s costs had instead been falling during the year:

UnitsUnit CostTotal Cost
Beginning inventory500$13.00$6,500
Purchase 1300$12.00$3,600
Purchase 2400$10.00$4,000
Goods available for sale1,200$14,100

Again, 900 units are sold and 300 remain. What is LIFO's cost of goods sold now, and is it higher or lower than FIFO's?

LIFO ending inventory = 300 units from the oldest layer (beginning inventory @ $13.00) = $3,900. LIFO COGS = $14,100 − $3,900 = $10,200.

FIFO ending inventory = 300 units from the most recent layer (Purchase 2 @ $10.00) = $3,000. FIFO COGS = $14,100 − $3,000 = $11,100.

This time LIFO's COGS ($10,200) is lower than FIFO's ($11,100) — the direction flipped completely because costs were falling instead of rising. LIFO isn't "always" the low-income method; it depends entirely on which way prices are moving.
