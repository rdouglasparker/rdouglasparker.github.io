Why this unit matters

Open any company’s income statement and you will find an income tax expense. Open its tax return and you will find a different number, the tax actually owed. This unit explains why those two figures are almost never equal, and how accountants keep the difference from misleading investors.

Three questions will guide you through the unit:

  • Why is the tax expense a company reports usually different from the tax it pays?
  • Why can a cut in tax rates reduce a company’s reported earnings in the year the cut becomes law?
  • What is an operating loss carryforward, and how can it save a company cash?

By the end of the unit you will be able to answer each one with numbers, not just words.

When you finish this unit, you should be able to do the following.

Temporary differences and deferred taxes

  • Explain why financial reporting and tax rules produce different income in a given year, and use the four-step method to calculate income tax expense.
  • Identify the temporary differences that create deferred tax liabilities, and calculate the amounts for the entry.
  • Identify the temporary differences that create deferred tax assets, and calculate the amounts for the entry.
  • Decide when a valuation allowance is needed for a deferred tax asset, and record it.

Permanent differences, rates, and multiple differences

  • Explain why permanent differences leave no deferred tax behind.
  • Describe how a change in tax rates changes deferred tax balances and reported income.

Operating losses and financial statement reporting

  • Describe when and how the tax effects of a net operating loss are recognized.
  • Show where deferred taxes belong on the balance sheet, and describe the tax disclosures companies make.

Uncertainty, allocation, and global practice

  • Account for tax positions that the tax authority might challenge.
  • Allocate a period’s income tax among the items in the income statement that caused it (intraperiod allocation).
  • Explain how U.S. GAAP and IFRS differ in accounting for income taxes.

The big idea

A company’s income statement and its tax return follow different rulebooks. When an item is counted in different years, the result is a temporary difference, and it leaves a future tax consequence behind. Accountants record that consequence now, in a deferred tax account, so each year’s expense matches the activity that caused it.

Every problem in this unit comes down to the same four steps:

  1. Compute income tax payable. Adjust pretax income to taxable income, then apply the tax rate.
  2. Find the ending deferred tax balances. Multiply the future taxable amounts by the enacted rate for the liability, and the future deductible amounts by the enacted rate for the asset.
  3. Find the changes in those balances. Ending balance minus beginning balance.
  4. Solve for income tax expense. It is the balancing figure in the journal entry. It is never calculated first.

Remember

Pay less tax now than the income statement suggests, and you record a deferred tax liability. Pay more now, and you record a deferred tax asset.

Cheat sheet

Which temporary differences create which deferred tax?

Quick reference: what each temporary difference creates
ItemIncome statement first, tax return laterTax return first, income statement later
Revenue or gainDeferred tax liability (installment sales, unrealized investment gains)Deferred tax asset (cash received in advance, taxed when received)
Expense or lossDeferred tax asset (warranty, bad debts, accrued losses)Deferred tax liability (faster tax depreciation, prepaid expenses)

Formulas

  • Taxable income = pretax accounting income ± permanent differences ± the year’s temporary differences.
  • Income tax payable = taxable income × tax rate.
  • Deferred tax liability = future taxable amounts × enacted rate. Deferred tax asset = future deductible amounts × enacted rate. (Or use the book value minus tax basis gap.)
  • Income tax expense = tax payable + increase in the liability − increase in the asset ± change in the valuation allowance.
  • Effective tax rate = income tax expense ÷ pretax accounting income.

Rules of thumb

  • A valuation allowance is needed if it is more likely than not (above 50%) that some of a deferred tax asset will not be realized.
  • Use enacted tax rates only, and restate deferred balances in the year a new rate is enacted.
  • All deferred taxes are classified as noncurrent on the balance sheet.

Special situations at a glance

Permanent differences

Tax-free interest, nondeductible fines, and similar items never reverse, so they are removed from pretax income to compute taxable income and they create no deferred tax. They do change the effective tax rate. See Detailed Notes B for the full treatment.

Changes in tax rates

When a new rate is enacted, deferred tax assets and liabilities are restated at once, and the entire effect goes through income tax expense in that year. A rate cut raises income for a company with a net deferred tax liability and lowers it for a company with a net deferred tax asset. See Detailed Notes B.

Net operating losses

A net operating loss occurs when deductible expenses exceed taxable revenue. There is no tax payable for that year, but the loss can reduce taxable income in later profitable years. Under current federal rules, most companies carry a loss forward indefinitely and can use it against up to 80% of each future year’s taxable income. The carryback that used to refund earlier taxes is now limited to certain businesses, such as some insurers and farms. Accounting records the benefit in the year of the loss, as a deferred tax asset, and a valuation allowance reduces it if its realization is doubtful.

Pandemic-era rules

For losses arising in certain tax years around 2018 to 2020, emergency legislation suspended the 80% limit and allowed a carryback of up to five years. Companies could claim immediate refunds, often of taxes paid at higher rates, and avoid a valuation allowance on the deferred tax asset. These were temporary provisions, so check the rules in effect for any problem you work.

Balance sheet presentation and disclosures

All deferred tax assets, deferred tax liabilities, and valuation allowances are classified as noncurrent. If they relate to the same taxpaying component of the company and the same tax jurisdiction, they are netted into a single amount. Otherwise they are reported separately.

Netting deferred taxes: a company with a $100,000 liability, a $50,000 asset, and a $20,000 allowance
SituationBalance sheet presentation
Same taxpayer and jurisdictionNoncurrent deferred tax liability, net: $70,000 ($100,000 − [$50,000 − $20,000])
Different taxpaying components or jurisdictionsNoncurrent deferred tax liability: $100,000, and separately a noncurrent deferred tax asset: $30,000 ($50,000 − $20,000)

The tax note must show, among other things: current and deferred portions of tax expense; the components of deferred tax assets and liabilities; the total valuation allowance and its change; a rate reconciliation; and the amounts and expiration dates of any operating loss carryforwards.

Uncertain tax positions

When a company takes a position on its tax return that the tax authority might challenge, it uses a two-step test:

  1. Recognition. Record a tax benefit only if it is more likely than not (above 50%) the position will be upheld on its technical merits, assuming the tax authority reviews it with full knowledge.
  2. Measurement. Record the largest amount of benefit that has a cumulative probability greater than 50% of being realized. The remainder goes in a long-term liability for uncertain tax positions.

Hunter Ridge Holdings (amounts in thousands)

A deduction reduces Hunter Ridge’s tax payable by $500. Management estimates the chances that various amounts of the benefit will be upheld. Tax payable, with the full deduction, is $1,200.

Intraperiod tax allocation

A period’s total income tax is spread across the items that created it, so users can see each piece in context. Income from continuing operations carries its own tax. Discontinued operations are reported net of tax, a gain with a tax expense and a loss with a tax benefit. Items of other comprehensive income also appear net of their tax effects.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

The two frameworks use similar approaches, but reported deferred taxes still differ often. First, U.S. GAAP records every deferred tax asset and then reduces it with a valuation allowance, while IFRS records an asset only when realization is probable. Second, nontax accounting differences between the frameworks (for example, the threshold for accruing a loss contingency) feed through to deferred taxes.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

  • Deferred tax liabilities and risk. A liability adds to total debt. Some analysts leave it out because, for a company that keeps investing in equipment, it may never be paid. Others count it as debt, and research suggests investors treat it as a real liability, discounted for how far away the payment is. Be alert to plant closures or shrinking asset bases that could force a payback.
  • Operating loss carryforwards. A large carryforward lets a company earn future income with little or no cash tax. That is a real advantage, and one reason why profitable companies sometimes acquire unprofitable ones.
  • Valuation allowances and earnings quality. Management judgment decides the allowance, so it can be used to smooth or manage earnings. An increase can signal pessimism about future profits.
  • Tax expense as a lever. Many executives care about the effect of tax expense on earnings per share at least as much as cash taxes paid. Question surprising swings in the effective tax rate.

Check yourself

Try each question before opening the answer.

1. In your own words, why can tax expense differ from tax paid?

Show the answer

Financial reporting standards and tax rules sometimes recognize an item in different years. Those temporary differences leave future taxable or deductible amounts. The company pays tax in the year the tax return says, but the income statement reports the tax belonging to that year’s activity, so the two numbers differ until the differences reverse.

2. A company with a 25% rate has pretax income of $200,000. Taxable income is $140,000 because of a temporary difference that will reverse. What are tax payable, the deferred tax change, and tax expense?

Show the answer

Tax payable is $140,000 × 25% = $35,000. The $60,000 of postponed income creates a deferred tax liability of $15,000, which is an increase of $15,000 if the beginning balance is zero. Tax expense is $35,000 + $15,000 = $50,000, which is 25% of pretax income.

3. Does a $10,000 nondeductible fine create a deferred tax asset?

Show the answer

No. A fine is a permanent difference. It will never be deductible, so nothing will reverse and there is no deferred tax. It increases taxable income in the year it is incurred and pushes the effective tax rate above the statutory rate.

4. Why might a cut in future tax rates reduce reported earnings?

Show the answer

A company with a net deferred tax asset must restate it at the lower rate, because future deductions will now save less tax. The reduction is recorded as income tax expense in the year the cut is enacted. A company with a net deferred tax liability gets the opposite result: its liability shrinks and income rises.

5. When may a company record the tax benefit of a questionable deduction, and how much?

Show the answer

Only if it is more likely than not (above 50%) that the position will be upheld. The amount recorded is the largest benefit with a cumulative probability above 50% of being sustained. The rest is recorded as a long-term liability for uncertain tax positions.

6. Where does the tax benefit of a net operating loss appear, and when?

Show the answer

It appears in the year of the loss, as a reduction of income tax expense (an income tax benefit), with a deferred tax asset on the balance sheet. A valuation allowance reduces the asset if it is more likely than not that the carryforward will go unused.
