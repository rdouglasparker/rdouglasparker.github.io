Why this unit matters

Inventory is often a company’s largest current asset, and cost of goods sold is usually its largest expense. Earlier you learned how to assign costs to inventory with FIFO, LIFO, and average cost. This unit finishes the job: what to do when inventory loses value, how to estimate inventory without counting it, how to handle LIFO when prices change, and how to fix things when the method changes or a mistake is found.

Three questions will guide you through the unit:

  • A store paid $42,000 for jackets that will now sell for less than that. Why is a loss recorded before the jackets are sold?
  • A national retailer with thousands of stores produces quarterly statements. How can it report inventory without counting every shelf?
  • A count error overstated last year’s inventory. Which years’ income is wrong, and why might no entry be needed to fix it?

What you will be able to do

When you finish this unit, you should be able to do the following.

Subsequent measurement

  • Apply the lower of cost or net realizable value rule to inventory costed with FIFO or average cost.
  • Apply the lower of cost or market rule, with its ceiling and floor, to inventory costed with LIFO or the retail method.
  • Record an inventory write-down and explain why U.S. GAAP does not allow reversals while IFRS does.
  • Record losses on purchase commitments.

Estimating inventory

  • Estimate ending inventory and cost of goods sold with the gross profit method.
  • Estimate ending inventory with the retail inventory method using average cost, the conventional (LCM) approach, and LIFO retail.
  • Handle freight, returns, discounts, shortages, and employee discounts in the retail method.

Dollar-value LIFO retail, changes, and errors

  • Determine ending inventory with the dollar-value LIFO retail method.
  • Account for a change in inventory method, retrospectively or prospectively.
  • Analyze the effects of an inventory error and correct it.

The big idea

Two ideas carry the whole unit.

  1. Inventory is never reported above what it is worth to the company. When its value falls below cost, write it down in the period the value is lost. The written-down amount becomes the new cost.
  2. Goods available for sale are either sold or still on hand. Every estimation method, every LIFO layer, and every inventory error comes back to this equation: beginning inventory + net purchases = cost of goods sold + ending inventory.

Remember

Which write-down test applies depends on the cost flow method: LCNRV for FIFO, average cost, and specific identification; LCM for LIFO and the retail method.

Check your understanding

Try each question, then open the answer.

1. Which test?

A company uses LIFO for its hardware and FIFO for its garden supplies. Which write-down test applies to each?

Show the answer

Hardware (LIFO): lower of cost or market. Garden supplies (FIFO): lower of cost or net realizable value.

2. Conventional retail

Why does leaving markdowns out of the cost-to-retail percentage produce a lower inventory amount?

Show the answer

Leaving markdowns out makes the retail denominator larger, so the percentage is smaller. A smaller percentage times the same ending retail gives a lower cost, which approximates lower of average cost or market.

3. Self-correcting errors

Why is no entry needed when a Year 1 inventory error is discovered after the Year 2 books are closed?

Show the answer

The Year 1 error reversed in Year 2, so retained earnings and inventory are already correct. Only the income statements presented for comparison are restated.
