ACCT 351 · Revenue Recognition — Chapter 6, Part C

Accounting for Long-Term Contracts

Reading Notes

Why Long-Term Contracts Need Special Treatment

Think of a construction company building an office tower over three years, or a defense contractor building a satellite over five. The five-step revenue recognition process you already know still applies — but two steps deserve extra attention: Step 2 (identifying the performance obligation) and Step 5 (deciding when revenue is recognized).

Step 2, simplified: even though a construction project involves dozens of distinct goods and services (concrete, wiring, labor, inspections), they usually are not separately identifiable because the seller's job is to combine them into one finished asset for the customer. That means most long-term contracts are treated as a single performance obligation.

Step 5: over time, or at a point in time?

A seller recognizes revenue over time (as work is performed) rather than waiting until completion when either of these is true:

  • The customer controls the asset as it's being built (common when the customer owns the work-in-process), or
  • The seller is building something with no alternative use to anyone else, and the seller has the right to be paid for progress even if the customer cancels.

Most long-term construction contracts meet one of these conditions, so most qualify for revenue recognition over time. That matters a lot: a builder who only recognized revenue when a five-year skyscraper was finished would report years of activity with no revenue at all — a poor picture of economic performance.

The Two Accounts That Track the Contract

Construction in Progress (CIP) is an asset account — think of it as the contractor's work-in-process inventory. Every cost incurred on the job goes in here, and (as you'll see below) any gross profit recognized gets added here too.
Billings on Construction Contract is a contra account to CIP. Every time the contractor bills the customer, this account grows.

Why do we need a contra account at all? In a normal sale, the seller gives up a physical asset (inventory) and picks up a financial asset (a receivable) at the same moment — they're never on the balance sheet together. But here, the contractor is building the physical asset (CIP) in the very same periods it's billing and collecting cash. Without a contra account, the company would be double-counting: the physical asset (CIP) and the financial claim on the same value (billings/receivables) would both sit on the balance sheet at once. The Billings account nets that out.

Net position = CIP − Billings on Construction Contract

CIP > Billings → reported as a contract asset ("CIP in excess of Billings").

Billings > CIP → reported as a contract liability ("Billings in excess of CIP") — conceptually just like deferred revenue.

These three entries never change

Regardless of which method is used to recognize revenue, these entries record the actual construction activity every period:

Two Ways to Recognize Revenue

The total revenue, cost, and gross profit recognized over the life of a contract is identical either way. Only the timing differs.

Upon completion

If a contract doesn't qualify for over-time recognition, nothing is recognized until the project is finished. In the final period, the seller records the entire contract's revenue, total cost, and gross profit at once — and that same gross profit is added to CIP just before the closing entry wipes both CIP and Billings off the books.

Over time — percentage of completion

Progress toward completion is most often measured with the cost-to-cost ratio: costs incurred to date, divided by total estimated cost (actual-to-date + estimated-remaining).

% complete = Cumulative actual costs to date ÷ Total estimated cost (actual + remaining)
Revenue this period = (Contract price × % complete to date) − Revenue recognized in prior periods

Whenever gross profit is recognized under this method, it's debited to CIP — not because CIP is "profit," but because CIP is meant to track the total value (cost + profit) of the asset the contractor has built for the customer so far.

When the News Turns Bad: Losses on Long-Term Contracts

Cost estimates get revised over a multi-year job, and sometimes those revisions turn a profit into a loss. How you handle it depends on whether the whole contract is still expected to be profitable.

1. Profitable throughout → recognize gross profit normally each period, as shown above.

2. A single bad period, but the contract overall is still profitable → treat it as a change in accounting estimate (no restating prior years). Compute revenue and gross profit to date using the new estimate, then back out what was already recognized — the plug for the current period can be a loss even though the contract as a whole still makes money.

3. The entire contract is now expected to lose money → the full estimated loss must be recognized immediately, in the period the loss becomes evident — regardless of whether revenue is recognized over time or at completion. You don't wait for the loss to "happen"; recognizing it right away keeps CIP from being overstated above what the company will actually collect.

The tricky part of Scenario 3: once an overall loss is projected, Cost of Construction is no longer equal to the cash costs incurred that period. Revenue is still recognized normally using the % complete. Cost of Construction is instead a plug, calculated as:
Cost of Construction (loss period) = Revenue recognized this period + Loss recognized this period

That plug is what forces the year's entry to produce exactly the loss needed to bring the cumulative gross profit (loss) recognized to date down to the full estimated contract loss.
