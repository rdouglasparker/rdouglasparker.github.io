The Long-Lived Assets in This Chapter

Some assets are not bought to be sold or used up within a year. A company holds them for a long time because they help it earn revenue year after year. This chapter follows those assets from the day they are acquired. Chapter 11 picks up the story afterward: spreading their cost over time, handling repairs and upgrades, testing for impairment, and disposing of them.

GroupWhat belongs in itWhat sets it apart
Property, plant, and equipmentLand, land improvements, buildings, machinery, vehicles, computers, furniture, and fixturesPhysical assets whose cost is spread over their useful lives through depreciation
Natural resourcesTimber tracts, mineral deposits, and oil and gas reservesPhysically used up as they are harvested or extracted, so cost is spread through depletion
Intangible assetsPatents, copyrights, trademarks, franchises, and goodwillNo physical form, and future benefits are less certain, so cost is spread through amortization (unless the life is indefinite)

Right-of-use assets created by operating leases also produce long-term benefits, but they are covered separately with leases in Chapter 15.

How these assets appear in the financial statements

Property, plant, and equipment is reported at historical cost less accumulated depreciation. The result is called the carrying value (or book value). Companies usually disclose the detail by category in a note. Intangibles are shown separately from PP&E, and goodwill typically gets a line of its own.

Intangibles come in many flavors, and companies group them in different ways: technology-based (patents, software), customer-related (customer lists and contracts), marketing-related (trademarks, domain names), contract-based (licenses, franchises), and artistic (copyrights on books, music, film).

What Goes Into an Asset's Cost

The starting point is the same idea used for inventory: an asset is recorded at everything it took to buy it and get it ready to work.

Initial cost = Purchase price + All other costs to get the asset to its location and ready for use

The real question for each dollar spent is whether it buys something that will help future periods or only the current one. Costs that help only the current period are expensed now. Costs that help future periods are capitalized: recorded as an asset first, then expensed gradually as the asset is used.

Capitalize it if the cost was needed to acquire the asset or to put it in place and working.

Expense it if it benefits only the current period, would have been incurred anyway, or cannot be tied reliably to the specific asset (employee training is the classic example).

Cost of Equipment

Equipment covers machinery, computers, vehicles, furniture, and fixtures. Its cost includes the purchase price (net of discounts), sales tax, freight paid by the buyer, insurance while in transit, installation and testing, legal fees to establish title, and anything else required to make it ready for use. Any of these that can be identified and measured belongs in the asset rather than in expense.

Example — Hiwassee Precision Machining buys a press

Hiwassee bought a stamping press for its plant. The invoice price was $84,500, with 7% sales tax. Freight was $1,650 and transit insurance was $420. A concrete foundation built just for the press cost $2,300, and test runs (including scrapped material) cost $900. Hiwassee also paid $1,100 to train operators.

Check yourself

Ridgecrest Printing bought a bindery machine. The invoice price was $36,000, freight was $1,750, installation and wiring cost $2,900, testing and calibration cost $1,100, and operator training cost $800. At what amount is the machine recorded?

$36,000 + $1,750 + $2,900 + $1,100 = $41,750. The $800 of training is expensed.

Land, Land Improvements, and Buildings

Land

Land's cost covers the price plus the closing costs (attorney, title search and insurance, recording), broker commissions, any past-due taxes or liens the buyer takes on, and the work needed to prepare the site: clearing, grading, draining, and tearing down old buildings. If the buyer later sells salvage from a demolished building, the proceeds reduce the cost of the land.

Example — Hemlock Valley Packaging buys a site

Hemlock Valley paid $640,000 for eight acres with an aging warehouse, which it plans to demolish. At closing it also paid title insurance of $4,200, a broker commission of $19,200, $5,800 of delinquent property taxes owed by the seller, and $3,100 of property tax for the part of the current year after closing. Soon after, it paid $14,500 to demolish the warehouse and $6,300 to grade the lot, and it sold scrap steel from the demolition for $2,000.

Land improvements

Parking lots, driveways, private roads, fences, and sprinkler systems are kept separate from land. Land lasts indefinitely, but these items wear out, so their cost is depreciated over the periods they serve.

Buildings

A purchased building is recorded at its price plus commissions and legal fees. If it has to be refurbished, remodeled, or reconditioned before the company can use it, those costs are added too.
