Why the statement exists

Net income can be healthy while cash is thin, because accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expense when it is incurred, not when cash moves. A company that grows quickly may report strong earnings while receivables and inventory soak up cash. Creditors and investors want to know whether the firm can pay its bills, fund growth, and pay dividends, and that is a cash question.

The statement of cash flows reports cash inflows and outflows during the period, classified as operating, investing, or financing, and it explains the change in cash and cash equivalents.

Cash equivalents

Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to cash with little risk of loss, generally with an original maturity of three months or less when acquired. Treasury bills, money market funds, and commercial paper often qualify. Moving money between cash and a cash equivalent is not a cash flow. The company must disclose its policy for what counts as a cash equivalent.

FASB Codification

The statement of cash flows is in Topic 230, Statement of Cash Flows. Classification of cash receipts and payments is in ASC 230-10-45, noncash activities are in ASC 230-10-50, and the direct method is encouraged in ASC 230-10-45-25.

Classifying cash flows

Every cash flow lands in one of three sections. Operating is the residual category: if it is not investing or financing, it is operating.

Common classification traps

  • Interest paid is operating, even though it relates to borrowing. Repayment of principal is financing.
  • Dividends paid are financing, but dividends received are operating. Use dividends paid (not declared) on the statement.
  • A stock dividend or split moves no cash and is not reported.
  • The whole cash price of an asset sale is investing. The gain or loss is only an income statement item that the indirect method removes from operating.

The direct method

The direct method reports the major classes of gross cash receipts and payments. Each line is the income statement amount converted from accrual to cash using the related balance sheet changes. The continuing example below is used again in Detailed Notes B.

The continuing example

Cullowhee Creek Trading Company sells outdoor and camping gear to shops in the Blue Ridge. All amounts are in thousands. Sales are $1,000 and cost of goods sold is $600. Salaries expense is $120, insurance expense is $20, depreciation expense is $40, interest expense is $10 (no interest payable at either date), and income tax expense is $75. The company sold land with a book value of $35 for $50, a gain of $15. During the year, accounts receivable rose $30, inventory rose $20, accounts payable rose $15, salaries payable fell $10, prepaid insurance rose $5, and income taxes payable fell $5. It bought equipment for $90 cash, borrowed $80 on long-term notes, sold stock for $20, and paid dividends of $50. Cash was $70 at the start of the year.

Why the signs work

A receivable that grew means some of this year's sales were not collected, so cash is less than sales. An inventory increase means you bought more than you sold, so cash paid is more than cost of goods sold. A payable that grew means you did not pay for everything you bought, so cash paid is less. Reason about the story and the signs follow.

Land with a book value of $35 sold for $50. The $50 is the investing inflow, not the $15 gain.

Noncash investing and financing activities

Suppose the company also acquired a $25 delivery truck by issuing a note payable to the dealer. No cash moved, so the truck and the note do not appear in the body of the statement. Instead they are reported in a separate disclosure schedule or note. Other examples: converting bonds to stock, and acquiring land by issuing stock.

Practice: classification and the direct method

Try each problem, then open the answer.

Problem 1

Classify each as operating (O), investing (I), financing (F), or not reported (N): (a) interest paid on bonds; (b) cash purchase of a patent; (c) a 10% stock dividend; (d) buying treasury stock; (e) cash dividends received on an investment; (f) buying a 60-day Treasury bill as a cash equivalent.

Show the answer

(a) O. (b) I. (c) N, no cash moves. (d) F. (e) O. (f) N, a purchase of a cash equivalent is a swap within cash.

Problem 2

Tuckasegee Ridge Outfitters had sales of $840 and cost of goods sold of $510 (in thousands). Accounts receivable decreased $22, inventory decreased $14, and accounts payable increased $9. Find cash from customers and cash paid to suppliers.

Show the answer

Cash from customers = 840 + 22 = $862. Purchases = 510 − 14 = 496. Cash to suppliers = 496 − 9 = $487.

Problem 3

Balsam Mountain Supply reports salaries expense of $200, insurance expense of $36, and income tax expense of $54 (in thousands). Salaries payable rose $8, prepaid insurance fell $6, and income taxes payable rose $11. Find the cash paid for each.

Show the answer

Employees: 200 − 8 = $192. Insurance: 36 − 6 = $30. Taxes: 54 − 11 = $43.

Problem 4

Mountain Heritage Supply sold equipment that cost $80 with accumulated depreciation of $55 for $20 cash. What is the gain or loss, and what is reported on the statement of cash flows under the direct method?

Show the answer

Book value = 80 − 55 = 25, so a $5 loss. Under the direct method, the investing section shows an inflow of $20 and the loss does not appear anywhere on the statement.
