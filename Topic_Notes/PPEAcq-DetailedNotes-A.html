A puzzle to start

Cullowhee Machine Works buys a computer-controlled milling machine with an invoice price of $84,000. Before the first part is cut, the company also pays sales tax, freight, shipping insurance, a concrete platform, installers, and trial runs, and it sends two operators to a training class. Which of these belong in the machine’s cost, and which go straight to the income statement?

Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E) are the long-lived, tangible assets a company uses to produce goods and services rather than to resell: land, buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture, and natural resources. The first accounting question is always the same one: how much should go on the books on the day the asset is acquired? Everything that follows (depreciation, repairs, impairment, disposal) is built on that starting number.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept small and round so you can focus on the reasoning.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

The general rule for measuring property, plant, and equipment at acquisition is in Topic 360, Property, Plant, and Equipment, subtopic 360-10, section 30, Initial Measurement. Asset retirement obligations are in Topic 410, and oil and gas accounting is in Topic 932.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 360-10-30-1: the historical cost of an asset includes the costs necessarily incurred to bring it to the condition and location necessary for its intended use.
  • ASC 410-20-25 and 410-20-30: a legal obligation to retire a tangible long-lived asset is recognized as a liability at fair value, and the same amount is added to the cost of the related asset.
  • ASC 932-360: oil and gas producers may account for exploration costs under either the successful efforts method or the full cost method.

The capitalization principle

The rule for almost every long-lived asset is one sentence: record the asset at its purchase price plus every expenditure necessary to get it ready for its intended use, in the place where it will be used. You already applied this idea to inventory, where freight-in is part of cost.

The goal is to separate costs that produce benefits for many periods from costs that produce benefits only now. The first group is capitalized: recorded as an asset and then expensed gradually as depreciation, depletion, or amortization. The second group is expensed immediately.

The test for every cost

Is this cost necessary to acquire the asset and make it ready for use? If yes, capitalize it. If it relates to using the asset after it is ready (operating, insuring, training, repairing), expense it.

Equipment

The cost of equipment includes the invoice price less any discounts, sales tax, transportation paid by the buyer, insurance while the equipment is in transit, installation, testing, and any special foundation or platform. Trial runs belong in cost because they prove the machine works before it starts producing. If a cost can be identified and measured, include it.

Some costs are hard to measure or relate more to people than to the asset. The cost of training employees is the usual example: it could be called necessary, but it is difficult to tie to one machine, so it is expensed.

Land, land improvements, and buildings

Land is recorded at its purchase price plus closing costs (title search and insurance, attorney fees, recording fees, broker commissions) and any obligations the buyer takes over, such as back taxes, liens, and mortgages. It also includes the cost of preparing the site: clearing, filling, draining, and razing an old building that must go. Money received from selling salvage from the old building reduces the cost of the land.

Land is special because it normally has an indefinite life, so it is never depreciated. That is why land improvements (parking lots, driveways, fences, landscaping, sprinklers) are recorded in a separate account: they wear out, so they are capitalized separately and depreciated over their useful lives.

The cost of a building includes the purchase price, commissions, and legal fees. If the building must be refurbished or remodeled to suit the new owner, those reconditioning costs are part of its cost too. A building the company constructs itself raises special issues, covered in Part C.

Land held for investment is not PP&E

Land bought for speculation or for a future use that has not been decided belongs in investments or other assets, not in property, plant, and equipment. The cost rules are the same; the classification is different.

Lump-sum purchases

Companies often buy several different assets for one price. If the assets are identical, the answer is easy: ten identical delivery trucks bought for $150,000 cost $15,000 each. When the assets are different, the single price must be allocated in proportion to each asset’s relative fair value. This matters because the assets have different lives: land is never depreciated, a building lasts decades, and equipment may last only a few years.

  1. Estimate fair value Get an appraisal or other evidence of what each asset would cost if bought separately.
  2. Total the fair values Add them to find the combined fair value.
  3. Compute each asset’s share Divide each fair value by the total.
  4. Multiply by the price paid Allocated cost = share × lump-sum price. The allocated costs must add back to the price.

Why the split can be an ethics issue

Land is not depreciated, but buildings and equipment are. Assigning too much of a lump-sum price to land lowers depreciation expense and raises reported income, which can raise bonuses and the stock price, but it also raises taxable income. The allocation must follow fair values, not the outcome someone wants. The conflict is between managers and the company’s owners, lenders, and the tax authorities who rely on honest numbers.

Natural resources and asset retirement obligations

Natural resources (timber tracts, mineral deposits, oil and gas reserves) are PP&E that are physically used up, which is why they are sometimes called wasting assets. If a company buys a developed resource, cost is the purchase price plus costs to make it ready. More often it develops the resource itself, and cost can include four elements:

  • Acquisition costs: amounts paid for the right to explore for or to extract the resource.
  • Exploration costs: drilling, excavating, and other searching.
  • Development costs: tunnels, wells, and shafts built after discovery but before production begins.
  • Restoration costs: returning the land to its original condition after extraction ends.

The heavy equipment used to dig or drill is not part of the resource’s cost. It is depreciable equipment (although if it cannot be moved and has no other use, its life is limited by the life of the resource).

Asset retirement obligations

Restoration is an example of an asset retirement obligation (ARO): a legal obligation to retire a tangible long-lived asset that arises from acquiring, building, developing, or operating it. AROs are not limited to natural resources. A power plant that must be decommissioned or a landfill that must be capped creates one too. When the obligation arises, the company:

  1. Measures the liability at fair value Usually the present value of the expected cash outflows.
  2. Credits a liability Asset retirement obligation.
  3. Debits the related asset The same amount increases the asset’s cost, so the cost of using the asset includes the cost of retiring it.

Because the cash outflow is uncertain, we use the expected cash flow approach: weight each possible outflow by its probability, then discount at the credit-adjusted risk-free rate. Risk is handled in the cash flows (through probabilities); the discount rate reflects only the company’s credit standing.

If exploration or development shows that a project is not economically feasible, the costs are expensed rather than capitalized (with an exception for oil and gas, below).

Oil and gas: successful efforts versus full cost

Drilling for oil and gas often produces dry holes. U.S. GAAP allows two methods for the cost of exploration:

  • Successful efforts: costs of wells that find oil or gas are capitalized; costs of dry holes are expensed in the year incurred.
  • Full cost: all exploration costs in a large geographic area are capitalized and expensed as the oil and gas from the successful wells is produced.

Cashiers Ridge Energy: eight wells, two successes

Cashiers Ridge Energy drills 8 wells at $1,500,000 each. Only 2 find gas.

Cashiers Ridge Energy: the two methods side by side
ItemSuccessful effortsFull cost
Capitalized as an asset$3,000,000$12,000,000
Expensed this year$9,000,000$0
Total drilling cost$12,000,000$12,000,000

Same wells, same cash. Successful efforts reports a large expense now; full cost reports a larger asset and defers the expense. Companies must disclose which method they use.

This is a famous example of accounting standard-setting as a political process. When the FASB tried to require successful efforts for everyone, oil and gas companies, Congress, and regulators objected that lower reported income would hurt capital raising and exploration. The standard was withdrawn, and both methods remain acceptable.

Common mistakes

  • Expensing freight, installation, or trial runs. They are necessary to get the asset ready, so they are capitalized.
  • Capitalizing training, first-year insurance, or property tax on equipment. These are costs of using or owning the asset, not of acquiring it.
  • Putting the parking lot into the land account. Land is not depreciated; improvements are.
  • Forgetting salvage proceeds. Money from selling the old building’s materials reduces the cost of the land.
  • Capitalizing current-year property taxes on newly bought land. Only delinquent (back) taxes assumed from the seller are part of cost.
  • Allocating a lump sum by percentages of cost instead of fair value. Use relative fair values.
  • Discounting restoration costs at the wrong amount. Use the probability-weighted cash flow and the credit-adjusted risk-free rate, and credit a liability, not cash.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: Equipment

Tuckasegee Packaging buys a labeling machine for $46,000, pays 7% sales tax, $1,100 freight, $250 transit insurance, and $1,650 to install it. It also pays $800 to train operators and $900 for the first year of insurance. What is the machine’s capitalized cost?

Show the answer

Capitalize: $46,000 + $3,220 tax + $1,100 + $250 + $1,650 = $52,220. Training ($800) and first-year insurance ($900) are expensed.

Problem 2: Land

Balsam Ridge Storage pays $400,000 for land with an old barn, plus $2,500 title insurance, $18,000 commission, and $5,000 of back taxes. It pays $9,000 to demolish the barn and sells scrap for $1,500, then pays $6,000 to grade the lot. What is the cost of the land?

Show the answer

$400,000 + $2,500 + $18,000 + $5,000 + $9,000 − $1,500 + $6,000 = $439,000.

Problem 3: Lump-sum purchase

Sylva Welding buys a shop for $900,000. Fair values: land $300,000, building $450,000, equipment $150,000. What cost is assigned to each?

Show the answer

Total fair value is $900,000 (equal to the price), so each asset is recorded at its fair value: land $300,000, building $450,000, equipment $150,000. If the price had been $720,000, each would be 80% of fair value: $240,000, $360,000, and $120,000.

Problem 4: Asset retirement obligation

A company must restore a site in 3 years. Expected restoration outlays are $200,000 (40%) and $300,000 (60%). The credit-adjusted risk-free rate is 8%. What liability is recorded, and what is year 1 accretion?

Show the answer

Expected outflow = $80,000 + $180,000 = $260,000. Present value = $260,000 ÷ 1.083 = $206,396. Year 1 accretion = 8% × $206,396 = $16,512. The deposit’s cost includes the $206,396.

Key terms

Capitalize
To record a cost as an asset and expense it in later periods, rather than expensing it now.
Historical cost
The amount paid for an asset, including all costs necessary to make it ready for use.
Land improvement
A depreciable enhancement to land, such as a parking lot, fence, or sprinkler system.
Lump-sum purchase
A purchase of several different assets for a single price, allocated by relative fair values.
Natural resource
An asset that is physically consumed in operations, such as timber, minerals, or oil and gas.
Asset retirement obligation (ARO)
A legal obligation to retire a tangible long-lived asset, recorded as a liability at fair value with an equal increase in the asset.
Expected cash flow approach
Weighting possible cash outflows by their probabilities and discounting at the credit-adjusted risk-free rate.
Accretion expense
The yearly growth of a discounted liability toward its expected settlement amount.
Successful efforts method
Capitalizes only the exploration costs of successful wells; dry-hole costs are expensed.
Full cost method
Capitalizes all exploration costs in a geographic area and expenses them as production occurs.
