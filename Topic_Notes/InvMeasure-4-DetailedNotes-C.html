A puzzle to start

A store’s ending inventory at retail rose from $90,000 to $113,400 during a year when its selling prices rose 8%. Did it really have more goods on the shelves? And if a count error overstated last year’s inventory by $20,000, which years’ profits are wrong, and does anything need to be fixed if the error has already reversed itself?

These notes finish the inventory story with three topics. Dollar-value LIFO retail separates real growth in inventory from price increases. Changes in inventory method explain how to switch methods without making the years incomparable. Inventory errors explain how a mistake flows through two years of income and how to correct it.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Years are labeled Year 1, Year 2, and so on. Unless stated otherwise, income taxes are ignored; in practice, the adjustments to retained earnings are made net of tax.

Where these rules live

Dollar-value LIFO retail is an application of the inventory rules in Topic 330. Changes in method and corrections of errors are governed by Topic 250, Accounting Changes and Error Corrections.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 250-10-45-5: a change in accounting principle is applied retrospectively to all prior periods presented, unless doing so is impracticable.
  • ASC 250-10-45-2: a company may change principle only if the new principle is preferable.
  • ASC 250-10-45-23: an error in prior-period statements is corrected by restating those statements (a prior period adjustment).
  • ASC 250-10-50: disclosures for changes in principle and error corrections.

Dollar-value LIFO retail

The LIFO retail method in Detailed Notes B assumed prices were stable. If retail prices rise, ending inventory at retail can grow even when the quantity of goods doesn’t. Dollar-value LIFO retail combines LIFO retail with the dollar-value LIFO idea of using a price index to remove the effect of price changes.

  1. Compute ending inventory at retail, at year-end prices.
  2. Deflate it to base-year prices: divide by the current year’s retail price index.
  3. Compare with the prior year at base-year prices to find any new layer (or any decrease).
  4. Convert each layer to cost: base-year retail × that layer’s price index × that layer’s cost-to-retail percentage.

Each layer carries two numbers

Every layer keeps the price index and the cost-to-retail percentage of the year it was added. They never change, even when later years have different indexes and percentages.

Changes in inventory method

Consistency makes financial statements comparable from year to year, but a company may change inventory methods if the new method is preferable, for example because it better matches how inventory actually flows. The goal is to make the statements look as if the new method had always been used.

Most changes: retrospective

For most changes, including a change from LIFO to another method, the company applies the retrospective approach:

  • Revise the comparative financial statements of prior years as if the new method had been used.
  • Adjust the beginning balance of retained earnings in the earliest year presented for the cumulative effect on earlier years.
  • Adjust the account balances (here, inventory) to what they would have been under the new method.
  • Disclose the nature of the change, why the new method is preferable, and its effects on the financial statements.

Change to LIFO: usually prospective

A company switching to LIFO usually can’t reconstruct the LIFO layers of earlier years, so retrospective application is impracticable. Instead, the inventory’s carrying value at the start of the year of the change becomes the base layer, and LIFO is used from then on. The company discloses why retrospective application wasn’t possible.

Not a change in method

A change in estimate (such as a revised gross profit ratio) is applied prospectively, and the correction of an error is handled as a prior period adjustment. Neither is a change in accounting principle.

Inventory errors

Inventory errors are often counting or pricing mistakes in a periodic system. Because ending inventory of one year is beginning inventory of the next, an error affects two years’ income statements, in opposite directions.

Effects of an overstatement of ending inventory in Year 1 (periodic system, taxes ignored)
ItemYear 1Year 2
Ending inventoryOverstatedCorrect (if counted correctly)
Beginning inventoryCorrectOverstated
Cost of goods soldUnderstatedOverstated
Net incomeOverstatedUnderstated
Retained earnings, end of yearOverstatedCorrect

An error like this is self-correcting (counterbalancing): by the end of Year 2, total income for the two years and retained earnings are correct. An understatement has the opposite effects.

A quick check

Cost of goods sold = beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory. Ending inventory is subtracted, so overstating it understates cost of goods sold and overstates income. Beginning inventory is added, so it works the other way.

Whiteside Supply: an overstated count

In Year 1, Whiteside Supply counted some goods twice and overstated ending inventory by $20,000. It uses a periodic system and presents comparative statements for two years.

Discovered during Year 2, before the books are closed

The error hasn’t counterbalanced yet: retained earnings is overstated by the $20,000 overstatement of Year 1 income, and the inventory account (Year 2’s beginning inventory) is overstated. Correct the books with a prior period adjustment and restate the Year 1 statements shown for comparison.

Journal entry: Year 2: correct the Year 1 error
AccountDebitCredit
Retained earnings20,000
Inventory20,000

Year 2’s cost of goods sold is then computed from the correct beginning inventory.

Discovered in Year 3, after Year 2 is closed

The error has self-corrected: retained earnings and inventory are correct, so no entry is needed. But the Year 1 and Year 2 income statements are still wrong, so any of them presented for comparison are restated, and the correction is disclosed.

  • If the error is discovered after it has counterbalanced and those years are no longer presented, nothing needs to be done.
  • A material error correction requires disclosure of the nature of the error and its effect on each year presented.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing retail amounts at different price levels. In dollar-value LIFO retail, deflate ending inventory to base-year prices before comparing years.
  • Using the current index for an old layer. Each layer uses its own year’s index and cost-to-retail percentage.
  • Rebuilding a layer that was used up. A new increase creates a new layer at current prices.
  • Recording a change in method only in the current year. Most changes are retrospective: revise prior years and adjust beginning retained earnings.
  • Recording an entry for an error that has already counterbalanced. No entry is needed; only the statements presented are restated.
  • Getting the direction of an error wrong. An overstated ending inventory overstates that year’s income and understates the next year’s.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: dollar-value LIFO retail

A base layer at the start of Year 1 retails for $50,000 at a 70% cost-to-retail percentage. At the end of Year 1, ending inventory at year-end prices is $66,000, the index is 1.10, and the Year 1 percentage is 72%. Compute ending inventory at cost.

Show the answer

At base prices: $66,000 ÷ 1.10 = $60,000, a $10,000 layer. Base layer cost = $50,000 × 70% = $35,000. Year 1 layer = $10,000 × 1.10 × 72% = $7,920. Ending inventory = $42,920.

Problem 2: a change in method

At the start of Year 4 a company changes from average cost to FIFO. Inventory at the start of Year 4 would have been $25,000 higher under FIFO. What entry is recorded (ignore taxes), and what else is required?

Show the answer

Debit inventory $25,000; credit retained earnings $25,000. Prior years presented for comparison are revised as if FIFO had been used, beginning retained earnings of the earliest year presented is adjusted, and the change and its justification are disclosed.

Problem 3: the direction of an error

Year 5 ending inventory was understated by $8,000. What were the effects on Year 5 and Year 6 net income, and on retained earnings at the end of Year 6?

Show the answer

Year 5 cost of goods sold was overstated, so Year 5 net income was understated by $8,000. Year 6 beginning inventory was understated, so Year 6 net income was overstated by $8,000. Retained earnings at the end of Year 6 is correct.

Key terms

Dollar-value LIFO retail
LIFO retail with a price index used to deflate ending inventory to base-year prices before identifying layers.
Retail price index
A measure of the change in retail prices since the base year.
Layer
An increase in inventory at base-year prices in a particular year, costed with that year’s index and cost-to-retail percentage.
Change in accounting principle
A switch from one acceptable method to another, such as from FIFO to average cost.
Retrospective approach
Revising prior-period statements as if the new method had always been used.
Prospective approach
Applying a new method from now on without revising prior periods; used for most changes to LIFO.
Counterbalancing error
An error that reverses itself in the next period, so retained earnings is correct after two years.
Prior period adjustment
A correction of an error in prior-period statements, recorded as an adjustment to beginning retained earnings.
