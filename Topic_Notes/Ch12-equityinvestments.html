Investments in Equity Securities

Reading Notes

How an Equity Investment Is Accounted For Depends on Influence

The events in the life of an equity investment parallel debt: purchase, dividends (for some securities), holding while fair value changes, and sale. But shares also carry votes, so the accounting also considers how much the investor can influence the company it invests in (the investee).

Three approaches, depending on the investor's influence
Investor's positionTypical ownershipMethod
No significant influence over the investee's operating and financial policiesLess than 20% of voting sharesFair value through net income: investment at fair value; unrealized gains and losses in net income (unless fair value isn't readily determinable)

The percentages are presumptions, not bright lines. Ownership above 20% doesn't create significant influence if, say, another shareholder holds a majority, the investee is fighting the investor's influence in court, or the investor tried and failed to get a board seat. Ownership below 20% can still give significant influence if, for example, an investor officer sits on the investee's board.

No Significant Influence: Fair Value through Net Income

The investor is in the same position as any individual who buys a share of stock: it hopes for dividends and price appreciation but can't tell the company what to do. Equity investments are accounted for like trading securities:

  • Record the purchase at cost.
  • Record dividends received as revenue.
  • At each reporting date, adjust the investment to fair value (using a Fair value adjustment account) and report the unrealized gain or loss in net income.
  • On sale, first adjust to fair value at the sale date; then remove the accounts. Because every change has already hit net income, there's typically no additional gain or loss on the sale.

Equity securities can't be classified as available-for-sale; the OCI approach used for debt isn't permitted here.

What if fair value isn't readily determinable?

Fair value is "readily determinable" when prices are available on an exchange or over-the-counter market. If not (for example, shares of a private company), the investor may elect a measurement alternative: carry the investment at cost, less any impairment, plus or minus changes from observable prices of identical or similar investments of the same issuer. Each period the investor reassesses whether fair value has become readily determinable and tests for impairment using qualitative indicators such as deteriorating earnings, cash flows, or business prospects. An impairment loss is recognized in net income.

Presentation

  • Balance sheet: current or noncurrent depending on intent.
  • Cash flows: investments held for short-term profit are typically operating activities; other equity investments are investing activities.
  • Notes: disclose the part of the period's unrealized gains and losses that relates to securities still held, and how carrying amount was determined when fair value isn't readily determinable.

Significant Influence: The Equity Method

When an investor can significantly influence an investee, the two companies' fortunes are intertwined: as the investee earns income, the investor's share of the investee's net assets grows, and when the investee pays dividends, the investor's share shrinks. The equity method reflects exactly that in the investment account.

Investment balance = Cost + Share of investee's net income − Share of investee's dividends ± Other adjustments (differential)
Dividends are not revenue under the equity method. The investor already recognized its share of the investee's earnings when the investee earned them. Recognizing revenue again at the dividend would double count, so dividends simply reduce the investment (the investee has handed back some of the net assets). The equity method also ignores changes in fair value.

The investor's share of the investee's earnings is often labeled "equity in earnings of affiliate" rather than "investment revenue." If the investee has a net loss, the investor reduces the investment and recognizes a loss for its share.

The equity method is sometimes called a "one-line consolidation": it mimics the effect of consolidating the investee on income and shareholders' equity, but reports the investor's interest in one investment line rather than combining items line by line.

The Differential: When the Price Exceeds Book Value

Investors usually pay more than the book value of the net assets they acquire. Part of the extra is because identifiable assets (buildings, land, inventory) are worth more than their carrying amounts. The rest is goodwill. The investor needs to track these pieces because they affect its income.

Example

On January 2, 2026, Catamount Capital Holdings pays $1,000,000 for a 25% interest in Hunter Hollow Software. The price implies a fair value for the whole company of $1,000,000 ÷ 25% = $4,000,000. The buildings have a 10-year remaining life, straight-line, no salvage value.

Reporting

  • Balance sheet: a single investment account (not fair value).
  • Income statement: the investor's share of earnings, as adjusted.
  • Cash flows: purchase and sale are investing activities; dividends received are operating. Under the indirect method, net income includes $85,000 of investment revenue but only $40,000 of cash arrived, so the operating section subtracts the $45,000 difference ("undistributed earnings of investee").
Deferred taxes: investment revenue is recognized when the investee earns income, but it's taxed when received as dividends. The temporary difference creates a deferred tax liability.

Special Situations

Acquisition during the year

Only the investee's income after the acquisition belongs to the investor, so record the fraction of the year the investment was owned. For example, if the stake was bought on October 1, record 3/12 of the investee's annual net income and 3/12 of the annual adjustments. Dividends received after the acquisition date are recorded in full.

When the investee has a net loss

The investor reduces its investment by its share of the loss (adjusted for differential items). If its share of losses would drive the investment below zero, it stops applying the equity method (the investment stays at zero) until its share of later earnings makes up the losses it didn't recognize.

Impairment

If the fair value of an equity-method investment falls below its carrying amount and the decline is viewed as other than temporary, reduce the investment to fair value and recognize an impairment loss in net income. The investor then continues with the equity method.

The fair value option for equity-method investments

A company can irrevocably elect to carry an investment that would qualify for the equity method at fair value instead, with unrealized gains and losses in net income. It can elect it for some investments and not others. The company can get there in two ways, with the same result: (a) use the fair-value-through-net-income entries, or (b) record equity-method entries all period and then add a fair value adjustment at the end of the period.

Both ways give the same answer

An investor buys 30% of an investee for $800. Its share of net income is $60, its share of dividends is $20, its differential amortization is $15, and fair value rises to $1,000.

  • (a) Fair value approach: dividend income $20 + unrealized gain $200 = $220 increase in pre-tax income.
  • (b) Equity method then adjust: equity income $60 − $15 = $45; the pre-adjustment balance is $800 + $45 − $20 = $825; the adjustment to reach $1,000 is $175; total = $45 + $175 = $220.

Decision Makers' Perspective

The method used changes net income and the balance sheet, though not cash. Consider an investor that buys 20% of a start-up for $3,000,000 (including $300,000 for its share of an inventory fair value excess that sells during the year). The start-up reports an $800,000 loss and pays a small dividend ($10,000 to the investor), but its prospects look so good that the investor's stake is worth $3,400,000 at year-end.

  • Equity method: a $460,000 loss (20% × $800,000 loss, plus the $300,000 inventory adjustment), and the investment is carried at $2,530,000.
  • Fair value through net income: a $410,000 gain (the $10,000 dividend plus a $400,000 unrealized gain), and the investment is carried at $3,400,000.

Same investment, same cash, very different reported results. Users must understand which method applies. Managers also have incentives and discretion:

  • Investors sometimes structure stakes to stay below the 20% to 25% threshold and avoid recognizing their share of investee losses.
  • Companies can time the sale of equity-method investments to realize gains in weak years and losses in strong ones.
  • Under fair value accounting, estimates (especially Level 3) can be biased or inaccurate; that's why extensive disclosures about the inputs exist.

A strength of the equity method is that it prevents an investor with significant influence from boosting its income by getting the investee to pay dividends.

GAAP vs. IFRS

Equity Investments Without Significant Influence

IFRS classifies equity investments as either FVPL (fair value through profit or loss) or FVOCI. Equity held for trading must be FVPL; otherwise the company may irrevocably elect FVOCI. With FVOCI, dividends are still income, but when the investment is sold the accumulated gain or loss is not recycled through net income. It's transferred directly from AOCI to retained earnings.

Equity Method (“Associates”)

IFRS also requires the equity method for investees with significant influence, but it requires the investee's accounting policies to be adjusted to match the investor's (U.S. GAAP doesn't), and it doesn't allow the fair value option for most of these investments (U.S. GAAP allows it for all of them).
