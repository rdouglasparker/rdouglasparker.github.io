Self-Constructed Assets and Capitalized Interest

What costs go into an asset a company builds itself, and how to calculate interest capitalized during construction.

A puzzle to start

Blue Ridge Furniture needs a new warehouse. A contractor quotes a price, but the company’s own crews can build it for less and finish sooner. Along the way the company will spend money on materials, wages, a share of factory overhead, and it will borrow to pay for the work. Which of those costs belong in the building’s cost?

Many companies, from utilities to homebuilders, construct assets for their own use. The cost of a self-constructed asset follows the same principle as a purchased one: all costs necessary to get the asset ready for use. Two issues need extra attention: how much overhead to include, and whether to capitalize the interest incurred while construction is in progress.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

Initial measurement is in ASC 360-10-30. The capitalization of interest is in Topic 835, Interest, subtopic 20, Capitalization of Interest.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 360-10-30-1: cost includes all costs necessarily incurred to bring the asset to its condition and location for use.
  • ASC 835-20-25: interest cost is capitalized as part of the historical cost of a qualifying asset during the period it is being prepared for use.
  • ASC 835-20-30: the amount capitalized is the amount of interest that could have been avoided if the expenditures had not been made, limited to interest actually incurred.
  • ASC 835-20-50: disclose total interest cost incurred and the amount capitalized.

Costs of a self-constructed asset

Materials and labor traceable to the asset are always capitalized. Overhead is harder, because some overhead would exist whether or not the company built the asset. Two methods are used:

  • Incremental (or variable) overhead: capitalize only the overhead that increased because of the construction.
  • Full-cost allocation: allocate a share of all overhead, fixed and variable, on the same basis as for products.

The full-cost approach is the more widely used. Whichever is chosen should be applied consistently. If the asset would cost less to buy than to build, the excess is not an asset; the cost is limited to what the company would have paid.

When interest is capitalized

Interest is the cost of using borrowed money. When a company borrows to fund an asset that takes a long time to get ready, that interest is as necessary as materials. So the interest incurred during construction is capitalized. Once the asset is ready, interest becomes an ordinary expense.

Qualifying assets

Interest is capitalized only for qualifying assets:

  • Assets constructed for the company’s own use (a building, a plant, a ship).
  • Assets constructed as discrete projects for sale or lease (a ship or real-estate development).
  • Investments accounted for by the equity method while the investee is building a qualifying asset.

Assets that are not qualifying: those already in use or ready for use, assets not being used in operations and not being prepared for use, and inventories routinely produced in large quantities.

The capitalization period

Capitalization begins when three conditions are met: (1) expenditures for the asset have been made, (2) activities to get the asset ready are underway, and (3) interest cost is being incurred. It ends when the asset is substantially complete and ready for its intended use.

Calculating the interest to capitalize

Capitalized interest is the interest that could have been avoided if the construction expenditures had not been made. It is found in four steps:

  1. Compute weighted-average accumulated expenditures Weight each expenditure by the fraction of the year it was outstanding. An expenditure on April 1 counts for 9/12 of the year.
  2. Apply the interest rates Use the rate on a specific construction loan first, up to the amount of the loan. Apply the weighted-average rate on the company’s other debt to any remaining expenditures.
  3. Add the pieces The total is the avoidable interest.
  4. Compare with actual interest Capitalize the lower of avoidable interest and actual interest incurred during the period.

Why the limit exists

Capitalized interest cannot exceed the interest the company actually incurred. If the company had borrowed only $1 million, it could not capitalize interest on $4 million of construction, because there is no more interest to capitalize.

After capitalization

The capitalized interest becomes part of the building’s cost and is recovered through depreciation over its life, just like any other construction cost. Interest expense on the income statement is lower than the interest paid, and interest paid on the cash flow statement is unchanged.

Companies disclose the total interest cost incurred and the amount capitalized for the period. Analysts use this when calculating interest coverage, because capitalized interest is not in interest expense but is still an obligation.

A construction project often spans more than one year. In the second year, the accumulated expenditures start from the building’s balance including the interest capitalized in the first year, because that interest is now part of the asset on which interest can be incurred.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging expenditures by simple average. Weight each by the time it was outstanding.
  • Applying the specific loan rate to the whole amount. It applies only up to the loan; the rest uses the weighted-average rate on other debt.
  • Including the construction loan in the weighted-average rate for the other debt.
  • Forgetting the actual-interest limit. Always compare avoidable with actual interest.
  • Capitalizing interest on land that is ready for use, or after the asset is complete.
  • Capitalizing the full amount of interest paid, not the avoidable amount.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: Overhead

Tuckasegee Fabricators builds a conveyor: materials $120,000, labor $80,000, variable overhead $15,000, allocated fixed overhead $25,000. What is the cost under the incremental and full-cost methods?

Show the answer

Incremental: $120,000 + $80,000 + $15,000 = $215,000. Full cost: $215,000 + $25,000 = $240,000.

Problem 2: Weighted average

Expenditures on a project: January 1 $600,000; July 1 $900,000; October 1 $300,000. What are the weighted-average accumulated expenditures for the calendar year?

Show the answer

$600,000 × 12/12 + $900,000 × 6/12 + $300,000 × 3/12 = $600,000 + $450,000 + $75,000 = $1,125,000.

Problem 3: Avoidable interest

Using the data in the previous problem, the company has a $500,000 construction loan at 8% and other notes of $2,000,000 outstanding all year at an average 6%. What is the avoidable interest?

Show the answer

$500,000 × 8% = $40,000. Remainder: $1,125,000 − $500,000 = $625,000 × 6% = $37,500. Avoidable interest = $77,500. Actual interest = $40,000 + $120,000 = $160,000, so capitalize $77,500.

Problem 4: Actual-interest limit

A company has weighted-average accumulated expenditures of $3,000,000 and only one note: $1,000,000 at 6%. How much can it capitalize?

Show the answer

Avoidable interest at 6% on $3,000,000 would be $180,000, but actual interest is only $60,000. Capitalize $60,000.

Key terms

Self-constructed asset
An asset a company builds for its own use.
Incremental overhead
Overhead that increases because of the construction project.
Full-cost overhead
A share of all overhead, fixed and variable, allocated to the asset.
Qualifying asset
An asset for which interest can be capitalized, such as one under construction for own use.
Capitalization period
From the start of expenditures, activity, and interest until the asset is ready for use.
Weighted-average accumulated expenditures
Expenditures weighted by the fraction of the year each was outstanding.
Avoidable interest
Interest that could have been avoided had the expenditures not been made.
Specific construction loan
Borrowing identified with the project; its rate is applied first.
