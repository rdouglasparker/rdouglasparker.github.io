Introduction to Revenue Recognition

Reading Notes

The Core Principle and the Five Steps

Core Revenue Recognition Principle: companies recognize revenue when goods or services are transferred to customers, for the amount the company expects to be entitled to receive in exchange for those goods or services.

Every revenue recognition question — no matter how complicated the contract — gets worked through the same five steps:

StepWhat it asks
1Identify the contract with a customer.
2Identify the performance obligation(s) in the contract.
3Determine the transaction price.
4Allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation.
5Recognize revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied.

A "contract" doesn't need to be written or signed — buying a coffee, a textbook, or a doctor's visit are all contracts for revenue recognition purposes, because an arrangement exists that specifies the legal rights and obligations of a seller and a customer.

Performance obligations are promises by the seller to transfer goods or services to a customer. Revenue is recognized when the seller satisfies a performance obligation — that is, when the customer obtains control of the good or service (direct influence over its use, and its benefits).

With only a single performance obligation, Steps 3 and 4 collapse: the whole transaction price is "allocated" to that one obligation, so the real question becomes just when control transfers (Step 5). Multiple performance obligations make Steps 2 and 4 the interesting ones — deciding what counts as separate promises, and splitting the price between them.

Recognizing Revenue at a Single Point in Time

When a contract has one performance obligation satisfied all at once, the question is simply: when does control pass from seller to customer? Usually that's obvious (delivery), but when it isn't, these indicators help:

The customer is more likely to control a good or service if the customer has:

• An obligation to pay the seller

• Legal title to the asset

• Physical possession of the asset

• Assumed the risks and rewards of ownership

• Accepted the asset

Why timing matters

An order placed, but not yet delivered, doesn't satisfy the performance obligation — no revenue yet, even though a contract exists. Once the seller delivers the good and the customer accepts it (gaining possession, title, and the obligation to pay), the performance obligation is satisfied and revenue is recognized — regardless of when cash actually changes hands. A seller that ships goods on credit recognizes revenue at delivery, not weeks later when payment arrives.

Recognizing Revenue over a Period of Time

Some performance obligations aren't satisfied all at once — think of a landlord renting an apartment, a bank lending money, or a gym selling a membership. Revenue is recognized over time if any one of these three criteria is met:

1. The customer consumes the benefit of the seller's work as it's performed (e.g., a cleaning service).

2. The customer controls the asset as it's being created (e.g., a contractor building an addition on a customer's existing building).

3. The seller is creating an asset with no alternative use to the seller, and has the legal right to be paid for progress to date (e.g., a custom-built fighter jet for the Air Force).

If none of the three criteria is met, revenue waits until the point in time when the performance obligation is completely satisfied — usually the end of the contract.

Measuring progress toward completion

When revenue is recognized over time, the seller needs some way to measure "how much" of the obligation has been satisfied so far:

Output measures: the proportion of goods or services actually transferred to date (units delivered, milestones reached, or — as with a subscription — simple passage of time).

Input measures: the proportion of effort expended so far relative to total effort expected (costs incurred to date compared to total estimated costs — the "cost-to-cost ratio" used for long-term construction contracts in Part C).

Cash is frequently collected before the performance obligation is satisfied in these arrangements (a year's gym membership paid up front, for example). That up-front cash is recorded as a liability — deferred revenue — and worked down to zero as revenue is recognized over the life of the arrangement.

Contracts with Multiple Performance Obligations

The same contract can bundle several promises together — a phone, a warranty, and network service, for example — each of which might need to be recognized at a different time. The first question is always: are these promises actually separate performance obligations, or one combined obligation?

A good or service is distinct — and therefore its own performance obligation — only if it is both:

(a) Capable of being distinct — the customer could use it on its own or combine it with things obtained elsewhere, and
(b) Separately identifiable — the seller is promising to deliver the items individually, rather than combining them into one output.

That second condition is why a homebuilder's lumber, paint, and labor are not separate performance obligations — even though each is capable of being distinct, the builder's real promise is to deliver one finished house. This is the same reasoning that makes most long-term construction contracts (Part C) a single performance obligation.

Allocating the transaction price

Once the performance obligations are identified, the transaction price is split between them based on their relative stand-alone selling prices — what each item sells for on its own.

Allocated price (obligation X) = Transaction price × [Stand-alone price of X ÷ Sum of all stand-alone prices]

Bundled discounts: if a package is sold for less than the sum of its parts' stand-alone prices, and there's no evidence the discount belongs to just one item, the discount is spread proportionally across all performance obligations in the allocation. The practical effect: bundling means less revenue is recognized for each piece than if it had been sold on its own.

After the price is allocated, each performance obligation is recognized on its own timeline — some at a point in time, some over time — exactly as described in the two sections above.
