A question to start

A hospital system reports a $200 million "provision for bad debts." Does that mean patients didn't pay $200 million this year? Not exactly. It means the hospital expects that much of what it is owed will never be collected, and it recorded the expense now, before anyone has actually defaulted.

Credit losses are a normal cost of selling on credit. These notes explain why GAAP requires companies to estimate them in advance, how the estimate is made under the CECL model, and what happens when a specific customer finally does not pay.

The direct write-off method (not GAAP)

The simplest approach waits until a specific account is clearly uncollectible and writes it off then.

When a $15,000 customer account proves uncollectible

Journal entry: When a $15,000 customer account proves uncollectible
AccountDebitCredit
Bad debt expense15,000
Accounts receivable15,000

It has two problems:

  • It overstates accounts receivable in the periods before the write-off, because it ignores accounts that everyone knows some customers won't pay.
  • It distorts net income by pushing the expense into the year the customer defaults, often long after the sale that caused it.

So the direct write-off method is not allowed for financial reporting unless bad debts are immaterial. It is, however, the method required for income tax purposes for most companies. That is why the allowance for bad debts creates a deferred tax asset, which you will meet in Intermediate Accounting II.

The allowance method

GAAP requires the allowance method whenever bad debts are material. A contra-asset account, the allowance for uncollectible accounts (also called the allowance for credit losses), reduces accounts receivable to the amount the company expects to collect. The expense is recognized when the allowance is estimated, not when a specific account goes bad.

Sylva Hardware Supply begins operations in Year 1. Credit sales are $900,000 and collections are $640,000, leaving $260,000 of accounts receivable at December 31. Management expects to collect $238,000 of it.

Key idea

Write-offs and reinstatements move accounts receivable and the allowance together, so they never change net receivables or net income. Only the estimate (bad debt expense) affects income.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 326-20-30-1 requires an allowance for credit losses so the net amount presented reflects the amount expected to be collected.
  • ASC 326-20-35-8 says write-offs are deducted from the allowance in the period a financial asset is deemed uncollectible, and recoveries of amounts previously written off are recorded in the allowance.
  • ASC 326-20-45-1 presents the allowance as a deduction from the amortized cost of the receivables.

Estimating the allowance under CECL

U.S. GAAP uses the current expected credit loss (CECL) model. CECL does not prescribe one method. It allows any reasonable approach that captures the losses the company expects over the life of its receivables, so net receivables reflect the cash it expects to collect. The estimate should use all relevant information: past experience, current conditions, and reasonable and supportable forecasts. Receivables with similar risk are evaluated together as a pool.

Common approaches:

  • Apply one loss percentage to total receivables.
  • Analyze individual customer accounts.
  • Use an aging schedule, applying higher percentages to older receivables. This is the most common in practice.

The balance sheet approach with an aging schedule

The aging schedule tells you what the allowance should be. Bad debt expense is then the plug that brings the allowance from its pre-adjustment balance to that required balance.

A credit to bad debt expense looks odd, but it is just a change in estimate: the allowance was built too high in an earlier period. As with other estimates, prior years are not restated.

The income statement approach and combined approaches

The income statement approach estimates bad debt expense directly as a percentage of the period's net credit sales, based on the company's history (or industry data for a new company). If Sylva expected 1.5% of its $900,000 Year 1 credit sales to go bad, it would record $13,500 of expense. The allowance balance is then a by-product, not the goal.

That approach is acceptable only if it does not distort the net amount expected to be collected. Many companies use it for convenience during the year and then true up the allowance at year-end with an aging.

A combined approach

Suppose Sylva recorded $13,500 of bad debt expense during Year 1 using 1.5% of credit sales, and the year-end analysis says the allowance should be $22,000.

Bad debts and earnings quality

An ethics checkpoint

Bad debt estimates involve judgment, and judgment can be abused. A division manager who has already beaten this year's bonus target might want the allowance raised from 3% to 5% of receivables to "bank" profit for next year, even though 3% is the better estimate. That creates a "cookie jar" reserve that can be released later. It misstates both years and is an ethical problem, even if auditors allow some range for estimates.

Analysts watch for signs of trouble: receivables growing much faster than sales, or an allowance that swings without a business reason. In one well-known case, unusually high receivables and big changes in the allowance were early clues that a company was inflating sales through "bill and hold" arrangements.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

IFRS 9 uses an expected credit loss (ECL) model. For most receivables it records a 12-month ECL, covering defaults that could happen in the next 12 months, and moves to a lifetime ECL only if credit quality has deteriorated significantly. For trade receivables, IFRS 9 allows a simplified approach that uses lifetime losses. U.S. GAAP's CECL always uses lifetime expected losses, so IFRS allowances are often smaller and recognized later. IFRS also tends to call the allowance a "provision for bad debts."

Common mistakes

  • Recording bad debt expense when an account is written off. Under the allowance method, the write-off debits the allowance, not expense.
  • Setting bad debt expense equal to the aging total. The aging gives the required balance; expense is the difference from the pre-adjustment balance.
  • Subtracting a debit pre-adjustment balance. A debit balance must be covered, so it adds to the expense.
  • Collecting a written-off account without reinstating it first.
  • Using the direct write-off method when bad debts are material.

Problem 3: Full roll-forward

Waterrock Woodworks starts the year with receivables of $420,000 and an allowance of $31,000. During the year: credit sales $1,600,000, collections $1,540,000, write-offs $27,000. The year-end aging requires an allowance of $36,000. Find ending receivables and bad debt expense.

Show the answer

Receivables: $420,000 + $1,600,000 − $1,540,000 − $27,000 = $453,000. Allowance before adjustment: $31,000 − $27,000 = $4,000 credit. Bad debt expense: $36,000 − $4,000 = $32,000. Net receivables: $453,000 − $36,000 = $417,000.

Key terms

Direct write-off method
Recognizes bad debt expense only when a specific account is written off; not GAAP unless immaterial.
Allowance method
Estimates uncollectible accounts in advance and reports receivables net of an allowance.
Allowance for uncollectible accounts
A contra-asset that reduces receivables to the amount expected to be collected; also called the allowance for credit losses.
CECL
Current expected credit loss model: the U.S. GAAP approach that estimates lifetime expected credit losses using past, current, and forecast information.
Aging schedule
A table that groups receivables by how long they have been outstanding and applies a loss rate to each group.
Balance sheet approach
Estimates the required allowance; bad debt expense is the adjustment needed to reach it.
Income statement approach
Estimates bad debt expense as a percent of credit sales.
Write-off
Removing a specific uncollectible account from receivables and the allowance.
Reinstatement
Reversing a write-off when a customer later pays.
