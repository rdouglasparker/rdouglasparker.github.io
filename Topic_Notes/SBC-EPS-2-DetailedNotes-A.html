A puzzle to start

Hunter Ridge Robotics hires a new chief executive. Her offer letter promises a modest salary, 50,000 shares of stock she cannot sell for four years, and options to buy another 200,000 shares at today’s price. On the day she signs, the company pays her nothing for the shares or the options. Has Hunter Ridge incurred an expense? If so, how much, and when should it show up in the income statement?

What the new CEO receives (Hunter Ridge Robotics)
ElementCash paid by the company today?Value to the CEO?
SalaryYes, as earnedYes
Restricted sharesNoYes: the shares are worth their market price
Stock optionsNoYes: the right to buy shares later at a fixed price has value now

Shares and options are valuable, and the CEO earns them by working for the company. They are compensation just as surely as salary is, even though no cash changes hands. The accounting answers two questions: what is the compensation worth, and over which periods is it earned?

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept round so the accounting, not the arithmetic, is the focus. Unless stated otherwise, ignore income taxes, and assume shares have a $1 par value.

The accounting objective

Share-based plans come in many forms: outright awards of shares, restricted stock units, stock options, stock appreciation rights, and employee share purchase plans. The accounting objective is the same for every one of them:

  1. Measure the fair value of the compensation at the grant date, the date the company and the employee agree to the award’s terms.
  2. Recognize that amount as compensation expense over the service period, the periods in which the employee performs the services the award pays for. The service period is usually the vesting period, from the grant date until the employee is entitled to the award.

The pattern behind every entry

Each year of the service period: debit Compensation expense, credit a paid-in capital account (for awards settled in shares) or a liability (for awards that can be settled in cash). Nothing is recorded on the grant date itself, because no service has been performed yet.

Why paid-in capital? When an award will be settled in shares, the employee is effectively contributing services in exchange for an equity interest. The credit builds up the equity the employee is earning, just as cash paid for shares builds paid-in capital.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 718-10-30 measures an equity-classified award at its fair value on the grant date.
  • ASC 718-10-35 recognizes the compensation cost over the requisite service period, which is usually the vesting period.

Restricted stock awards and restricted stock units

A restricted stock award gives the employee actual shares, registered in the employee’s name, that cannot be sold during a restriction period and are forfeited if the employee leaves before the shares vest. A restricted stock unit (RSU) is a promise to deliver a specified number of shares when the vesting requirement is met; no shares are issued until then. RSUs are now far more common than restricted stock awards, and both have largely replaced stock options as the main form of executive pay.

The accounting for the two is essentially the same when they will be settled in shares:

  • Measure the compensation as the market price of an unrestricted share on the grant date times the number of shares.
  • Expense it straight-line over the vesting period, crediting Paid-in capital—restricted stock.
  • At vesting, replace paid-in capital—restricted stock with Common stock (par) and Paid-in capital—excess of par.
  • Market price changes after the grant date do not change total compensation.

Forfeitures of restricted stock

If an employee leaves before vesting, the shares or units are forfeited. The entries previously recorded for that employee are reversed, lowering compensation expense in the year of the forfeiture. The remaining compensation, adjusted for the forfeiture, is then spread over the rest of the service period.

Two variations

  • Deferred compensation. Some companies record the full grant-date value at the grant date: debit Deferred compensation (a contra-equity account) and credit the stock accounts. Deferred compensation is then credited as compensation expense is recorded. The income statement and total equity end up exactly the same as the approach above.
  • Cash-settled RSUs are liabilities. If the employee will receive cash, or can choose cash, the award is a liability. The credit each year goes to Liability—restricted stock, and the liability (and compensation) is adjusted each period to the shares’ current fair value until it is paid. The same idea applies to cash-settled stock appreciation rights, covered later in these notes.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 718-20 covers awards classified as equity, including restricted shares and restricted stock units settled in shares.
  • Awards that the employee can require the company to settle in cash are classified as liabilities under ASC 718-30 and remeasured at fair value each reporting date until settled.

Stock options

A stock option gives an employee the right to buy a specified number of shares at a fixed exercise price during a specified period. Options usually cannot be exercised until they vest, which encourages the employee to stay. The exercise price is usually set equal to the market price on the grant date, so the option has no intrinsic value when it is granted. It still has value, though: the employee can wait and buy later at today’s price if the share price rises.

Measuring the fair value of an option

Employee stock options do not trade, so their fair value is estimated with an option-pricing model (for example, Black-Scholes-Merton or a binomial lattice model). The model must consider six inputs:

The inputs to an option-pricing model, and how each affects an option’s value
If this input is higher……the option’s fair value is
Exercise priceLower
Expected term of the optionHigher
Current market price of the stockHigher
Expected dividendsLower
Risk-free interest rateHigher
Expected volatility of the stock priceHigher

An option’s fair value has two parts: intrinsic value (market price minus exercise price, if positive) and time value (the benefit of delaying payment of the exercise price, plus the chance the share price will rise while the holder’s loss is limited to the option’s value).

Forfeitures: two acceptable policies

Some options will be forfeited when employees leave before vesting. A company chooses, and discloses, one of two policies:

  • Estimate forfeitures (the default). Reduce total compensation for the options expected to be forfeited. When the estimate changes, adjust the cumulative expense in the year of the change: expense = (new total × fraction of service period completed) − amount already recorded.
  • Record forfeitures as they occur (a practical expedient for forfeitures caused by employee turnover). Start with all options granted, and reduce compensation only when a forfeiture actually happens, again with a cumulative catch-up.

  • ASC 718-10-55 describes the factors an option-pricing model must consider: the exercise price, expected term, current share price, expected volatility, expected dividends, and the risk-free rate.

Income tax effects (connecting to Accounting for Income Taxes)

The tax law treats two kinds of option plans differently, and that drives the deferred tax accounting you learned in the income tax unit:

  • Incentive (qualified) stock options. The employee pays no tax until the shares are later sold, and the company gets no tax deduction at all. With no future deduction there is no temporary difference, so there are no deferred taxes. The expense is effectively a permanent difference.
  • Nonqualified stock options. The company deducts the intrinsic value on the exercise date (market price − exercise price) on its tax return. Book expense comes first and the deduction comes later, so each year’s compensation expense creates a deferred tax asset equal to the expense × the tax rate. Restricted stock awards are handled the same way.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 718-740 requires a deferred tax asset for the cumulative compensation cost of awards that will produce a future tax deduction, and recognizes differences between the deduction and the recorded cost in income tax expense when the deduction occurs.

Plans with graded vesting

The options above all vest on one date (cliff vesting). More often, awards vest in pieces over several years, called graded vesting; for example, 25% after the first year, 25% after the second, and 50% after the third. A company with graded vesting can account for the award in either of two ways:

  • Separate tranches. Treat each vesting group (tranche) as its own award. Value each tranche separately, because each has a different expected life, and spread each tranche’s cost evenly over its own vesting period. Most companies choose this method because it usually produces a lower total fair value.
  • Straight-line. Spread the total cost of the whole award evenly over the entire vesting period.

Either way, the cumulative expense at any date must be at least the value of the options that have vested by that date.

Employee share purchase plans

An employee share purchase plan (ESPP) lets employees buy the company’s shares directly, usually through payroll deductions, often at a discount. The question is whether the plan is compensatory. A plan is noncompensatory (no expense) only if all three conditions are met:

  1. Substantially all employees can participate on an equitable basis.
  2. Employees have no more than one month after the price is fixed to decide whether to participate.
  3. The discount is no more than 5% (or can be justified as no greater than the cost of a public offering).

If any condition is not met, the discount is compensation expense.

Mountain Heritage Foods lets all employees buy its $1 par common stock at 15% below market price. During the year, employees buy 1,000 shares when the market price is $50.

Stock appreciation rights (going further)

A stock appreciation right (SAR) pays the employee the increase in the share price above a set price, without the employee having to buy any shares. Who chooses the form of payment decides the accounting:

  • The company can choose to settle in shares → equity. Measure fair value at the grant date and expense it over the service period, crediting Paid-in capital—SAR plan, exactly like stock options. The amount is not remeasured.
  • The employee will receive cash, or can choose cash → liability. Remeasure the SARs at fair value at every reporting date. Each period, expense = current fair value × fraction of the service period completed − amount already recognized. The adjustments continue after vesting until the SARs are exercised or expire.

Reading the numbers like an analyst

  • Incentives cut both ways. Managers paid in shares and options gain when the share price rises, which aligns their interests with shareholders but also creates pressure to meet earnings expectations. The risk of earnings management is probably higher at companies with generous share-based pay.
  • Watch the model inputs. Option fair values depend on subjective inputs such as expected volatility and expected term. Lower assumptions mean lower expense, so compare the assumptions in the disclosure note from year to year.
  • Non-GAAP measures often exclude it. Many companies report “adjusted” earnings that add back share-based compensation. It is a real cost to shareholders, paid in shares instead of cash, and it dilutes their ownership.
  • The shift to RSUs. Restricted stock is a more certain, though potentially less lucrative, form of pay than options, and many believe it ties pay to performance more sensibly.

Common mistakes

  • Recording an entry on the grant date. No service has been performed yet; the expense begins at the end of the first period.
  • Remeasuring equity awards. Restricted stock and options settled in shares are fixed at grant-date fair value. Only liability awards (cash-settled) are remeasured.
  • Using the market price at exercise. The exercise entry uses the cash received and the paid-in capital—stock options balance; the market price that day is irrelevant (except for the tax deduction on nonqualified options).
  • Spreading a change in forfeiture estimate over future years. Share-based compensation uses a cumulative catch-up in the year of the change.
  • Reversing expense when vested options expire. Expiration only renames paid-in capital; services were already received.
  • Treating a market condition like a performance condition. A share-price target does not affect whether compensation is recognized.
  • Calling every ESPP noncompensatory. A discount larger than 5% makes the discount compensation expense.

Options for 300,000 shares vest one-third each year for three years. Fair values are $3, $4, and $5 per option for the three tranches. Compute Year 1 compensation expense under the separate-tranche approach and under straight-line.

Show the answer

Tranche values: 100,000 × $3 = $300,000; 100,000 × $4 = $400,000; 100,000 × $5 = $500,000; total $1,200,000. Separate tranches, Year 1: $300,000 + $400,000 ÷ 2 + $500,000 ÷ 3 = $666,667. Straight-line: $1,200,000 ÷ 3 = $400,000, which is at least the $300,000 vested by the end of Year 1.

Key terms

Share-based compensation
Pay whose value depends on the value of the company’s shares: shares, units, options, or cash tied to the share price.
Grant date
The date the company and employee reach a shared understanding of the award’s terms; fair value is measured on this date.
Service period
The periods over which the employee performs the services the award pays for; usually the vesting period.
Vesting
The point at which the employee becomes entitled to the award and it is no longer subject to forfeiture.
Restricted stock award
Shares issued to an employee that cannot be sold and are forfeited if the employee leaves before vesting.
Restricted stock unit (RSU)
A right to receive a specified number of shares once vesting requirements are met; no shares are issued until then.
Stock option
The right to buy a specified number of shares at a fixed exercise price during a specified period.
Exercise price
The price an option holder pays for each share when exercising the option.
Intrinsic value
Market price minus exercise price, if positive.
Option-pricing model
A mathematical model, such as Black-Scholes-Merton or a binomial lattice, used to estimate an option’s fair value.
Cliff vesting
An entire award vests on a single date.
Graded vesting
An award vests in portions (tranches) over several dates.
Performance condition
A vesting target based on the company’s own operations, such as revenue or EPS; compensation is recorded only if achievement is probable.
Market condition
A vesting target based on the share price; reflected in fair value, with no special accounting.
Employee share purchase plan (ESPP)
A plan that lets employees buy company shares, often at a discount.
Stock appreciation right (SAR)
A right to receive the increase in share price above a set price, paid in cash or shares.
Incentive (qualified) stock option
An option plan that gives the employer no tax deduction, so it creates no deferred taxes.
Nonqualified stock option
An option plan in which the employer deducts the intrinsic value at exercise, creating a deferred tax asset as expense is recorded.
