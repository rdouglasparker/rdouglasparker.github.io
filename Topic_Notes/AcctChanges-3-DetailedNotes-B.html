Why error corrections matter

An accounting change is a choice. An error is a mistake. The distinction matters because errors mean the statements people already relied on were wrong.

Errors come from mathematical mistakes, misapplied accounting principles, or oversight and misuse of facts that existed when the statements were prepared. They are not the same as a change in estimate (where new information arrives later) or a change in principle (where one acceptable method replaces another). Fraud is an error too, and the correction is reported the same way, although the consequences for the company and its managers are far more serious.

Remember

If the error is discovered in the same period it was made, just fix it with an ordinary correcting entry before the statements are issued. The rest of these notes cover errors discovered in a later period, after statements containing the error were issued.

Where these rules live

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 250-10-45-23 Correction of an error in previously issued financial statements is reported as a prior period adjustment through restatement of those statements.
  • ASC 250-10-50 Disclosure: that the statements were restated, the effect of the correction on each line item and per share amounts, and the cumulative effect on retained earnings.

Error corrections share Topic 250 with accounting changes. That is why the two are taught together.

Reporting an error correction: four steps

When an error that affected prior-period statements is discovered, the company does four things:

  1. Record a correcting entry. The entry brings the accounts to the correct balances now. Any effect on prior years’ income goes to retained earnings, not to the current income statement.
  2. Restate the prior statements that are presented again for comparison, so they show correct amounts.
  3. Adjust beginning retained earnings of the earliest year presented. This is a prior period adjustment. If only the current year is presented, the adjustment goes to the current year’s beginning retained earnings.
  4. Disclose. A note says that previously issued statements were restated, describes the error, and gives the effect on each line item and on per share amounts.

Watch out

A prior period adjustment is never included in the current year’s net income. It appears in the statement of shareholders’ equity as an adjustment to the beginning balance of retained earnings, and the effect is shown after tax.

Example: an asset charged to expense

The most common long-lived asset error is expensing something that should have been capitalized. The error understates income in the year of the mistake (too much expense) and overstates income in later years (too little depreciation).

Pisgah Crest Machining: equipment expensed

On January 1, Year 1, Pisgah Crest Machining paid $210,000 for equipment with a five-year life and no residual value. The accountant expensed the cost. The error is discovered during Year 3, before the Year 3 books close. The company uses straight-line depreciation.

Step 3: Correcting entry (25% tax rate)

If the company paid less tax than it should have by deducting the full cost in Year 1, the tax effect of the correction is $126,000 × 25% = $31,500, owed to the government. If the books are still open for the tax years involved, the company would amend its returns.

Year 3: correct the error, with taxes

AccountDebitCredit
Equipment210,000
Accumulated depreciation84,000
Income tax payable ($126,000 × 25%)31,500
Retained earnings ($126,000 × 75%)94,500

Year 3 depreciation of $42,000 is recorded as usual in the current year. If statements for Year 1 and Year 2 are presented for comparison, they are restated and a note describes the error.

If the opposite mistake had been made (an expense capitalized as an asset), the same logic applies in reverse: income was overstated in the first year, and the correcting entry debits retained earnings and reduces the asset.

Inventory errors and how they correct themselves

Ending inventory of one year is the beginning inventory of the next. An error in ending inventory therefore affects two years of cost of goods sold, in opposite directions, and then disappears. The error is said to be self-correcting. Retained earnings is wrong only until the second year is closed.

The same logic applies to errors in beginning inventory, but the effects run in the opposite direction.

Mount Mitchell Mercantile: inventory understated

At the end of Year 1, Mount Mitchell Mercantile counted its inventory $80,000 too low. Income before tax was understated by $80,000 in Year 1 and overstated by $80,000 in Year 2. No other errors exist. Ignore taxes.

If Year 2 is already closed, the error has corrected itself. Even so, the Year 1 and Year 2 statements that contained the error, if presented again, are restated, and the company discloses the error.

Self-correcting is not the same as harmless

Self-correcting does not mean harmless. Year 1 income, Year 2 income, and the Year 1 balance sheet were all wrong, and any decision based on them was affected. The error still needs to be disclosed when the prior statements are presented again.

Cutoff errors and errors that do not affect income

Revenues and expenses recorded in the wrong period

If a sale was recorded in the wrong year or an accrued expense was left out, income in the affected year is wrong. Some of these also reverse themselves, because the transaction is eventually recorded in the next period, but the correcting entry depends on when the error is found and whether the books are closed.

Cullowhee Creek Outfitters: two cutoff errors

At the end of Year 1, Cullowhee Creek Outfitters recorded no entry for $36,000 of salaries earned in the last week of December (paid in January and expensed then). It also recorded a $45,000 sale in Year 2 although the goods were shipped, and title passed, on December 28 of Year 1. Both errors are found during Year 2, before the books close. Ignore taxes.

Salaries. Year 1 expense was understated by $36,000, so Year 1 income was overstated. In January of Year 2, the company debited salaries expense for the full amount. That overstates Year 2 expense. Correction: reduce the expense in Year 2 and reduce retained earnings for the Year 1 overstatement.

Year 2: correct the salaries error

Journal entry: Year 2: correct the salaries error
AccountDebitCredit
Retained earnings (prior period adjustment)36,000
Salaries expense36,000

Sale. The Year 1 sale was not recorded in Year 1, so Year 1 income was understated. The Year 2 sale record is in the wrong period. Correction: remove the sales revenue from Year 2 and increase retained earnings.

Year 2: correct the sales cutoff error

Journal entry: Year 2: correct the sales cutoff error
AccountDebitCredit
Sales revenue45,000
Retained earnings (prior period adjustment)45,000

The related cost of goods sold would be corrected the same way. The correction leaves Year 2 with only Year 2 activity and retained earnings equal to what it would have been had the transactions been recorded properly.

Errors that do not affect net income

Some errors misstate the balance sheet or the income statement presentation without changing net income. Examples are recording a payable as a receivable, classifying a current liability as noncurrent, or recording an expense in the wrong expense account. The correction is a reclassifying entry, and the prior statements presented for comparison are restated. No retained earnings adjustment is needed.

A mix of the two

Suppose a company discovers that the useful life of a machine was set at 10 years even though the company knew when it bought the machine that its life was 5. That is an error. If instead the company learns this year that technology has made the machine obsolete sooner, that is a change in estimate. The question to ask is: was the information available, and ignored or misused, at the time?

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

Under IFRS (IAS 8), material prior period errors are corrected retrospectively by restating comparative amounts, just as under U.S. GAAP. The difference: if it is impracticable to determine the effect of the error on a particular period, IFRS allows the company to correct it in the earliest period practicable, which may be the current period. U.S. GAAP also recognizes impracticability for changes in principle, but its error correction guidance is stricter about restating prior statements.

Common mistakes

  • Putting the correction in current income. The effect on prior years goes to retained earnings. Only current-year amounts, such as the current-year depreciation, stay in the current income statement.
  • Forgetting depreciation on the capitalized asset. When an expensed asset is capitalized, accumulated depreciation for the years already passed must also be recorded.
  • Ignoring taxes. If the question gives a tax rate, the retained earnings adjustment is net of tax and the other side of the tax effect is income tax payable (or a refund receivable).
  • Calling an inventory error self-correcting too soon. It is not corrected until both years are closed. Discovered after one year, you still need the entry.
  • Reversing the effect. Understated ending inventory means overstated cost of goods sold and understated income that year, and the opposite next year.

Practice with answers

Problem 1

A company expensed a $60,000 machine on January 1, Year 1. Its life is 6 years, with no residual value, straight-line. The error is found at the start of Year 3 (before Year 3 entries). Ignoring taxes, give the correcting entry.

Show the answer

Depreciation should have been $10,000 a year. Accumulated depreciation after two years: $20,000. Retained earnings was understated by $60,000 − $20,000 = $40,000.

Year 3

Journal entry: Year 3
AccountDebitCredit
Machine60,000
Accumulated depreciation20,000
Retained earnings40,000

Problem 2

Ending inventory at December 31, Year 1 was overstated by $30,000. The error is discovered in March of Year 2. Give the entry, ignoring taxes.

Show the answer

Year 1 income was overstated by $30,000, so retained earnings is overstated.

March, Year 2

Journal entry: March, Year 2
AccountDebitCredit
Retained earnings30,000
Inventory30,000

Problem 3

The error in the previous question is not discovered until Year 3. What entry is required?

Show the answer

None. The error counter-balanced: Year 2 income was understated by $30,000, so retained earnings is correct at the start of Year 3. The prior statements are restated if presented, and the error is disclosed.

Problem 4

Name two differences between how a change in estimate and an error correction are reported.

Show the answer

A change in estimate is prospective, with no restatement and no retained earnings adjustment. An error correction restates prior statements and adjusts beginning retained earnings as a prior period adjustment.

Key terms

Error
A mistake in previously issued financial statements: math, misapplied principles, or misused facts.
Prior period adjustment
The after-tax effect of an error correction on retained earnings, reported as an adjustment to the beginning balance.
Restatement
Revising prior-period financial statements to correct an error.
Self-correcting error
An error that reverses itself by the end of the next period, such as an inventory error.
Cutoff error
A transaction recorded in the wrong period.
