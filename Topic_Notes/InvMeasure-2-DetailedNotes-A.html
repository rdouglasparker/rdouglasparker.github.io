A puzzle to start

The Catamount Campus Store paid $42,000 for a shipment of rain jackets in August. By December a new style has arrived, and the old jackets will sell for only $45,000, with $6,000 of shipping and sales commissions to move them. The store expected to make money on these jackets. Does anything need to be recorded before they are sold?

Inventory is recorded at cost when it is bought. But an asset should not be reported at more than the company expects to get back from it. When the benefit expected from unsold inventory drops below its cost, because of damage, obsolescence, falling prices, or weaker demand, the company writes the inventory down at the end of the period. This inventory write-down reduces both the asset and net income in the period the value is lost, not later when the goods are sold.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Amounts are kept small and round so you can focus on the reasoning.

Where these rules live: the FASB Codification

The rules for measuring inventory after it is acquired are in Topic 330, Inventory, in subtopic 330-10, section 35, Subsequent Measurement. Which test a company applies depends on its cost flow method.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 330-10-35-1B: inventory measured using any method other than LIFO or the retail inventory method (FIFO, average cost, specific identification) is measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value.
  • ASC 330-10-35-1C: inventory measured using LIFO or the retail inventory method is measured at the lower of cost or market.
  • ASC 330-10-35-14: a write-down creates a new cost basis that is not marked back up if the value later recovers.
  • ASC 330-10-35-17 and 35-18: losses on firm purchase commitments are recognized when the market price falls below the contract price.

Lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV)

Net realizable value (NRV) is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, minus reasonably predictable costs of completion, disposal, and transportation. If NRV is below cost, the inventory is written down to NRV.

The rule in one line

Carrying value = the lower of cost or NRV, where NRV = estimated selling price − costs to complete and sell.

Item by item, by category, or in total

U.S. GAAP lets a company apply the test to individual items, to logical categories, or to the entire inventory. The broader the grouping, the more an item that has lost value is offset by items that have gained value, so the write-down usually gets smaller.

Recording the write-down

Most companies include a routine write-down in cost of goods sold. A material write-down is often reported as a separate line item, such as a loss on write-down of inventory, so readers can see it.

The written-down amount becomes the inventory’s new cost. Under U.S. GAAP, if the selling price of the rain jackets recovers next year, the write-down is not reversed.

Lower of cost or market (LCM) for LIFO and retail

Companies that use LIFO or the retail inventory method apply an older test, the lower of cost or market. The twist is how “market” is defined.

  1. Start with replacement cost (RC): what it would cost to buy or make the item today.
  2. Ceiling = NRV. Market can’t be higher than net realizable value.
  3. Floor = NRV − normal profit margin. Market can’t be lower than this.
  4. Designated market = the middle value of replacement cost, the ceiling, and the floor.
  5. Compare designated market with cost and keep the lower amount.

The ceiling keeps inventory from being reported above what the company will collect. The floor keeps it from being written down so far that the company reports an unusually large profit when the goods are sold later.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

International standards (IAS 2, Inventories) use lower of cost and net realizable value for all inventory; LIFO isn’t allowed under IFRS, so there is no LCM test. The biggest difference is what happens when value recovers.

Catamount Campus Store under IFRS

Next year the rain jackets (cost $42,000, written down to $39,000) are still on hand, and their NRV recovers to $41,000.

Under IFRS the store reverses $2,000 of the write-down, bringing the jackets back to $41,000. If NRV had recovered to $44,000, the reversal would stop at $3,000, the original cost. Under U.S. GAAP, nothing is recorded.

Purchase commitments

A purchase commitment is a contract that obligates a company to buy a set amount of goods at a set price by a set date. It secures supply and protects against price increases, but if the market price falls, the company is stuck paying the higher contract price. The loss belongs in the period the price falls, so it is recorded then, consistent with the write-down rules above.

Tuckasegee Textile Works (fiscal year ends December 31) signs two noncancelable contracts in September: one to buy yarn for $300,000 by November 30, and one to buy wool for $480,000 by February 15 of next year.

Common mistakes

  • Using selling price as NRV. NRV is the selling price minus the costs to complete and sell.
  • Applying LCNRV to a LIFO company. LIFO and retail-method companies use LCM; everyone else uses LCNRV.
  • Picking the lowest of the three market values. Designated market is the middle value of replacement cost, the ceiling, and the floor.
  • Forgetting the final comparison with cost. Designated market is compared with cost; if cost is lower, nothing is written down.
  • Reversing a write-down under U.S. GAAP. The written-down amount is the new cost; only IFRS allows reversals, and never above original cost.
  • Waiting until delivery to record a purchase commitment loss. An open commitment with a market price below the contract price produces a loss at year-end.

Practice with answers

Try each problem on paper, then open the answer to check your work.

Problem 1: LCNRV for one product

Sylva Outdoor Supply (FIFO) has kayaks that cost $36,000. They are expected to sell for $40,000, with $5,500 of delivery and sales commissions. At what amount are they reported, and what entry is needed?

Show the answer

NRV = $40,000 − $5,500 = $34,500. That is below cost, so the kayaks are reported at $34,500, and a $1,500 write-down is recorded: debit cost of goods sold (or loss on write-down) $1,500; credit inventory $1,500.

Problem 2: designated market

A LIFO company’s product has cost $14, replacement cost $11, NRV $16, and a normal profit of $3. What is its LCM per unit?

Show the answer

Ceiling = $16; floor = $16 − $3 = $13. The middle of $11, $16, and $13 is $13. Since $13 is below cost of $14, LCM is $13 per unit.

Problem 3: an open purchase commitment

At year-end a company has a noncancelable contract to buy materials for $90,000 next March. The market price at year-end is $82,000; in March it is $79,000. Record the year-end and March entries.

Show the answer

Year-end: debit estimated loss on purchase commitment $8,000; credit estimated liability on purchase commitment $8,000. March: debit inventory $79,000, debit estimated liability $8,000, debit loss on purchase commitment $3,000; credit cash $90,000.

Key terms

Inventory write-down
An end-of-period adjustment that reduces inventory, and net income, when the inventory’s expected benefit has fallen below cost.
Net realizable value (NRV)
Estimated selling price minus reasonably predictable costs to complete, dispose of, and transport the goods.
Lower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV)
The measurement rule for inventory costed with any method other than LIFO or the retail method.
Lower of cost or market (LCM)
The measurement rule for inventory costed with LIFO or the retail method.
Replacement cost
The current cost to buy or produce the item.
Ceiling
NRV; the most that designated market can be.
Floor
NRV minus a normal profit margin; the least that designated market can be.
Designated market
The middle value of replacement cost, the ceiling, and the floor.
Purchase commitment
A contract obligating a company to buy specified goods at a specified price by a specified date.
