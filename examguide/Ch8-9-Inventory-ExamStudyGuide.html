What Belongs in Inventory

Key idea: Inventory is what the company owns, not just what is in its building, and its cost includes everything needed to get the goods ready for sale.

Concept notes: ownership and inventory cost

FASB ASC 330, Inventory

What inventory is

Inventory is the goods a company (1) holds for sale in the normal course of business, (2) is producing for future sale, or (3) will use in producing goods for sale. The primary basis for measuring it is cost.

Types of inventory
Type of companyAccountWhat the cost includes
MerchandiserMerchandise inventoryPurchase price plus costs to get the goods ready for sale
ManufacturerRaw materialsPurchase price of parts and materials, plus freight
ManufacturerWork in processDirect materials used + direct labor + allocated manufacturing overhead
ManufacturerFinished goodsAll costs accumulated in work in process for completed products

Which goods belong in inventory

The test is not “what is in the building” but who holds title (and the risks of ownership).

Ownership rules at year-end
SituationWhose inventory?
Goods in transit, f.o.b. shipping pointThe buyer: title passed when the goods were shipped
Goods in transit, f.o.b. destinationThe seller: title passes when the goods arrive
Goods out on consignmentThe consignor (owner), even though the consignee holds them
Goods held on consignment for another companyNot yours: exclude them from your count

Right of return

When customers can return goods, the seller records a refund liability at the sales price of expected returns and an asset (inventory — estimated returns) for the goods expected back at their cost.

Which costs belong in inventory

  • Product costs (inventory, expensed when the goods are sold): purchase price net of trade discounts, freight-in, insurance in transit on goods bought f.o.b. shipping point, and other costs of getting goods ready for sale.
  • Period costs (expensed when incurred): freight-out on goods shipped to customers, sales commissions, advertising, abnormal spoilage, and idle-facility costs.

Study tip: Work every ownership question from the point of view of the company doing the count. Ask: who has title right now? Then add what you own but did not count, and remove what you counted but do not own.

Periodic and Perpetual Systems and Purchase Discounts

Key idea: A periodic system records purchases in temporary accounts and finds cost of goods sold at year-end; a perpetual system updates Inventory and cost of goods sold with every transaction.

Concept notes: periodic and perpetual entries

Periodic vs. perpetual

A perpetual system updates Inventory and records cost of goods sold with every transaction. A periodic system uses temporary accounts during the year and updates Inventory only at year-end, based on a physical count.

Which account is debited or credited?
EventPerpetual systemPeriodic system
Buy inventory on accountDebit InventoryDebit Purchases
Pay freight on purchasesDebit InventoryDebit Freight-in
Return goods to supplierCredit InventoryCredit Purchase returns
Take a discount (gross method)Credit InventoryCredit Purchase discounts
Sell goodsRecord the revenue and debit Cost of goods sold, credit InventoryRecord the revenue only: no cost entry at the time of sale
End of periodAdjust for shrinkage if the count differs from the recordsAdjusting entry: record ending inventory and cost of goods sold, remove beginning inventory, and close the temporary purchase accounts

The cost of goods sold equation (periodic)

Beginning inventory + net purchases − ending inventory = cost of goods sold

Net purchases = purchases + freight-in − purchase returns − purchase discounts.

Purchase discounts: gross vs. net

Terms of 2/10, n/30 mean a 2% discount if paid within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days.

Gross vs. net method
Gross methodNet method
Purchase recorded atFull invoice priceInvoice price less the discount

A trade discount (for example, 25% off list price) is never recorded separately: the purchase is simply recorded at the discounted invoice price, and any cash discount is figured on that invoice price.

Study tip: Under the net method a missed discount is a financing cost, so it is interest expense, never part of Purchases, Inventory, or cost of goods sold. Leave it out of the year-end adjusting entry.

Cost Flow Methods

Key idea: FIFO, LIFO, and average cost split the same goods available between ending inventory and cost of goods sold; they differ only in which costs go where.

Concept notes: FIFO, LIFO, and average cost

The allocation problem

Every cost flow method divides the same cost of goods available for sale between ending inventory and cost of goods sold. FIFO, LIFO, and average cost are assumptions about the flow of costs; they do not have to match the physical flow of the goods.

The four methods
MethodCost of goods sold carries…Ending inventory carries…
Specific identificationThe actual cost of the units soldThe actual cost of the units on hand
Weighted average (periodic)Total cost available ÷ total units availableThe same average cost
FIFOThe oldest costsThe newest costs
LIFOThe newest costsThe oldest costs

Specific identification works best for unique, high-cost items sold in low volume (boats, cars, jewelry).

Periodic vs. perpetual results

  • FIFO always gives the same answer either way: the oldest units are the first out no matter when you compute.
  • LIFO differs: perpetual LIFO takes the newest units on hand at the date of each sale.
  • Average cost differs: perpetual uses a moving average recomputed after each purchase.
When unit costs are rising
FIFOLIFO
Cost of goods soldLowerHigher
Ending inventoryHigher (closer to current cost)Lower
Net income and income taxesHigherLower

When costs fall, the results reverse. When costs are stable, all methods give the same results. Average cost usually falls between FIFO and LIFO.

Rules about choosing a method

  • LIFO conformity rule: a company that uses LIFO on its tax return must also use LIFO in its financial statements.
  • IFRS (IAS 2) permits specific identification, FIFO, and weighted average, but prohibits LIFO.
  • A company must disclose its method, and a voluntary change must be to a preferable method.

Study tip: Before you compute anything, write down the goods available in units and dollars. Every method must split that same total, so ending inventory + cost of goods sold must equal it.

LIFO Liquidations

Key idea: When sales outpace purchases, LIFO dips into old, low-cost layers; the gap between current and old cost raises income and taxes, and the company discloses it.

Concept notes: LIFO liquidations

What a LIFO liquidation is

Under LIFO, each year’s increase in inventory adds a new layer at that year’s cost. When units sold exceed units purchased, the company dips into old layers, and their old, low costs flow into cost of goods sold.

LIFO liquidation profit = units liquidated × (current cost − old layer cost)

Layers are liquidated newest first: use up the most recent layer before touching an older one. The older the layer, the bigger the profit per unit.

How it is reported

  • No separate journal entry: the effect is already inside cost of goods sold.
  • Because the profit is nonrecurring and does not reflect current margins, the company discloses the effect on income in the notes to the financial statements.
  • Liquidation profit is taxable. Buying enough inventory before year-end avoids the liquidation and lowers income tax currently payable.

Study tip: After-tax effect on net income = pre-tax liquidation profit × (1 − tax rate). Tax saved by avoiding the liquidation = pre-tax profit × tax rate.

Subsequent Measurement: LCNRV, LCM, and Purchase Commitments

Key idea: Inventory is written down when its value falls below cost: FIFO and average-cost companies compare cost with NRV; LIFO and retail-method companies compare cost with designated market.

Concept notes: lower of cost rules

FASB ASC 330-10-35, Subsequent measurement

Which rule applies?

LCNRV vs. LCM
Inventory methodRuleCompare cost with
FIFO, average cost, specific identificationLower of cost or net realizable value (LCNRV)NRV = estimated selling price − costs to sell
LIFO or the retail inventory methodLower of cost or market (LCM)Designated market (below)

Designated market (LCM only)

  • Ceiling = NRV.
  • Floor = NRV − normal profit.
  • Designated market = the middle value of replacement cost, the ceiling, and the floor.
  • Then compare designated market with cost and use the lower amount.

Applying the rule and recording the write-down

The rule can be applied to individual items, categories, or the entire inventory; the broader the grouping, the smaller the write-down usually is, because gains on some items offset losses on others. A routine write-down is usually included in cost of goods sold; a material one may be reported as a separate loss.

Recoveries: U.S. GAAP vs. IFRS

Under U.S. GAAP the written-down amount becomes the new cost and is never reversed. IFRS reverses a write-down when value recovers, but not above original cost.

Purchase commitments

  • Record a loss in the period the market price falls below a noncancelable contract price, not when the goods are sold.
  • Goods received during the year: record inventory at market; the difference is a loss.
  • Contract still open at year-end: debit a loss and credit an estimated liability on purchase commitment.
  • Material commitments are disclosed even when no loss is recorded.

Study tip: For LCM, write the three numbers (replacement cost, ceiling, floor) in order from low to high and circle the middle one. Only then compare it with cost.

Estimating Inventory

Key idea: When a count isn’t practical, estimate ending inventory from sales and a stable cost relationship: the gross profit ratio or a cost-to-retail percentage.

Concept notes: gross profit and retail methods

The gross profit method

Used for interim reports and to estimate losses from theft or fire; it is not acceptable for annual financial statements.

  • Estimated cost of goods sold = net sales × (1 − gross profit ratio).
  • Estimated ending inventory = goods available − estimated cost of goods sold.
  • A markup on cost converts to a gross profit ratio: markup ÷ (1 + markup). A 25% markup on cost = 25% ÷ 125% = 20% of sales.

The retail inventory method

Ending inventory at retail = goods available at retail − net sales (and other deductions). Convert to cost with a cost-to-retail percentage.
