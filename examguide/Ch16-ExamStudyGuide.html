Temporary and Permanent Differences

Key idea: Income tax expense includes the tax due now and the change in taxes that will be paid or saved later because of temporary differences. Permanent differences never create deferred taxes.

Concept notes: temporary differences, permanent differences, and the four-step process

FASB ASC 740-10, Income Taxes — Overall

Why deferred taxes exist

Pretax accounting income follows GAAP; taxable income follows the tax code. When the two recognize the same revenue or expense in different years, the difference is temporary: it originates now and reverses later. Income tax expense is matched with the income in the income statement, so the future tax effect of a temporary difference is recorded now as a deferred tax liability (DTL) or deferred tax asset (DTA).

The four-step process

Recording income taxes in four steps
StepWhat you calculate
1. Income tax payableTaxable income × the current year’s enacted rate
2. Ending deferred balancesFuture taxable amounts × enacted future rate = DTL; future deductible amounts × enacted future rate = DTA
3. Change in each accountEnding balance − beginning balance (in the first year, the change is the whole ending balance)
4. Income tax expenseThe plug: payable + increase in DTL − increase in DTA (a decrease flips the sign)

The income tax entry

Every year-end entry has the same shape. Income tax expense is the amount that makes it balance; a DTA increase is a debit, a DTL increase is a credit (decreases are the reverse).

The four types of temporary differences

Types of temporary differences
Revenue or gainExpense or loss
Creates a DTL (future taxable amounts)Recognized in the income statement before it is taxed. Example: installment sales taxed as cash is collected.Deducted on the tax return before it is expensed. Examples: accelerated tax depreciation; prepaid expenses deducted when paid.
Creates a DTA (future deductible amounts)Taxed before it is recognized as revenue. Examples: rent or subscriptions collected in advance.Expensed before it is deducted on the tax return. Examples: warranties, loss contingencies, paid future absences, bad debts.

Quick test: ask what happens to taxable income when the difference reverses. More taxable income later = future taxable amount = DTL. Less taxable income later = future deductible amount = DTA.

Permanent differences

A permanent difference never reverses, so it creates no deferred tax. Adjust for it when computing taxable income, then ignore it. Common examples:

  • Interest on state and municipal bonds (not taxable)
  • Fines and penalties for violating the law (not deductible)
  • Life insurance premiums when the company is the beneficiary (not deductible) and life insurance proceeds (not taxable)
  • The portion of dividends excluded by the dividends-received deduction
  • Compensation expense for some employee stock option plans (not deductible)

Check figure: when the tax rate is the same every year, income tax expense = (pretax accounting income ± permanent differences) × rate. Temporary differences change when tax is paid, not total expense.

Multiple Differences and Financial Statement Reporting

Key idea: Reconcile pretax accounting income to taxable income, measure ending deferred balances from year-end temporary differences, and let income tax expense be the plug.

Concept notes: multiple differences and reporting

FASB ASC 740-10-45 (presentation) and 740-10-50 (disclosure)

Work from a schedule

With several differences, start at pretax accounting income and adjust line by line to reach taxable income. Then build a second schedule of the temporary differences that still exist at year-end, sorted into future taxable amounts and future deductible amounts.

Signs in the reconciliation from pretax accounting income to taxable income
ItemAdjustment
Nontaxable revenue (municipal interest)Subtract
Nondeductible expense (fines, officers’ life insurance)Add
Tax depreciation greater than book depreciationSubtract the excess
Installment gross profit not yet taxedSubtract
Expense accrued but not yet deductible (warranty, contingency, vacation pay)Add the expense, subtract the amount paid (net: the increase in the liability)
Revenue collected in advance and taxed nowAdd the increase in the unearned revenue balance

Use balances, not just this year’s flows

When there are beginning deferred balances, compute the ending DTL and DTA from the cumulative temporary differences at year-end, then subtract the beginning balance to find the change. A quick check: the change in each account equals this year’s originating (or reversing) difference × the rate.

Current and deferred components

Income tax expense is disclosed in two pieces: the current portion (income tax payable) and the deferred portion (the net change in the DTL and DTA). Companies also reconcile their statutory rate with their effective rate (income tax expense ÷ pretax accounting income); permanent differences are what move the two apart.

Balance sheet classification

  • All deferred tax assets and liabilities are noncurrent.
  • Those that relate to the same tax jurisdiction (and the same taxpaying component) are netted into one noncurrent amount.
  • Balances from different jurisdictions (federal vs. a state, or the U.S. vs. another country) are not offset.

Valuation Allowance

Key idea: Recognize every deferred tax asset, then reduce it if it is more likely than not that some of it will not be realized.

Concept notes: the valuation allowance

FASB ASC 740-10-30-5 and 30-16 through 30-25

Recognize the DTA, then test it

A DTA is recognized for every deductible temporary difference and carryforward. Then ask: is it more likely than not (greater than 50%) that some or all of it will not be realized? If so, reduce it with a valuation allowance, a contra-asset.

Year-end
Journal entry, Year-end
AccountDebitCredit
Income tax expenseamount
Valuation allowanceamount

Amount = the portion of the DTA not expected to be realized. The entry increases income tax expense.

Evidence to weigh

  • Negative: recent cumulative losses, a history of carryforwards expiring unused, losses expected in early future years.
  • Positive: existing contracts or backlog that will produce taxable income, DTLs that will reverse and create taxable income, a strong earnings history apart from an unusual loss.

Revisit the allowance every year-end. If prospects improve, reduce the allowance (debit Valuation allowance, credit Income tax expense).

Watch the wording: a question that says “more likely than not that one-fourth will not be realized” wants an allowance of one-fourth of the DTA, not three-fourths.

Tax Rates: Scheduled Rates and Rate Changes

Key idea: Deferred taxes use the enacted rate for each reversal year. When a new rate is enacted, remeasure existing balances in the year of enactment through income tax expense.

Concept notes: enacted rates and changes in rates

FASB ASC 740-10-30-8 (enacted rates) and 740-10-35-4 (changes in tax laws or rates)

Use enacted rates only

Measure deferred taxes with the rate enacted for the year each difference will reverse. A rate that is only proposed or expected is ignored until it becomes law.

Scheduled (phased-in) rates

If enacted rates differ by year, multiply each year’s reversal by that year’s rate and add them up. Income tax payable still uses the current year’s rate.

A change in the enacted rate

When a new rate is enacted, remeasure every existing DTL and DTA at the new rate in the year of enactment, not the year the rate takes effect and not retrospectively. The adjustment flows through income tax expense from continuing operations. Income tax payable for the current year is unaffected unless the current year’s own rate changed.

How a rate change affects income tax expense
Rate goes upRate goes down
Existing DTLDTL increases → expense increasesDTL decreases → expense decreases
Existing DTADTA increases → expense decreasesDTA decreases → expense increases

Split the change: in a year with both a reversal and a rate change, separate the DTL (or DTA) change into (1) the part from this year’s reversal at the old rate and (2) the remeasurement of what remains at the difference in rates. The two pieces add to the total change.

Net Operating Losses

Key idea: The tax benefit of an NOL is recognized in the year of the loss: a DTA for a carryforward, a refund receivable for an allowed carryback.

Concept notes: net operating losses

FASB ASC 740-10-25-3 and 740-10-45-6

Carryforwards (most companies)

  • An NOL is carried forward indefinitely.
  • In any future year it can offset at most 80% of taxable income; the rest of that year’s income is taxed.
  • In the year of the loss, recognize a DTA = NOL × enacted future rate, and an income tax benefit (a credit to income tax expense). Apply the valuation allowance test to that DTA.

Carrybacks (some farm businesses and insurance companies only)

Income statement

The benefit is recognized in the year of the loss and shown below the operating loss: Operating loss before income taxes − Income tax benefit = Net loss. The benefit can be split into a current portion (carryback refund) and a deferred portion (carryforward).

Common slip: the 80% limit applies to the year the NOL is used, not the year of the loss. In the loss year, the DTA is the full NOL × rate.
