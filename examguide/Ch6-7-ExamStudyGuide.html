Revenue Recognition: The Five-Step Model

Key idea: Revenue is recognized when (or as) a seller transfers control of goods or services to a customer, in an amount that reflects what the seller expects to be entitled to receive.

Problem 1 — The Five-Step Model: Ground Rules

Settle three ground rules before you calculate anything.

Explanation

  1. Probable. A contract exists when the parties have approved it, rights and payment terms can be identified, it has commercial substance, and it is probable the seller will collect. Delivering the goods is not a contract criterion; it's how an obligation is satisfied.
  2. Both tests. A good or service must be capable of being distinct (the customer could benefit from it alone or with readily available resources) and separately identifiable in the contract (not significantly integrated with, modifying, or dependent on other promises). If both tests aren't met, combine the promises into one performance obligation.
  3. Simultaneous receipt and consumption. Other over-time criteria: the seller's work creates or enhances an asset the customer controls, or the asset has no alternative use to the seller and the seller has a right to payment for work done. Payment terms and contract length don't decide timing.

The five steps: (1) identify the contract, (2) identify the performance obligations, (3) determine the transaction price, (4) allocate the price to the obligations, (5) recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied.

Explanation

  • Expected value (probability-weighted) fits when there are many possible outcomes.

No journal entry is recorded when the contract is signed. Cashiers records revenue (and the related receivable) as it performs the work, measured using the $268,000 transaction price.

Long-Term Contracts

Key idea: Over-time recognition uses a cumulative catch-up. Each year: percent complete × contract totals = amounts to date; then subtract what earlier years already recognized.

Explanation

Estimated total cost = $1,140,000 + $960,000 = $2,100,000, so the contract will lose $100,000.

When an overall loss is projected, the entire loss is recognized right away. Cashiers must reverse the $150,000 profit recognized in 2026 and record the full $100,000 projected loss:

$150,000 + $100,000 = $250,000 loss recognized in 2027

The same rule applies when revenue is recognized at completion (a point in time): no profit has been recognized yet, but the $100,000 projected loss is still recognized immediately. Accounting is conservative about losses.

For the entry, revenue is still recognized based on progress: $1,140,000 ÷ $2,100,000 = 54.29% complete; $2,000,000 × 54.29% = $1,085,714 to date − $500,000 recognized in 2026 = $585,714 for 2027. Cost of construction is the amount that produces the $250,000 loss: $585,714 + $250,000 = $835,714.

Cash

Key idea: Cash and cash equivalents must be available for current use. Anything restricted, longer-term, or not really cash is reported somewhere else.

Receivables

Key idea: Receivables are reported at net realizable value: the amount the company expects to collect. Discounts, returns, and expected credit losses all reduce that amount.
