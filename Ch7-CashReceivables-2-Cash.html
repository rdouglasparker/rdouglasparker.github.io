Cash and Cash Equivalents

What counts as cash, how companies protect it, how restrictions change its classification, and how to reconcile the bank account and run a petty cash fund.

A question to start

Laurel Branch Brewing has $4.2 million in its bank accounts at year-end, yet its balance sheet reports only $3.6 million of cash and cash equivalents. Nobody stole anything. So where did the other $600,000 go?

It is still there, but $500,000 sits in a fund the bank requires the brewery to keep for a long-term loan, and a $100,000 account is overdrawn at a bank where the brewery has no other accounts. Cash looks like the simplest asset on the balance sheet, but deciding what counts as cash, and protecting it, takes judgment. That is what these notes are about.

About the examples

Every company in these notes is fictional. Years are labeled Year 1 and Year 2 so the examples never go out of date.

What counts as cash

Cash includes currency and coins, balances in checking accounts, and items a bank will accept for deposit, such as checks and money orders received from customers. It is money available to pay debts or run operations with no legal or contractual restriction.

Companies rarely leave large balances sitting in checking. They invest idle cash in short-term, highly liquid investments that can be turned back into cash quickly with little risk. Those investments are cash equivalents, and they are combined with cash on the balance sheet.

The three-month test

To be a cash equivalent, an investment must mature within three months of the date the company acquires it. Typical examples are money market funds, Treasury bills, and commercial paper. What matters is the time to maturity on the day this company buys it, not the instrument's original term.

Companies have some flexibility in choosing which short-term investments they treat as cash equivalents, so they must disclose their policy. Many include credit and debit card receivables that settle within a few business days.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 230-10-20 (glossary) defines cash equivalents as short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and so near maturity that changes in interest rates pose little risk; generally only investments with original maturities of three months or less to the entity holding them qualify.
  • ASC 230-10-50-1 requires each company to disclose its policy for deciding which items it treats as cash equivalents.
  • ASC 305 (Cash and Cash Equivalents) is the Codification topic for cash itself.

Internal control over cash

Cash is the most liquid asset and the easiest to steal, so every company needs strong internal control: a plan to encourage adherence to company policies, promote efficient operations, minimize errors and theft, and make accounting data reliable.

For public companies, Section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires management to document and assess its internal control over financial reporting, and the auditor must give its own opinion. The COSO framework defines internal control as a process designed to give reasonable assurance about three objectives: effective and efficient operations, reliable financial reporting, and compliance with laws and regulations.

Separation of duties

The key principle is simple: the people who handle cash should not keep the records of cash, and neither should reconcile the bank account. That way no single person can take cash and hide it in the books.

  1. Receipts: open and list. One employee opens the mail and prepares a list of every check received.
  2. Receipts: deposit. A second employee takes the checks and a copy of the list to be deposited.
  3. Receipts: record. A third employee in accounting records the receipts from another copy of the list. The bank's deposit slip is compared to the list.
  4. Disbursements: authorize and pay by check. Pay everything except very small items by check or electronic transfer, approve each payment before it is made (matching invoice, purchase order, and receiving report), and limit who can sign.

Two more controls round out the system: a monthly bank reconciliation and a petty cash fund for small payments. Both are covered later in these notes.

Restricted cash and compensating balances

Only cash available for current operations or to pay current liabilities is a current asset. Cash that is restricted and not available for current use is usually reported as a noncurrent asset (investments or other assets).

  • Informal restrictions come from management's own plans, such as cash set aside to build a warehouse. If material, report it outside current assets.
  • Contractual restrictions come from agreements. A bond agreement may require a sinking fund to repay the debt. The restricted cash follows the debt: noncurrent if the debt is noncurrent, current if the debt is current.

Disclose material restrictions and the line items where restricted cash appears. On the statement of cash flows, restricted cash is included with cash and cash equivalents when reconciling the beginning and ending balances.

Compensating balances

A bank may require a borrower to keep a minimum balance, often 2% to 5% of the loan or line of credit, in a low- or no-interest account. This compensating balance compensates the bank for the loan. Because the borrower cannot use that money, the effective interest rate is higher than the stated rate.

Jackson Ridge Builders borrows $6,000,000

The loan carries an 8% stated rate, and the bank requires a $750,000 compensating balance in a noninterest-bearing account. The requirement is legally binding, and the loan is a five-year term loan.

Effective cost of the loan
ItemAmount
Annual interest ($6,000,000 × 8%)480,000
Cash actually available ($6,000,000 − $750,000)5,250,000
Effective interest rate ($480,000 ÷ $5,250,000)9.14%

Because the restriction is legally binding and the loan is long-term, the $750,000 is reported as a noncurrent asset, with note disclosure. If the arrangement were only informal, the balance could stay in cash and cash equivalents, still with disclosure.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 210-10-45-4 excludes from current assets cash that is restricted for purposes other than current operations, designated for acquiring noncurrent assets, or segregated to pay long-term debt.
  • ASC 210-10-S99-2 (SEC guidance) requires disclosure of compensating balance arrangements.
  • ASC 230-10-45-4 requires restricted cash to be included with cash and cash equivalents in the statement of cash flows reconciliation.

IFRS: bank overdrafts

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

Cash and cash equivalents are treated much the same under both frameworks. One difference involves bank overdrafts. U.S. GAAP generally reports an overdraft as a current liability. IFRS (IAS 7) allows an overdraft to be offset against other cash when it is repayable on demand and part of the company's normal cash management, with the balance often swinging between positive and negative.

Example. Balsam Grove Outfitters has $300,000 at one bank and is overdrawn $15,000 at another. U.S. GAAP: cash of $300,000 and a current liability of $15,000. IFRS (if the conditions are met): cash of $285,000.

Managers try to keep only the cash they need. Too much cash earns little; too little raises the risk of not paying bills. Analysts watch liquidity with the current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) and the stricter acid-test (quick) ratio, which counts only cash and cash equivalents, short-term investments, and accounts receivable.

Bank reconciliation

Because all receipts are deposited and all payments go through the bank, the bank statement is an independent record of cash. A bank reconciliation compares it with the company's books each month and explains every difference. Differences come from two sources:

  • Timing differences: items one side has recorded and the other has not yet (deposits in transit, outstanding checks, service charges the company has not seen yet).
  • Errors by either the bank or the company.

Petty cash

Writing a check for every $12 delivery charge would waste time. A petty cash fund handles small payments. A custodian holds a fixed amount of cash and pays small bills, keeping a receipt for each one. At any moment, cash plus receipts should equal the fund.

  1. Establish the fund. Write a check to the custodian: debit Petty cash, credit Cash.
  2. Pay small bills. No journal entry is made when the custodian pays a bill.
  3. Replenish the fund. Write a check for the total of the receipts and debit the related expenses. Petty cash is debited only if the fund itself is being increased (or credited if decreased).

Common mistakes

  • Calling a six-month certificate of deposit a cash equivalent. Measure maturity from the date this company acquired it; it must be three months or less.
  • Recording journal entries for deposits in transit or outstanding checks. Those are bank-side items; the books already have them.
  • Debiting Petty cash when the fund is replenished. Debit the expenses; Petty cash changes only when the fund size changes.
  • Netting an overdraft against other cash under U.S. GAAP. Report it as a current liability.
  • Forgetting that a legally restricted compensating balance follows the classification of the related debt.

Practice with answers

Problem 1: Classify the items

Cardinal Creek Supply has: (a) $8,000 of currency; (b) a $50,000 Treasury bill bought 80 days before maturity; (c) a $40,000, five-month certificate of deposit bought this month; (d) $120,000 in a sinking fund for bonds due in six years; (e) $2,500 of customer checks dated next month. How much is cash and cash equivalents?

Show the answer

Cash and cash equivalents = (a) $8,000 + (b) $50,000 = $58,000. The CD is a short-term investment, the sinking fund is noncurrent restricted cash, and the postdated checks are receivables.

Problem 2: Bank reconciliation

Bank balance $18,400; book balance $17,935; deposits in transit $1,250; outstanding checks $2,180; service charge $25; NSF check $440. Find the corrected balance and prepare the entry.

Show the answer

Problem 3: Effective rate

A company borrows $2,000,000 at 7% and must keep a $200,000 compensating balance. What is the effective interest rate?

Show the answer

$140,000 ÷ ($2,000,000 − $200,000) = $140,000 ÷ $1,800,000 = 7.78%.

Key terms

Cash
Currency, coins, checking balances, and items acceptable for deposit, available without restriction.
Cash equivalent
A short-term, highly liquid investment maturing within three months of the date it is acquired.
Internal control
A company's plan to encourage policy adherence, promote efficiency, minimize errors and theft, and make accounting data reliable.
Separation of duties
Keeping custody of assets, recordkeeping, and reconciliation in different hands.
Restricted cash
Cash not available for current operations because of management intent or a contract.
Compensating balance
A minimum balance a bank requires a borrower to keep, which raises the effective interest rate.
Bank reconciliation
A schedule that explains differences between the bank and book balances and finds the corrected cash balance.
Deposit in transit
A receipt recorded in the books but not yet credited by the bank.
Outstanding check
A check recorded in the books that has not yet cleared the bank.
NSF check
A customer check returned by the bank for nonsufficient funds.
Petty cash fund
A small fixed fund for minor payments, replenished from the checking account.
