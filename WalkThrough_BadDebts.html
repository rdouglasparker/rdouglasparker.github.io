Estimating Bad Debts

A guided walkthrough of two receivables problems: reconstructing an accounts receivable balance, and comparing the three ways to estimate uncollectible accounts.

Problem 1 — Raintree Cosmetic Company

2023 year-end balance sheet: Receivables, net of a $40,000 allowance = $482,000 → gross AR = $522,000.

2024 activity: credit sales $1,800,000  ·  collections $1,880,000  ·  write-offs $45,000  ·  recovery of a 2023 write-off $4,000.

Year-end aging (by % of receivables in each bucket):

STEP 1

Write-offs and the recovered account

Writing off a specific customer's account under the allowance method never touches Bad Debt Expense — the expense was already estimated and booked earlier. A write-off just admits that a specific piece of the estimate has become real: it moves the loss out of the Allowance account and off Accounts Receivable.

A recovery is a write-off run in reverse, in two steps: first reinstate the receivable (undo the write-off), then record its collection like any normal cash receipt.

STEP 3

Comparing three ways to estimate bad debts

All three methods land on the same Allowance account, but they answer different questions — and that changes the math.

(a) 2% of credit sales — an income-statement approach

This method asks: "of what we sold this year, how much won't we collect?" It ignores the existing Allowance balance entirely and just adds this year's estimate on top.

Where 36,000 comes from: 2% × $1,800,000 credit sales.

(b) 10% of ending AR — a balance-sheet approach

This method flips the question: "what should the Allowance balance itself be at year end?" That means the existing balance matters a lot — you're not adding 10%, you're adjusting to 10%.

Trace the Allowance T-account first: it began at $40,000 credit, gained $4,000 from the recovery, then lost $45,000 to the write-off — landing at a $1,000 debit balance before any adjustment. The target is 10% × $397,000 = $39,700 credit. Getting there takes a $40,700 swing.

Problem 2 — Swathmore Clothing Corporation

30-day credit terms. A monthly accrual of 3% of that month's credit sales is booked all year; at year-end, an aging schedule true-ups the balance.

STEP 1

The monthly accrual, summarized for the year

Rather than wait for year-end, Swathmore books an estimate every single month — 3% of that month's credit sales — so Bad Debt Expense tracks revenue as it's earned rather than arriving in one lump at year-end. Add up all twelve months and it's mathematically identical to 3% of the full year's credit sales.

Combine that running accrual with the year's write-offs into one summary entry — the accrual side credits the Allowance, the write-off side debits it and clears the specific receivables.
