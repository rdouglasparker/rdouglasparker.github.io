Inventory Systems & Cost of Purchases

Reading Notes — Perpetual vs. Periodic Systems, Goods to Include, and Purchase Discounts

LO8-1

Perpetual vs. Periodic Inventory Systems

Every company needs a way to keep track of how much inventory it has and what it cost. There are two systems for doing this, and they differ in when the Inventory account gets updated.

Perpetual system: the Inventory account is updated continuously — every purchase, sale, and return immediately changes the balance. Cost of Goods Sold is also recorded at the time of each sale.
Periodic system: the Inventory account is not updated as transactions occur. Instead, a physical count at the end of the period determines the ending balance, and Cost of Goods Sold is calculated using a formula — it's never directly tracked transaction by transaction.

That formula is one of the most useful things to memorize in this chapter:

Beginning inventory + Net purchases − Ending inventory = Cost of goods sold

Net purchases means purchases plus freight-in, minus purchase returns and purchase discounts — more on that in the next section.

Side-by-side comparison

EventPerpetual SystemPeriodic System
Purchase inventoryDebit InventoryDebit Purchases
Sell inventoryRecord revenue and a separate entry debiting Cost of Goods Sold, crediting InventoryRecord revenue only — no entry to cost of goods sold at the time of sale
Return goods to supplierCredit InventoryCredit Purchase Returns and Allowances
Pay freight on a purchaseDebit InventoryDebit Freight-In

Because perpetual systems give managers up-to-the-minute inventory data, nearly all major companies use a perpetual system today — but companies still take a physical count periodically (even under perpetual) to catch theft, damage, or recordkeeping errors.

Example 1

A company's books show a debit to Cost of Goods Sold every time a sale is recorded. Is this company using a perpetual or periodic system?

Perpetual. A periodic system never touches Cost of Goods Sold until the year-end adjusting entry — seeing it recorded sale-by-sale is the signature of a perpetual system.
Example 2

A retailer begins the year with $80,000 of inventory. During the year it purchases $410,000 of additional inventory (net of returns and discounts, including freight). A physical count at year-end shows $95,000 of inventory on hand. What is cost of goods sold for the year?

$80,000 + $410,000 − $95,000 = $395,000. This is exactly the periodic-system formula — notice that no individual sale transactions were needed to get this answer.
LO8-2

Which Goods Belong in Ending Inventory?

It seems simple — inventory is whatever is sitting in the warehouse. But ownership (not physical location) is what actually determines whether goods belong in your inventory. Three situations trip people up.

Goods in transit: shipping terms decide ownership

F.O.B. shipping point: title passes to the buyer the moment the seller hands the goods to the carrier. The buyer includes the goods in inventory as soon as they're shipped — even though they haven't arrived yet.
F.O.B. destination: title doesn't pass until the goods arrive at the buyer's location. The seller keeps the goods in its own inventory the whole time they're in transit.

Goods on consignment

When a company (the consignor) ships goods to another company (the consignee) to sell on its behalf, the consignor keeps legal title — and keeps the goods in its own inventory — until the consignee actually sells them to a third party. The consignee never includes consigned goods in its own inventory, even though the goods are sitting on its shelves.

Goods expected to be returned

When a seller expects some units to come back from customers under a right of return, it must include the cost of those expected returns in its own ending inventory (with an offsetting reduction to cost of goods sold) — even though those units are, for the moment, sitting in the customer's hands.

Example 1

On December 29, Ridgeline Supply ships goods to a customer, terms F.O.B. shipping point. The goods arrive at the customer's warehouse on January 3. Both companies have a December 31 year-end. Who includes these goods in their December 31 inventory?

The customer (buyer). Under F.O.B. shipping point, title transferred the moment the goods left Ridgeline's dock on December 29 — before year-end — so Ridgeline should not count them, even though it's the one holding a "cost of goods sold" position and no longer has the goods physically on hand.
Example 2

Alarka Outfitters holds $18,000 of inventory in its retail store on consignment from a supplier, Panthertown Wholesale. Alarka's own physical count (which included these goods) came to $142,000. What amount should Alarka actually report as its own ending inventory?

$142,000 − $18,000 = $124,000. The consigned goods belong to Panthertown (the consignor) until sold, so Alarka must remove them from its own inventory balance even though they were physically counted on its shelves.
LO8-3

Transactions Affecting Net Purchases

The cost of inventory includes everything necessary to get it to its condition and location for sale — not just the invoice price.

Net purchases = Purchases + Freight-in − Purchase returns and allowances − Purchase discounts

Freight

If goods are shipped F.O.B. shipping point, the buyer pays the freight and includes it in the cost of inventory (Freight-In under periodic, a direct debit to Inventory under perpetual). If F.O.B. destination, the seller pays it, and it's a selling expense to the seller — not part of the buyer's inventory cost at all.

Purchase discounts: gross method vs. net method

Purchase terms like "2/10, n/30" mean a 2% discount is available if paid within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days. There are two ways to record this:

Gross MethodNet Method
At purchaseRecord at the full (gross) invoice priceRecord already net of the discount, assuming it will be taken
Paid within discount periodReduce Inventory/Purchases for the discount at the time of paymentNo adjustment needed — it was already recorded net
Paid after discount periodJust pay the full amount — nothing extra to recordRecord Purchase Discounts Lost for the discount that was assumed but not earned

Under the net method, most companies expect to pay within the discount period, so "Purchase Discounts Lost" is meant to be the exception, not the rule — which is also why it's a useful account for managers to monitor (a large balance suggests the company isn't managing its cash and payables well).

Example 1 — Gross Method

A company purchases $30,000 of inventory, terms 2/10, n/30, using a perpetual system and the gross method. It pays the full $30,000 within the discount period.

At purchase:
Inventory30,000
Accounts payable30,000
At payment (within discount period):
Accounts payable30,000
Inventory (30,000 × 2%)600
Cash29,400
Example 2 — Net Method

Same facts as Example 1, but the company uses the net method, and this time pays after the discount period has passed.

At purchase (recorded net of the 2% discount):
Inventory (30,000 × 98%)29,400
Accounts payable29,400
At payment (after the discount period — discount is lost):
Accounts payable29,400
Purchase discounts lost600
Cash30,000

A seller records the mirror image of all of this on its own books — Sales Revenue and Accounts Receivable instead of Purchases/Inventory and Accounts Payable, and Sales Discounts (or Sales Discounts Forfeited, if the discount period lapses) instead of Purchase Discounts (or Purchase Discounts Lost).
