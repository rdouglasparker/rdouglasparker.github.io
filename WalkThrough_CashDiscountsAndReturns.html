Cash Discounts & Sales Returns

A guided walkthrough of two receivables scenarios: recognizing revenue under different collection timings — compared across the gross and net methods — and estimating a refund liability for expected returns.

How this works: Each transaction is taught like a mini-lecture — first the reasoning, then the completed entry. Read the reasoning and try to picture the entry yourself before you click "Reveal the entry." That struggle is where the learning happens.

Problem 1 — Harwell Company

Harwell Company manufactures tires. On July 15, 2024, it sold 1,200 tires to Nixon Car Company at $65 each.

Terms: 3/15, n/30  ·  Method: gross method for cash discounts  ·  Ignore cost of goods sold.

Sale amount = 1,200 × $65 = $78,000

STEP 1

Recording the sale on July 15

Under the gross method, Harwell records the sale at its full invoice price — $78,000 — and doesn't worry yet about whether Nixon will actually take the discount. The discount only gets recorded if and when Nixon pays early. So on the date of sale, this looks like any ordinary credit sale.

STEP 2 · PART 1

Collection on July 23 — inside the discount window

The "3/15" in 3/15, n/30 means Nixon gets a 3% discount if it pays within 15 days of the invoice date. July 23 is only 8 days after July 15 — Nixon is inside the window, so it earns the discount.

Discount = 3% × $78,000 = $2,340. Cash received = $78,000 − $2,340 = $75,660. The full $78,000 receivable still has to come off the books, so the $2,340 gap goes to a Sales Discounts account — a contra-revenue account that reduces net sales.

STEP 2 · PART 2

Alternative: collection on August 15 — outside the discount window

Now suppose Nixon waits until August 15 instead — 31 days after the sale. The 15-day discount window closed on July 30, so Nixon has forfeited the discount. Nixon owes, and pays, the full $78,000.

Notice there's no Sales Discounts account anywhere in this version. Whether a discount ever appears in the books depends entirely on the customer's actual payment date — that's the defining feature of the gross method.

Notice the pattern Both collection scenarios start from the exact same July 15 sale entry. The only thing that changes downstream is when Nixon pays. That's the whole logic of the gross method — the discount is never assumed; it only shows up if it's actually earned.

Problem 1B — Harwell Company, revisited: the net method

Same facts as Problem 1: 1,200 tires × $65 = $78,000, terms 3/15, n/30. This time Harwell uses the net method instead of the gross method — same numbers, a different assumption baked into the very first entry.

THE BIG IDEA

Gross method vs. net method: a different starting assumption

The gross method assumes nothing — it records the full invoice price and only acknowledges a discount if the customer actually takes it. The net method assumes the opposite: that a rational customer will almost always take a 3% discount to avoid paying 3% more, so the sale is recorded at the discounted amount right from the start.

That flips the whole problem around. Under the net method, taking the discount is the "nothing special happened" case, and it's missing the discount that requires an extra entry — because now Harwell has to record revenue it didn't originally expect to earn.

STEP 1

Recording the sale on July 15 — at the net amount

Instead of $78,000, the sale goes on the books at what Harwell expects to actually collect: $78,000 less the 3% discount, or $75,660. Compare this side-by-side with Problem 1, Step 1 — same sale, $2,340 less revenue recognized on day one.

STEP 2 · PART 1

Collection on July 23 — the discount is taken (the expected case)

Nixon pays within the window, exactly as the net method assumed it would. Since the receivable was already sitting on the books at the discounted amount, collecting it is now completely uneventful — Nixon pays $75,660, and $75,660 comes off Accounts Receivable. No discount account appears anywhere, because none was ever "owed" in the net method's eyes.

STEP 2 · PART 2

Alternative: collection on August 15 — the discount is forfeited

Here's where the net method has to do extra work. Nixon misses the window and owes the full $78,000 — but Harwell's books only show a $75,660 receivable. The $2,340 gap has to be recognized as revenue Harwell wasn't originally expecting: it goes to an account often called Sales Discounts Forfeited (or Interest Revenue), and it's additional income, not a correction of the original sale.

The real difference isn't the ending number — it's the timing and the label Both methods land on exactly the same cash collected and the same total revenue once the dust settles. What differs is when the $2,340 shows up and what it's called: under the gross method, it's a reduction to revenue that only appears if the discount is taken; under the net method, it's a boost to revenue that only appears if the discount is missed. The gross method assumes the discount won't be taken until proven otherwise; the net method assumes it will be.

Problem 2 — Halifax Manufacturing

Halifax Manufacturing lets customers return merchandise for credit. All sales are on account.

Beginning (1/1/24) refund liability: $320,000  ·  2024 sales on account: $11,700,000  ·  COGS = 75% of selling price.

2024 returns: $460,000 total — $255,000 from pre-2024 sales, the rest ($205,000) from 2024 sales.

Estimated returns = 4% of sales, recorded as a year-end adjusting entry.

THE BIG IDEA

Why a "refund liability" even exists

Under ASC 606, a company can't recognize revenue it doesn't expect to keep. If Halifax knows a slice of this year's sales will come back for credit, it sets up a refund liability — its best estimate of what it will owe back to customers — rather than waiting for the returns to actually happen.

That liability gets drawn down as actual returns occur, and then topped back up to the correct estimate at year-end. That's exactly the three-part structure this problem walks through.

STEP 1

(a) Actual 2024 returns of merchandise sold before 2024

These $255,000 in returns were already anticipated — they're exactly what the beginning $320,000 refund liability was sitting there to cover. So when the return actually happens, Halifax simply draws down the liability it already had. No new expense, no hit to this year's revenue — that already happened (or didn't) in a prior year.

Because the goods are coming back into inventory, the offsetting piece is inventory-side: Halifax had also set up an "Estimated Inventory Returns" asset when it built the original liability (at 75% of the sales-price estimate). The returned goods move out of that asset and into regular inventory.

STEP 2

(b) Actual 2024 returns of merchandise sold during 2024

This is different. The $320,000 beginning liability had nothing to do with 2024's own sales — and the adjusting entry that will estimate returns on 2024 sales hasn't happened yet (that's Step 3). So these $205,000 in returns aren't drawing down any liability yet; they're a direct reversal of this year's revenue and receivable.

STEP 3

(c) Adjusting the refund liability to its year-end balance

Now for the estimate. Halifax expects 4% of $11,700,000 = $468,000 in total returns related to this year's sales — that's the balance the refund liability needs to show on the year-end balance sheet.

But the account doesn't start the adjustment at zero. Walk the T-account: it began the year at $320,000, and Step 1 already drew it down by $255,000, leaving $65,000. To get from $65,000 up to the required $468,000, Halifax needs one more credit of $403,000.
