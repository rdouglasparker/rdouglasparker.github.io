Key idea: A note receivable is recorded at its present value, so any interest hidden in its face amount is recognized over time. When a company uses receivables to raise cash, the key question is whether it borrowed against them (they stay on the books as collateral) or sold them (they leave the books, and any fee is a loss).

Assigning vs. Factoring Receivables

The company used in Problems 9–12

At the end of June 2026, Blue Ridge Paper Supply has accounts receivable of $880,000. It needs about $540,000 in cash and is considering two offers from its bank on July 1:

  • Alternative A (assignment): Borrow $540,000 on a note, assigning the entire $880,000 of receivables as collateral. At the end of each month, remit collections to the bank along with 12% annual interest on the note's beginning-of-month balance.
  • Alternative B (factoring): Transfer $600,000 of specific receivables to the bank without recourse for a 3.5% fee; the sale criteria are met.
  • By July 31, 80% of the end-of-June receivables are collected.
