Accounts Receivable: Discounts and Returns

How a company records a credit sale when customers may take a cash discount or send goods back, and why both reduce the receivable and the revenue.

A question to start

Nantahala Paddle Works sells a $30,000 order of kayaks on credit. Should it record $30,000 of revenue and a $30,000 receivable? Maybe not. If the customer is likely to take a 2% discount for paying early, or likely to send some kayaks back, the company does not really expect to receive $30,000.

Receivables are recorded at the amount of consideration the seller expects to be entitled to receive. That links these notes directly to revenue recognition: discounts and returns are variable consideration, and they change both revenue and the receivable.

Receivables basics

A receivable is a claim to collect cash, other assets, or services in the future. One company's receivable is another's payable.

  • Accounts receivable (trade receivables) come from selling goods or services on account. They are informal arrangements supported by an invoice and are usually due in 30 to 60 days.
  • Nontrade receivables include tax refund claims, interest receivable, and advances to employees.
  • A receivable backed by a formal promissory note is a note receivable, covered in Detailed Notes 4.

Accounts receivable are recognized when revenue is recognized, usually when goods or services are delivered. They are almost always current assets, because they are collected within one year (or the operating cycle, if longer).

Why short-term receivables ignore present value

A credit sale is partly a loan to the customer, but when payment is due within a year the financing component is not significant. Sellers therefore record short-term receivables at the full amount expected, not at present value. Long-term receivables are different; see the notes receivable file.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 606-10-32-18 offers a practical expedient: a seller need not adjust for a significant financing component when the time between transfer of goods and payment is one year or less.
  • ASC 310-10 (Receivables) covers presentation and disclosure of receivables.

Trade discounts

A trade discount is a reduction from a list price, used for quantity buyers, preferred customers, or to keep real prices out of a printed catalog. It is not variable consideration, because the price does not depend on a future event. The sale is simply recorded at the discounted price.

Nantahala lists a touring kayak at $3,200 and gives an outfitter a 15% trade discount. The sale and receivable are recorded at $2,720 ($3,200 × 85%). The $480 never appears in the accounts.

Cash (sales) discounts: gross and net methods

A cash discount (sales discount) rewards a customer for paying quickly. Terms of 2/10, n/30 mean a 2% discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise the full amount within 30 days. Because the amount collected depends on a future event, a cash discount is variable consideration. In practice, sellers use one of two simplified methods.

  • Gross method: record the sale at the full price. When a customer takes the discount, debit Sales discounts, a contra revenue account.
  • Net method: record the sale net of the discount. When a customer pays late, credit Sales discounts forfeited, which is added to revenue.

Why the net method is conceptually better

Skipping a 2/10, n/30 discount means paying $100 instead of $98 twenty days later. That is a 2.04% return for 20 days, or about 37.2% a year ($2 ÷ $98 × 365 ÷ 20). Few sensible customers pass that up, so the seller expects to receive the net amount, which is what the net method records. Still, both methods are used, and the difference is usually immaterial.

Key idea

Under either method, net sales are reduced only by discounts customers actually take. The methods differ only in where the bookkeeping starts.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 606-10-32-5 through ASC 606-10-32-9 explain variable consideration, including discounts, and how to estimate it (expected value or most likely amount).

Sales returns

Customers often have a right to return goods. Returns are variable consideration too. If a company recorded returns only when they happened, it would overstate revenue and profit in the year of sale and understate them in the year of return. So revenue is reduced for returns that have happened and for returns still expected.

  1. Record actual returns as they occur. Debit Sales returns (contra revenue) and credit Cash or Accounts receivable. Put the goods back into Inventory and reduce Cost of goods sold.
  2. At year-end, estimate returns still to come from this year's sales. Debit Sales returns and credit a Refund liability. Record the goods expected back as an asset, Inventory—estimated returns, and reduce Cost of goods sold.
  3. Next year, record returns against the liability. If the estimate turns out wrong, adjust it in the year you learn the new information. That is a change in estimate, not a restatement.

Points worth noticing

  • A refund liability is recorded even if the original sale was on credit and the receivable is still unpaid. Depending on the contract, some sellers present receivables net of the refund liability.
  • Estimates rely on history adjusted for changes in customers, payment terms, and the economy. Many retailers end their fiscal year in late January, after most holiday returns are known.
  • If returns are hard to estimate, the company recognizes less revenue (a larger estimate of returns) so revenue is unlikely to reverse later.
  • Price reductions to customers who keep goods they wanted to return (sales allowances) are handled the same way.

In the FASB Codification

  • ASC 606-10-55-22 through ASC 606-10-55-29 cover sales with a right of return: recognize revenue for goods not expected back, a refund liability, and an asset for the right to recover goods.
  • ASC 606-10-32-11 constrains variable consideration to amounts that are probable not to result in a significant revenue reversal.
  • ASC 606-10-32-10 requires a refund liability for consideration the seller expects to refund.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a trade discount as a sales discount. Trade discounts just set the price; nothing is recorded for them.
  • Using Sales discounts under the net method, or Sales discounts forfeited under the gross method. Each method uses only one of the two accounts.
  • Recording only the returns that have happened. Year-end needs an estimate of returns still to come from this year's sales.
  • Putting estimated returned goods back into regular Inventory. Use Inventory—estimated returns until the goods actually come back.
  • Restating last year when the returns estimate turns out wrong. Adjust in the current year.

Key terms

Accounts receivable
Amounts owed by customers for goods or services sold on account; also called trade receivables.
Nontrade receivable
A receivable from something other than sales to customers, such as a tax refund or an employee advance.
Trade discount
A reduction from list price that sets the transaction price; not variable consideration.
Cash (sales) discount
A reduction offered for paying within a short period, such as 2/10, n/30.
Gross method
Records sales at full price and recognizes sales discounts when taken.
Net method
Records sales net of the discount and recognizes sales discounts forfeited when customers pay late.
Sales returns
A contra revenue account for actual and expected returns and allowances.
Refund liability
The amount the seller expects to refund for future returns of current sales.
Inventory—estimated returns
An asset for the cost of goods the seller expects customers to return.
