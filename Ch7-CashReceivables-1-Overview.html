Why this unit matters

Cash and receivables are listed first on the balance sheet because they are the closest thing a company has to money. But "how much cash do we have?" and "how much will customers actually pay us?" both take judgment. A receivable is only a promise, and this unit is about reporting how much of that promise a company really expects to collect.

The same ideas show up everywhere in practice: auditors test cash controls and bad-debt estimates, lenders watch receivables as collateral, and analysts use receivables to spot companies that are pumping up sales.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain internal control over cash and the role of separation of duties.
  • Decide what counts as cash and cash equivalents, and classify restricted cash and compensating balances.
  • Prepare a bank reconciliation and petty cash entries.
  • Record cash discounts under the gross and net methods and account for sales returns with a refund liability.
  • Estimate bad debts under the CECL model with an aging schedule or a percent of sales, and record write-offs and recoveries.
  • Account for interest-bearing and noninterest-bearing notes, including the effective interest method for long-term notes.
  • Decide whether a transfer of receivables is a sale or a secured borrowing, and record factoring, assigning, and discounting.
  • Compute and interpret receivables turnover and the average collection period.

The big idea

Receivables are measured at the cash the company expects to collect. Every topic in the unit adjusts one link in this chain:

  1. Amount the seller is entitled to. The sale price, reduced for discounts customers are expected to take and goods they are expected to return (variable consideration).
  2. Less: time value of money. For long-term notes, revenue is the present value; the rest is interest earned over time.
  3. Less: expected credit losses. An allowance for uncollectible accounts, estimated under CECL.
  4. Equals: net receivables, the cash the company expects to collect. If the company sells the receivables and gives up control, they leave the balance sheet entirely.

One sentence to remember

Cash is about availability (can we use it now?); receivables are about collectibility (how much will we really get, and when?).

Check yourself

Question 1

Why does writing off a specific account receivable not affect net income under the allowance method?

Show the answer

The expense was recognized earlier when the allowance was estimated. The write-off reduces both receivables and the allowance by the same amount, so net receivables and income do not change.

Question 2

A company sells receivables with recourse and meets all three surrender-of-control conditions. Sale or borrowing?

Show the answer

A sale. Recourse does not prevent sale treatment; the company records a recourse liability at fair value, which increases the loss.

Question 3

Is a one-year Treasury bill bought two months before maturity a cash equivalent?

Show the answer

Yes. The test is three months or less to maturity from the date this company acquired it.
