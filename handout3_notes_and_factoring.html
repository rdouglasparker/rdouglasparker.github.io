Problem 1 — Evergreen Company

Fiscal year-end: December 31. Ignore COGS entries for merchandise sales throughout.

A running set of receivables transactions from late February through the end of April, with year-end adjustments still to come.

FEB 28

An interest-bearing note at a market rate

Evergreen sells $18,000 of merchandise to Lennox and takes an 8%, 7-month note instead of cash. Since 8% is stated to be the appropriate market rate for a note like this, there's no hidden discount to unwind — the note's face amount already equals its present value. This is the simple case.

MAR 31

A noninterest-bearing note — imputing the real rate

Maddox's note has no stated interest, but that doesn't mean there's no interest — it just means the interest is buried inside the $13,000 face amount. The merchandise's fair value, $11,960, tells you what was actually sold today; the $1,040 gap is interest Evergreen will earn over the year until the note matures.

So the note goes on the books at its $13,000 face, revenue is recognized at the $11,960 it's really worth today, and a Discount on Notes Receivable — a contra-asset — soaks up the difference until it's earned.

APR 30 · PART 1

Factoring $67,000 of receivables without recourse

"Without recourse" and "sale criteria met" together mean this is a true sale — the receivables leave Evergreen's books entirely, and Evergreen is done with them. The bank isn't lending against them; it owns them now, risk and all. The 2% finance charge is the price of getting cash today instead of waiting for customers to pay — that's a loss, not an expense.

APR 30 · PART 2

Discounting the Lennox note at the bank

This is a two-step move. First, Evergreen owns two months of accrued (unpaid) interest on the Lennox note as of April 30 — that has to go on the books before anything else happens, because it's part of what's being sold.

Then the note itself (plus that accrued interest) is discounted to the bank at the bank's own 10% rate, applied over the remaining five months to maturity — not the original 7. The bank effectively buys the note's future maturity value at a discount; whatever cash Evergreen nets versus its carrying amount is a gain or loss.

SEP 30

Lennox pays the note — but not to Evergreen

This is the conceptual punchline of the whole problem. Evergreen sold this note without recourse back on April 30 — it's off Evergreen's books for good, gain or loss already recognized. When Lennox eventually pays, it pays the bank, which now owns the note. Evergreen has no journal entry at all.

No entry required — Evergreen no longer holds this receivable.
DEC 31 — YEAR-END ADJUSTMENTS

What's still outstanding needs one adjustment: the Maddox discount

By December 31, the Lennox note and the factored receivables are long gone from Evergreen's books — nothing to adjust there. Problem 2 — Lonergan Company

End of June 2024: Accounts Receivable = $960,000. Lonergan needs about $590,000 in cash and is weighing two bank offers on July 1.

Alternative A (assignment): borrow $590,000 on a note, pledging the entire $960,000 AR balance as collateral. Each month, remit collections plus 12% interest on the note's beginning balance.

Alternative B (factoring): transfer $640,000 of specific receivables to the bank without recourse; sale criteria met; 4% factoring fee.

THE BIG IDEA

Borrowing against receivables vs. selling them

These two alternatives look similar — both get Lonergan cash today using its receivables — but they're structurally opposite. In Alternative A, the receivables never leave Lonergan's balance sheet; they're just collateral for a loan. Lonergan still owns them, still collects them, and still owes the bank money. In Alternative B, the receivables are truly sold — they disappear from Lonergan's books, and with them, all further involvement.

JULY 1

Alternative A — signing the note

Since this is a loan, not a sale, the $960,000 of assigned receivables stays exactly where it is on the balance sheet (typically just disclosed as pledged, in the notes). The only new item is the debt itself.

JULY 31

80% collected — what happens under each alternative

This is where the two alternatives really diverge. Under Alternative A, Lonergan still owns all $960,000 of receivables, so 80% of the whole thing — $768,000 — flows through Lonergan's own Cash and AR accounts. Under Alternative B, only the $320,000 that wasn't factored still belongs to Lonergan; the $640,000 piece is the bank's problem now, and customers pay the bank directly with no entry on Lonergan's books at all.

Alternative A: collection, then remittance to the bank

Lonergan first collects the $768,000 like normal. Then it owes the bank interest for July — 12% annual, one month, on the note's beginning balance of $590,000 — plus, since collections comfortably exceed what's owed, it can retire the note entirely in this one remittance. Whatever's left over after paying off principal and interest stays with Lonergan; the assignment only ever secured the bank up to what it was owed.
