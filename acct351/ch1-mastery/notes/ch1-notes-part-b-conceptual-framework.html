1. Purpose of the conceptual framework

The conceptual framework is often called an "accounting constitution." It is a coherent system of interrelated objectives and fundamentals intended to lead to consistent standards and to prescribe the nature, function, and limits of financial accounting and reporting. It does not directly prescribe GAAP.

The FASB communicates the framework through Statements of Financial Accounting Concepts (SFACs):

Concepts statements you will hear referenced
StatementTopic
SFAC 8Objective of financial reporting, qualitative characteristics — and now the chapters on elements, recognition, measurement, presentation, and notes
SFAC 7Using cash flow information and present value in accounting measurements
SFAC 6 (historical)Defines the 10 elements of financial statements (now superseded by SFAC 8, Chapter 4)
SFAC 5 (historical)Recognition and measurement concepts (now superseded by SFAC 8, Chapters 5 and 6)
Not authoritative In U.S. GAAP, the framework guides the FASB when it develops standards. It is not part of the Codification, and a company may not use it to override a specific standard.

2. The objective of financial reporting

Objective Provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to present and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors (capital providers) in making decisions about providing resources to the entity — buying, selling, or holding equity or debt, and providing or settling loans.

Capital providers care about the amount, timing, and uncertainty of future cash flows. Information about the company's economic resources (assets) and claims against them (liabilities) also helps users identify financial strengths and weaknesses and assess liquidity and solvency.

3. Qualitative characteristics

The framework arranges the qualities that make information decision-useful in a hierarchy.

Fundamental characteristic #1: Relevance

Information is relevant if it can make a difference in a decision. It has:

  • Predictive value — helps users predict future outcomes (e.g., this year's net income helps forecast future cash flows).
  • Confirmatory value — helps users confirm or change prior expectations.

Predictive and confirmatory value sit at the heart of earnings quality — the ability of reported earnings to predict future earnings.

Materiality is a company-specific aspect of relevance. Information is material if including or correcting it would probably affect a user's judgment. It depends on:

  • Magnitude — $15,000 of anticipated bad debts is immaterial for a multibillion-dollar retailer but could be very material for a single campus coffee shop.
  • Nature — a small bribe to a foreign official, or a small amount that turns a net loss into net income, can be material for qualitative reasons.

The FASB has avoided bright-line materiality thresholds; materiality is left to the judgment of preparers and auditors.

Fundamental characteristic #2: Faithful representation

Information is faithfully represented when there is agreement between a measure or description and the real-world phenomenon it purports to represent. (If "inventory" included accounts receivable, it would not be faithfully represented.) It requires information to be:

  • Complete — includes everything necessary to depict the phenomenon.
  • Neutral — free from bias; standards should not be designed to favor particular companies, groups, or outcomes.
  • Free from error — no errors or omissions in the amount or process. An estimate can be faithfully represented if it is clearly described as an estimate and users understand its potential inaccuracy.
What about conservatism? Conservatism means requiring more verification before recognizing good news than bad news, so losses hit income sooner than gains and net assets tend to be biased downward. The framework explicitly rejects conservatism because it conflicts with neutrality. Even so, it persists in practice — e.g., companies write down inventory and long-lived assets for declines in value but generally may not write them up. Justifications include reduced litigation risk and earlier triggering of debt covenants that protect creditors.

The term objectivity is often linked with verifiability: historical cost is objective; fair value estimates can be subjective.

4. The cost constraint

The cost effectiveness constraint says the benefits of providing information must exceed its costs. Costs include gathering, processing, and disseminating information; users' interpretation costs; and adverse economic consequences. Example: segment disclosures (ASC 280) impose competitive disadvantage costs because rivals can see disaggregated data. Benefits — better resource allocation — are real but impossible to quantify precisely, which is one reason the FASB's due process seeks such broad input.

Together with materiality, the cost constraint explains practical choices like expensing a $90 stapler that will last years instead of capitalizing and depreciating it.

5. Elements of financial statements

The elements are the building blocks with which financial statements are constructed. There are 10:

The 10 elements (traditional definitions)
ElementDefinition
AssetsProbable future economic benefits obtained or controlled by a particular entity as a result of past transactions or events
LiabilitiesProbable future sacrifices of economic benefits arising from present obligations to transfer assets or provide services to other entities in the future as a result of past transactions or events
Equity (net assets)The residual interest in assets after deducting liabilities (shareholders' equity for a corporation)
Investments by ownersIncreases in equity from transfers of something of value to the entity to obtain or increase ownership interests
Distributions to ownersDecreases in equity from transfers to owners (e.g., dividends)
Comprehensive incomeThe change in equity during a period from nonowner sources — all changes except investments by and distributions to owners
RevenuesInflows or other enhancements of assets, or settlements of liabilities, from delivering goods, rendering services, or other ongoing major or central operations
ExpensesOutflows or other using up of assets, or incurrences of liabilities, from delivering goods, rendering services, or other ongoing major or central operations
GainsIncreases in equity from peripheral or incidental transactions
LossesDecreases in equity from peripheral or incidental transactions
Sort them Balance sheet (a point in time): assets, liabilities, equity. Changes during a period: revenues, expenses, gains, losses (which together make up comprehensive income), plus investments by and distributions to owners.
Revenue vs. gain? Ask whether it comes from central operations (revenue) or a peripheral transaction (gain). Selling a used delivery van for more than its book value is a gain for a bakery; it would be revenue for a car dealer.
Current development In December 2021 the FASB finalized Chapter 4 of Concepts Statement 8, which updates these definitions. An asset is now "a present right of an entity to an economic benefit," and a liability is "a present obligation of an entity to transfer an economic benefit." The word "probable" is gone. Revenues and expenses are no longer tied to "ongoing major or central operations," and gains and losses are no longer tied to "peripheral or incidental" transactions. The FASB completed the remaining framework chapters — recognition and derecognition, and measurement — by 2024. Existing standards in the Codification were not changed by these chapters.

6. The four underlying assumptions

Assumptions underlying GAAP
AssumptionDescriptionImplication / example
Economic entityAll economic events can be identified with a particular economic entityThe owner's personal residence is not an asset of her sole proprietorship. A parent and its controlled subsidiaries (>50% voting stock) are separate legal entities but one accounting entity — consolidated statements.
Going concernAbsent information to the contrary, the entity will continue to operate indefinitelyJustifies historical cost and depreciating a building over 40 years. If liquidation were imminent, assets and liabilities would be measured at liquidation values.
PeriodicityThe life of a company can be divided into artificial time periodsSupports timeliness: quarterly and annual SEC reports. Companies often choose a natural business year ending at the low point of activity — many retailers end their fiscal year around January 31.
Monetary unitElements are measured in nominal units of money, without adjustment for changes in purchasing powerU.S. dollar in the United States, euro in the EU. Inflation strains this assumption because dollars spent in different years have different purchasing power.

7. Recognition: revenues and expenses

Recognition is the process of admitting information into the basic financial statements. Measurement associates numerical amounts with the elements. Disclosure includes pertinent information that is not recognized in the statements themselves.

General recognition criteria

Subject to the cost constraint and materiality, an item is recognized when it:

  1. Meets the definition of an element.
  2. Has a relevant attribute that is measurable with sufficient reliability.
  3. Is relevant — capable of making a difference in decisions.
  4. Is reliable — representationally faithful, verifiable, and neutral.

Revenue recognition

Historically, the realization principle required (1) an earnings process that is complete or virtually complete and (2) reasonable certainty of collection. Today, under ASC 606, companies recognize revenue when goods or services are transferred to customers, in the amount the company expects to be entitled to receive.

  • Most sales of goods → at a point in time (usually delivery).
  • Services like renting apartments or lending money → over time as the service is provided.
  • No revenue if collection of the amount the seller is entitled to is not probable.

Revenue recognition does not depend on when cash is received — that is the accrual model at work.

Expense recognition — four approaches

Expenses are recognized, where possible, by matching them with revenues from the same transactions or events. Because many costs relate only indirectly to revenue, four approaches are used:

Four ways to recognize expenses
ApproachExample
1. Exact cause-and-effect relationshipCost of goods sold; sales commissions
2. Associate with revenues of a specific time periodMonthly salary of an office administrator
3. Systematic and rational allocation over periods benefitedStraight-line depreciation of equipment; amortization
4. In the period incurred, without regard to revenueAdvertising — it is impossible to tell when, whether, or how much revenue results

Note: the conceptual framework does not formally use the term "matching principle." Standard setters worry that applying matching too broadly would put amounts on the balance sheet that don't meet the definition of an asset.

8. Measurement attributes and fair value

GAAP uses a mixed-attribute model: the attribute chosen for an item should maximize relevance and faithful representation. Five attributes:

Measurement attributes
AttributeMeaningTypical use
Historical costValue given (asset) or received (liability) in the original exchange; later adjusted for depreciation/amortizationLand, equipment (depreciated cost)
Net realizable value (NRV)Estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less reasonably predictable costs of completion, disposal, and transportationInventory at lower of cost or NRV; receivables
Current costCost to acquire the same item todaySometimes reported in inflationary economies
Present valueDiscounted future cash flows (SFAC 7) — objective is to approximate fair valueLong-term notes, bonds, leases, pensions
Fair valuePrice received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement dateMany investments; impairment tests

NRV example — Tuck River Kayak Works

Unfinished kayaks could be sold for $14,500 after $1,800 of finishing work and $700 of shipping.

NRV = $14,500 − $1,800 − $700 = $12,000

Why historical cost? (1) It reflects actual cash paid or received, and (2) it results from an exchange between independent parties, so it is objective and highly verifiable.

Fair value measurement (ASC 820)

Fair value takes the perspective of market participants, not the specific company. (If a company buys a patent only to keep a rival from using it, it still values the patent at what a market participant would pay.)

Valuation techniques:

  • Market approach — market information (e.g., earnings × P/E multiples of similar companies).
  • Income approach — convert estimated future amounts to a single present value.
  • Cost approach — what it would cost to buy or build an asset of similar quality and condition.
Fair value hierarchy of inputs (Level 1 is most preferred)
LevelInputsExample
Level 1Quoted prices in active markets for identical assets or liabilitiesShares of stock traded on the NYSE or Nasdaq
Level 2Observable inputs other than Level 1 quoted prices — prices for similar items, or inputs derived from observable market dataA building acquired in a noncash exchange, valued from recent sales of similar buildings or price per square foot
Level 3Unobservable inputs reflecting the entity's own assumptions about what market participants would useAn asset retirement obligation valued with the company's own cash flow estimates

Companies must disclose the inputs used; for recurring Level 3 measurements they also disclose the effect on earnings.

For fair valueHighly relevant; historical cost may be stale for many decisions.
Against fair valueEstimates — especially Level 3 — may lack faithful representation and verifiability and may invite earnings manipulation.

The fair value option (ASC 825)

Companies may elect to report most financial assets and liabilities (investments, notes, bonds payable) at fair value, with changes in fair value reported as gains and losses in income. It reduces inconsistencies when related items are measured differently and offers an alternative to complex hedge accounting. It does not extend to nonfinancial assets such as land or buildings, and some financial items (e.g., pension obligations) are excluded.

9. Disclosure

The full-disclosure principle says financial reports should include any information that could affect the decisions of external users — subject to the cost constraint. Information can be disclosed through:

  1. Parenthetical or modifying comments on the face of the statements.
  2. Disclosure notes — accounting policies, contractual agreements, pending litigation, and more.
  3. Supplemental schedules and tables with more detail than the primary statements.

"Disclosure overload" is a common complaint. In 2018 the FASB added Chapter 8, Notes to Financial Statements, to Concepts Statement 8 — a set of questions the Board uses to decide which notes new standards should require.

10. Revenue/expense vs. asset/liability approach

Two approaches to standard setting
Revenue/expense approachAsset/liability approach
Starting pointPrinciples for recognizing revenues and expensesRecognize and measure the assets and liabilities that exist at the balance sheet date
Then…Record whatever assets and liabilities are needed to make the balance sheet reconcileRecognize revenues, expenses, gains, and losses as the changes in those assets and liabilities
Sales exampleHas the good or service been delivered? If so, record revenue and the related receivableDoes an enforceable receivable (a probable future benefit) exist? Record the revenue implied by that asset inflow
Matching?Yes — match expenses to revenuesNo — recognize expenses for the net assets used up

U.S. GAAP and IFRS have been moving toward the asset/liability approach (recent standards on revenue, investments, and income taxes), and with it, toward greater use of fair value. Both approaches often produce the same results, but the emphasis can change recognition and measurement in important ways.

11. U.S. GAAP vs. IFRS frameworks

IFRS comparison
  • Role of the framework. In U.S. GAAP, the framework mainly guides standard setters. Under IFRS it guides standard setters and gives practitioners a basis for judgments when no IFRS standard applies.
  • 2018 revised IFRS framework includes explicit definitions of materiality, reporting entity, and executory contracts, and explicit criteria for derecognition.
  • Asset and liability definitions. IFRS definitions do not depend on probability ("a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events"). The FASB's 2021 definitions moved in the same direction.
  • Organizations. Similar structures (SEC/Monitoring Board, FAF/IFRS Foundation, FASB/IASB), and both face political pressure.
