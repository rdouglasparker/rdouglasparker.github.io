1. What financial accounting does — and for whom

Think of accounting as a language: it is how a business communicates its economic activity to the people who make decisions about it. Financial accounting is the branch of accounting concerned with providing financial information to external users. (Managerial accounting, by contrast, serves internal users — managers.)

Key idea The primary focus of financial accounting is the information that profit-oriented companies provide to their present and potential investors and creditors.
Providers and external users of financial information
ProvidersExternal users
  • Profit-oriented companies
  • Not-for-profit entities (government units, charities, schools)
  • Households
  • Investors and creditors (banks, bondholders, other lenders)
  • Employees, labor unions, customers, suppliers
  • Government regulatory agencies (IRS, SEC)
  • Financial intermediaries — analysts, stockbrokers, mutual fund managers, credit-rating agencies — who advise or act for investors and creditors

How the information is delivered

The primary vehicle is the set of financial statements and related disclosure notes. Financial reporting is the process of providing this information to external users.

  1. Balance sheet (statement of financial position)
  2. Income statement (statement of operations)
  3. Statement of comprehensive income — either a separate statement immediately following the income statement or a single combined statement (FASB ASC 220)
  4. Statement of cash flows
  5. Statement of shareholders' equity

Users also get information from press releases, management forecasts, prospectuses, and SEC filings (Form 10-K annually, Form 10-Q for each of the first three quarters).

2. The investment-credit decision: a cash flow perspective

Our free-enterprise economy relies on the capital markets — the composite of all investors and creditors — to allocate resources to the companies that will use them best. Companies raise capital two ways:

  • From investors, in exchange for an ownership interest (shares of stock).
  • From creditors, by borrowing (individual loans or publicly traded debt such as bonds).

Investors and creditors provide capital only if they expect to get more cash back in the future. Two variables drive the decision:

  • Expected rate of return — the amount received over and above the investment.
  • Risk — the uncertainty (variability) of that return.
Rate of return = (Dividends + Share price appreciation) ÷ Initial investment

Example — Catamount Coffee Roasters

An investor buys Catamount Coffee Roasters stock for $8,000 at the start of the year, receives $240 in dividends during the year, and sells the shares at year-end for $8,560.

($240 + $560) ÷ $8,000 = 10%

Compare that to a government-insured savings account paying 4%: the 4% is nearly certain, while the 10% was uncertain going in. Investors accept more risk only for a higher expected return.

The objective A company can return cash to investors and creditors in the long run only if it generates a profit — cash receipts from selling goods and services that exceed the cash needed to provide them. So the objective of financial accounting is to give investors and creditors information that helps them assess the amounts, timing, and uncertainty of the company's future cash flows.

3. Cash-basis vs. accrual accounting

Predicting future cash flows is the goal — but, perhaps surprisingly, the model that does this best is accrual accounting, not cash-basis accounting.

Cash-basis accounting

Measures net operating cash flow: cash receipts minus cash payments from providing goods and services to customers during the period.

Problem: over short periods, cash flows are lumpy. A company may collect cash for prior sales or prepay costs for future periods.

Accrual accounting

Measures net income: revenues (resources provided by operations) minus expenses (resources sacrificed to produce revenues), whenever they occur — not just when cash moves.

Result: a smoother, more representative picture of performance and a better predictor of future cash flows.

Illustration — Balsam Ridge Landscaping (first three years)

  • Credit sales of $120,000 each year; cash collected $70,000, $140,000, $150,000 (nothing owed at end of year 3).
  • At the start of year 1, prepaid three years of equipment-yard rent: $45,000 ($15,000 per year).
  • Wages of $60,000 paid in full each year.
  • Fuel and utilities cost $12,000 per year; $4,000 of year 1's cost was paid in year 2.

What to notice
  • Over the whole life of the business, the two measures are equal ($99,000). They differ only in timing.
  • Year 1 cash flow (−$43,000) badly predicts years 2 and 3. Net income of $33,000 each year reflects the company's steady activity.
  • The gaps come from (a) collecting cash in a different pattern than sales occur and (b) paying for resources in different periods than they are used.

Cash-flow information is still valuable — that's why the statement of cash flows is a required statement. Accrual income plus cash flows gives the most complete view.

4. Who sets the rules? The development of GAAP

Generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) are a dynamic set of broad and specific guidelines that companies follow when measuring and reporting information in their financial statements and notes. Common rules make companies comparable.

Historical timeline

Milestones in U.S. standard setting
WhenEventWhy it matters
1929Stock market crashMisleading statements were blamed for inflated prices; pressure for uniform standards begins.
1933Securities ActAccounting and disclosure requirements for initial offerings of stocks and bonds.
1934Securities Exchange Act — creates the SECCovers secondary market trading; the SEC gets the authority to set accounting standards for public companies.
1938–1959Committee on Accounting Procedure (CAP) of the AIA (later AICPA)Issued 51 Accounting Research Bulletins; piecemeal, no theoretical framework.
1959–1973Accounting Principles Board (APB)Issued 31 Opinions. Part-time volunteers, no accepted framework, and perceived lack of independence (nearly all CPAs).
1973–presentFinancial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)Seven full-time, independent members from varied backgrounds; issues Accounting Standards Updates (ASUs).
Important The SEC has the authority to set standards for public companies but has delegated the task to the private sector (today, the FASB). The power still lies with the SEC — it can and has forced changes to private-sector standards.

The FASB's support structure

  • Financial Accounting Foundation (FAF) — selects FASB members, ensures funding (largely fees on securities issuers under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act), and exercises oversight. Also oversees and funds the GASB.
  • Financial Accounting Standards Advisory Council (FASAC) — advises the FASB on agenda priorities.
  • Emerging Issues Task Force (EITF) — formed in 1984 to resolve narrow implementation issues quickly; its consensuses are ratified by the FASB and become GAAP.
  • Private Company Council (PCC) — created in 2012; determines whether private companies need exceptions or modifications to GAAP. Its proposals must be endorsed by the FASB.
  • Governmental Accounting Standards Board (GASB) — created in 1984 to set standards for state and local governments.

The FASB's due process

Before issuing a substantive standard, the FASB follows an elaborate, public, information-gathering process:

  1. Identify a financial reporting issue (from stakeholder requests or other means).
  2. Decide whether to add a project to the technical agenda.
  3. Deliberate the issues at one or more public meetings.
  4. Issue an Exposure Draft (sometimes preceded by a Discussion Paper).
  5. Hold a public roundtable on the Exposure Draft, if needed.
  6. Staff analyzes comment letters and other input; the Board redeliberates in public.
  7. Issue an Accounting Standards Update amending the Codification.

5. The FASB Accounting Standards Codification

Before 2009, an accountant might have had to search ARBs, APB Opinions, FASB Statements, Interpretations, Staff Positions, EITF consensuses and SEC guidance to answer one question. The FASB Accounting Standards Codification (ASC), effective 2009, integrates all of it into one searchable online database (FASB ASC 105).

Key idea The Codification is the single source of authoritative nongovernmental U.S. GAAP (other than SEC rules and interpretive releases). A new ASU becomes authoritative when it is entered into the Codification. The conceptual framework is not authoritative GAAP.

6. International standard setting (IFRS)

Different national standards reduced comparability and made it harder for companies to raise capital internationally. The International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC) formed in 1973 and issued 41 International Accounting Standards (IASs). In 2001 it reorganized into the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), which issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). More than 120 jurisdictions — including the European Union, Canada, and Australia — require or permit IFRS.

The FASB and IASB have worked toward convergence on major standards (revenue recognition, leases, fair value measurement), though the SEC has not required U.S. public companies to adopt IFRS. Foreign companies listed in the U.S. may file using IFRS without reconciling to U.S. GAAP.

7. Standard setting as a political process

A new or changed standard can redistribute wealth: it can change reported profits, loan covenant compliance, executive bonuses, and stock prices. These are called economic consequences. Because the FASB's due process is public, affected groups lobby for treatments that serve their interests.

Classic examples

  • Employee stock options (1990s–2004). The FASB proposed expensing the fair value of options. Heavy lobbying, especially from high-tech companies, led the FASB to require only note disclosure. About a decade later, in a different political climate, the FASB required expense recognition as originally proposed.
  • Fair value during the 2008–2009 financial crisis. Critics argued that fair value write-downs in illiquid markets worsened the crisis. Pressure from lobbyists and Congress led the FASB to revise its guidance on recognizing investment losses.
  • IFRS in the EU (2008). The EU pressured the IASB to suspend due process and immediately permit reclassifications so European banks could avoid recognizing large losses.
Neutrality Standards are supposed to be neutral — designed to produce high-quality information, not to achieve particular economic or social outcomes or to favor particular groups. Political pressure makes neutrality difficult to maintain.

8. Encouraging high-quality financial reporting

Management and auditors

Management is responsible for preparing the financial statements and applying GAAP. Independent auditors examine the statements and express a professional opinion on whether they present fairly, in all material respects, the company's financial position, results of operations, and cash flows in conformity with GAAP. Audits add credibility.

  • Most companies receive an unmodified ("clean") opinion, partly because they correct material misstatements the auditor finds.
  • Public-company audit reports also describe critical audit matters (CAMs) — especially challenging, subjective, or complex areas — and give an opinion on internal control over financial reporting.
  • In most states only licensed CPAs can sign audit reports. Licensure requires education, the Uniform CPA Examination, and experience.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX)

Enacted after the collapses of Enron, WorldCom, and others (and the end of Arthur Andersen). Key provisions for public companies:

  • Created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to regulate auditors and the non-audit services they provide to audit clients.
  • Requires rotation of the lead audit partner every five years.
  • Increases accountability of corporate executives and adds stiff criminal penalties for violators.
  • Addresses conflicts of interest for securities analysts.
  • Section 404 — management must document and report on the adequacy of internal controls; the auditor must opine on whether effective internal control over financial reporting was maintained. (The most controversial and costly provision.)

9. Ethics in accounting

Ethics is a code or moral system that provides criteria for evaluating right and wrong. An ethical dilemma is a situation that tests that code. Pressure to meet short-term earnings targets can tempt managers to bend or break accounting rules — and technical competence alone is not enough to resist.

Professions accept responsibility for the interests of those they serve, often through a code of ethics: the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct, the IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice, and the Institute of Internal Auditors' Code of Ethics. Codes help, but the motivation to behave ethically must come from within.

A seven-step framework for analyzing ethical issues

  1. Determine the facts — who, what, where, when, how.
  2. Identify the ethical issue and the stakeholders — shareholders, creditors, management, employees, the community.
  3. Identify the values related to the situation (e.g., honesty, confidentiality vs. the right to know).
  4. Specify the alternative courses of action.
  5. Evaluate the alternatives for consistency with the values in step 3.
  6. Identify the consequences of each alternative, if step 5 does not settle the decision.
  7. Make your decision and take any indicated action.
Scenario to think about The controller of Pinnacle Peak Outdoor Supply spent $900,000 on a spring ad campaign in April and May. The CFO wants the cost moved from advertising expense to a "prepaid advertising" asset at quarter-end so the company shows a profit — which its bank loan agreement requires. GAAP requires advertising to be expensed as incurred because its future benefit can't be reliably linked to specific periods. Who are the stakeholders? What values are in conflict? What are the alternatives and their consequences?
