Part 1 — The quarter-end request

You are a staff accountant at Blue Ridge Paddle & Pack, an outdoor retailer with stores in Sylva, Bryson City, and Asheville. Its largest lender, Mountain Commerce Bank, requires quarterly statements and a loan covenant states that the company must report a profit every quarter to keep its line of credit in good standing.

During April and May of , the company spent $840,000 producing and airing a streaming and radio campaign for its summer line. All of it was charged to advertising expense. Before the adjustment below, the quarter ended June 30 shows a pre-tax loss of $310,000.

The CFO asks you to record a June 30 entry moving the cost to an asset called prepaid advertising, to be expensed in July. "The campaign drove sales in May and June and will keep bringing customers in through July," the CFO says. "That's matching. And if we show a loss, the bank will call the line."

Advertising is the classic example of the fourth expense recognition approach: because it is impossible to determine when, whether, or how much revenue results, it is expensed as incurred. The CFO's "matching" argument fails because the costs don't create a measurable future economic benefit that meets the definition of an asset.

$

−$310,000 + $840,000 removed from expense = $530,000 of income. The entry turns a loss into a substantial profit — which is exactly why it is material both quantitatively and qualitatively.

Reporting an asset that doesn't meet the definition of an asset, to produce a desired result, means the statements no longer agree with the economic phenomena they represent. The information is neither neutral nor free from error.

Materiality has qualitative dimensions. An adjustment that flips a loss to a profit — and determines whether a loan covenant is met — would probably affect a user's judgment regardless of size. The FASB has no bright-line threshold.

Part 2 — Work the ethics framework

Write at least 150 characters to unlock the model response.

Facts: $840,000 of advertising was properly expensed; the quarter shows a $310,000 loss; the CFO wants the costs reclassified as an asset so the company shows a profit and avoids a covenant violation.

Ethical issue: whether to knowingly misstate the financial statements to avoid a loan consequence. Stakeholders: Mountain Commerce Bank (relies on the statements to manage credit risk), shareholders, the CFO and management, you (professional responsibility and potential legal exposure), employees whose jobs depend on the company, the external auditors, and suppliers and other creditors.

Write at least 150 characters to unlock the model response.

Values: honesty and integrity, objectivity, and the bank's right to know versus loyalty to your employer and concern for employees if the line of credit is called.

Alternatives: (1) record the entry as requested; (2) refuse and explain the GAAP requirement to the CFO; (3) escalate to the controller, audit committee, or internal audit if pressure continues; (4) suggest the company approach the bank proactively about a covenant waiver or amendment, using accurate statements.

Write at least 150 characters to unlock the model response.

Recording the entry conflicts with honesty and objectivity and violates GAAP. Consequences could include misleading the bank, a later restatement, loss of credibility, and legal and professional sanctions for you and the CFO. It also only delays the problem — the cost hits July instead. Refusing, explaining the requirement, and recommending a candid conversation with the bank (seeking a waiver) is consistent with the values identified and protects the stakeholders who rely on the statements. If the CFO insists, escalate through the company's appropriate channels. Decision: do not record the entry.

Part 3 — A standard-setting controversy

Suppose the FASB issues an Exposure Draft requiring retailers to recognize a liability for gift cards sold and to measure their merchandise-return obligations at fair value using Level 3 inputs. The Mountain Retailers Coalition submits a comment letter arguing the proposal would "reduce reported profits for small retailers, raise borrowing costs, and hurt local economies," and asks members of Congress to pressure the FASB to drop the project.

The coalition focuses on how the standard would redistribute wealth — lower profits, higher borrowing costs — rather than on whether the resulting information would be more useful. That is an economic-consequences argument.

The SEC holds the authority to set standards for public companies under the 1934 Act and has delegated the task to the FASB. Congress can apply pressure (and could change the law), but the SEC is the body with authority over the FASB's standards.

Write at least 150 characters to unlock the model response.

The FASB would acknowledge the coalition's concerns and its participation in due process, but explain that standards must be neutral — designed to produce relevant, faithfully represented information rather than to achieve particular economic outcomes. Recognizing gift card obligations improves completeness and faithful representation of liabilities. The Level 3 measurement concern is fair: it raises verifiability questions, which the Board would weigh. Under the cost constraint, the Board would consider whether simplified measurement or private-company alternatives (through the Private Company Council) could reduce costs while preserving the benefits of better information to capital providers.
