Step 1 — Qualitative characteristics

Ask: Is this about whether information can change a decision (relevance), whether it depicts reality (faithful representation), or a quality that makes good information better (enhancing)?

Changing methods over time makes it hard to compare the same company across periods. Consistency is part of comparability; a change must be disclosed.

Clue: information that confirms or changes prior expectations has confirmatory value. Using earnings to forecast next year would be predictive value.

Materiality is company-specific: whether an item would probably affect a user's judgment depends on its size relative to the company and its nature.

Verifiability means different knowledgeable, independent measurers would reach consensus. Appraisals are verified indirectly; historical cost would be verified directly.

Standards should be free from bias and should not favor particular groups or outcomes. Lobbying for favorable economic consequences threatens neutrality.

Step 2 — Elements of financial statements

Ask: Is it a resource, an obligation, or the residual? If it's a change in equity, is it from central operations, a peripheral transaction, or a transaction with owners?

A bike shop doesn't sell vans as part of its central operations, so the excess is a gain (a peripheral or incidental transaction).

A transfer to owners that decreases equity is a distribution to owners. Dividends are not expenses.

Franklin Gem Mine now owes services in the future — a present obligation from a past event. Revenue will be recognized when the field trips occur.

Comprehensive income includes every change in equity except investments by and distributions to owners.

Quoted prices in active markets for identical assets are Level 1 — the most preferred inputs.

Observable prices for similar (not identical) items are Level 2.

The company's own, unobservable assumptions are Level 3 — the least preferred and least verifiable inputs.
