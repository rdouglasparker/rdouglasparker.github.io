Step 1 — Cash basis: net operating cash flow

Under the cash basis, only cash in and cash out counts. List every operating cash receipt and payment in each year.

Cash collected $205,000 − salaries $110,000 − insurance paid up front $36,000 − utilities actually paid ($18,000 − $3,000 unpaid = $15,000) = $44,000.

Step 2 — Accrual basis: revenue

Revenue is recognized when the service is performed (transferred to the client), not when cash arrives.

$

Ramsey Ridge performed and billed $250,000 of services. The $45,000 not yet collected is still revenue — it becomes accounts receivable.

$

$310,000 of services performed. Some of the $325,000 collected relates to year-1 billings, so collections exceed revenue this year.

Step 3 — Accrual basis: expenses

$

$36,000 ÷ 3 years = $12,000 per year. The cost is allocated to the periods that receive the coverage — a systematic and rational allocation. The remaining $24,000 is an asset (prepaid insurance).

$

The expense is the cost of resources used in year 1: $18,000, even though only $15,000 was paid. The unpaid $3,000 is a liability.

Staff salaries aren't tied to a specific event or sale; they benefit the company during the year in which revenue is earned, so they are expensed in that period.

Step 4 — Accrual basis: net income

$

$250,000 − salaries $110,000 − insurance $12,000 − utilities $18,000 = $110,000.

$

$310,000 − salaries $125,000 − insurance $12,000 − utilities $20,000 = $153,000.

Step 5 — The balance sheet connection

Every difference between cash flow and income leaves a footprint on the balance sheet.

$

Billed $250,000 − collected $205,000 = $45,000.

$

Beginning $45,000 + billed $310,000 − collected $325,000 = $30,000.

$

$36,000 − two years used ($24,000) = $12,000 — one more year of coverage remains.

$

The $3,000 of year-1 cost not yet paid. It is settled in year 2, which is why year-2 utility payments ($23,000) exceed year-2 expense ($20,000).

Step 6 — Pull it together

$

Income $263,000 − cash flow $221,000 = $42,000. That gap equals the ending balances still "in the pipeline": receivables of $30,000 + prepaid insurance of $12,000. Once the receivables are collected and the policy expires, the totals will match — the models differ only in timing.

Year-1 cash flow ($44,000) was depressed by the up-front insurance payment and slow collections, then jumped to $177,000. Net income ($110,000 → $153,000) tracks the growth in services performed and is the better indicator of ongoing cash-generating ability.

Takeaways
  • Revenue follows the transfer of services; expenses follow the use of resources.
  • Receivables, prepaid assets, and payables are the bridge between cash flow and income.
  • Over a company's whole life the totals converge; accrual accounting gives a better period-by-period picture.
