1. Why adjusting entries are needed

Journal entries during the period capture external transactions when they happen. But under accrual accounting, revenues must be recognized when goods or services are transferred and expenses when resources are used — which often doesn't line up with when cash moves or when a source document arrives. Adjusting entries, recorded at the end of each reporting period, bring the accounts up to date.

Two rules for every adjusting entry
  1. Each one affects at least one income statement account (revenue or expense) and one balance sheet account (asset or liability).
  2. Adjusting entries never involve cash.
Three categories of adjusting entries
CategoryCash flow timingTypes
PrepaymentsCash flow comes before expense or revenue recognitionPrepaid expenses; unearned (deferred) revenues
AccrualsCash flow comes after expense or revenue recognitionAccrued liabilities (expenses); accrued receivables (revenues)
EstimatesAmount depends on a judgment about the futureBad debts (uncollectible accounts)

2. Prepayments

Prepaid expenses

The company paid for a benefit in advance and recorded an asset. At period-end, the portion used up becomes expense: debit an expense, credit the asset.

(a) Rent: $9,000 covered six months; one month used ($9,000 ÷ 6)

(b) Supplies: $2,400 purchased; a count shows $1,600 on hand

Depreciation — a prepayment for a long-lived asset

Buying equipment is a prepayment for several years of service. Depreciation allocates the cost to the periods that benefit. The credit goes to Accumulated Depreciation, a contra asset that keeps the original cost intact while reducing the asset's book value.

(c) Depreciation: $36,000 ÷ 6 years ÷ 12 months
AccountDebitCredit
Depreciation Expense500
Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment500

Unearned (deferred) revenues

The company collected cash before providing the service and recorded a liability. As the service is provided, the liability becomes revenue: debit the liability, credit revenue.

(d) Memberships: $12,000 for 12 months; one month earned
AccountDebitCredit
Unearned Membership Revenue1,000
Membership Revenue1,000
Alternative approach Some companies record prepayments directly to an expense (or revenue) when the cash moves. Then the adjusting entry does the reverse — it moves the unexpired portion to an asset (or the unearned portion to a liability). The ending balances are identical either way; only the adjusting entry differs.

Accrued liabilities (accrued expenses)

The company has used a resource or incurred an obligation but hasn't paid or recorded it: debit an expense, credit a liability.

4. Estimates

Some amounts can only be estimated. The most common is bad debt expense: at period-end the company estimates how much of its receivables won't be collected. The credit goes to Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts, a contra asset to Accounts Receivable.

(h) Estimated uncollectible receivables of $200
AccountDebitCredit
Bad Debt Expense200
Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts200

Estimates are revised as new information arrives; that topic is developed further when receivables are covered in depth.

The statements are prepared in order — income statement → statement of shareholders' equity (retained earnings) → balance sheet — because net income feeds retained earnings, which feeds the balance sheet. The statement of cash flows classifies cash receipts and payments as operating, investing (e.g., buying equipment), or financing (e.g., issuing stock, borrowing, paying dividends).

On a classified balance sheet, current assets are expected to be converted to cash or used within one year (or the operating cycle, if longer); current liabilities will be paid with current assets in that time.

7. The closing process

At year-end, closing entries (1) transfer the balances of the temporary accounts to retained earnings and (2) reset the temporary accounts to zero for the next year. (To illustrate, we close Cullowhee Ridge Climbing Co.'s July balances as if July were the year-end.)

Some companies first close revenues and expenses to a temporary Income Summary account and then close its balance (net income) to retained earnings. The result is the same.

The post-closing trial balance lists only permanent accounts. Retained earnings now shows its ending balance ($8,650), and all revenue, expense, and dividend accounts are zero.

8. Converting from cash basis to accrual basis

Small businesses sometimes keep cash-basis records that must be converted to accrual-basis amounts. Use the change in the related balance sheet account:

Conversion formulas
Accrual amountStart with cash, then…
RevenueCash collected + increase in receivables (− decrease) − increase in unearned revenue (+ decrease)
Expense with a payableCash paid + increase in the payable (− decrease)
Expense with a prepaid assetCash paid − increase in the prepaid asset (+ decrease)

Example — Sylva Shuttle Service

  • Insurance: paid $20,000 cash; prepaid insurance fell from $5,000 to $3,000. Insurance expense = $20,000 + $2,000 decrease = $22,000.
  • Salaries: paid $150,000; salaries payable rose from $12,000 to $18,000. Salaries expense = $150,000 + $6,000 = $156,000.
  • Revenue: collected $410,000; receivables fell from $40,000 to $32,000; unearned revenue rose from $6,000 to $9,000. Revenue = $410,000 − $8,000 − $3,000 = $399,000.

Check the logic with a T-account: beginning balance + additions − reductions = ending balance.

9. Worksheets and reversing entries

  • A worksheet is an optional spreadsheet tool: unadjusted trial balance → adjusting entries → adjusted trial balance → income statement and balance sheet columns. It helps organize the end-of-period process but is not a financial statement.
  • Reversing entries are optional entries made on the first day of the next period that reverse certain adjusting entries (usually accruals). They let routine entries be recorded the normal way. Example: after accruing $700 of salaries on July 31, an August 1 reversing entry (debit Salaries Payable, credit Salaries Expense) lets the August 5 payroll be recorded as a simple debit to expense — the net August expense is still correct.
