1. The accounting equation and the dual effect

Every accounting system rests on the accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity

Assets are the company's resources. Liabilities and shareholders' equity are the claims to those resources — creditors' claims and owners' claims. Shareholders' equity has two sources:

  • Paid-in capital — amounts invested by shareholders (e.g., common stock).
  • Retained earnings — net income earned over the life of the company, less dividends distributed.
Assets = Liabilities + Paid-in Capital + (Revenues − Expenses − Dividends)

The equation must always balance, so every transaction has a dual effect: at least two accounts change. A purchase of equipment for cash increases one asset and decreases another; borrowing cash increases an asset and a liability.

External events

An exchange between the company and a separate entity — selling services, buying supplies, borrowing from a bank, paying employees. These are called transactions.

Internal events

Affect financial position without an exchange with another entity — using up supplies, depreciating equipment, earning revenue that was collected in advance. These are mostly captured by adjusting entries.

2. Accounts, debits, and credits

An account accumulates the increases and decreases in one financial statement item. The left side of an account is the debit side; the right side is the credit side. "Debit" and "credit" just mean left and right — not good or bad.

Why expenses and dividends are debits Revenues increase retained earnings, so they follow retained earnings' credit rule. Expenses and dividends decrease retained earnings, so they increase with debits. In every entry, total debits = total credits, which keeps the equation in balance.

Permanent vs. temporary accounts

  • Permanent (real) accounts — assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity. Their balances carry forward from period to period.
  • Temporary (nominal) accounts — revenues, expenses, gains, losses, and dividends. They measure changes in retained earnings for one period and are closed to retained earnings at the end of each year.

The general ledger is the collection of all accounts. A chart of accounts lists every account and its number.

3. The accounting processing cycle

The ten steps
WhenStep
During the period1. Obtain information about transactions from source documents
2. Analyze the transaction
3. Record the transaction in a journal
4. Post from the journal to the general ledger accounts
At the end of each period5. Prepare an unadjusted trial balance
6. Record adjusting entries and post them to the general ledger
7. Prepare an adjusted trial balance
8. Prepare financial statements
At the end of the year9. Close the temporary accounts to retained earnings
10. Prepare a post-closing trial balance

Computerized systems automate posting and trial balances, but the logic is the same — and understanding it is what lets you spot errors and judge whether the output makes sense.

4. Steps 1–3: Analyze and record transactions

Source documents — invoices, receipts, bank statements, contracts — provide the evidence for each transaction. You analyze each one by asking:

  1. Which accounts are affected?
  2. Does each account increase or decrease?
  3. Which accounts are debited and which are credited, and for how much?

The result is recorded chronologically in a journal (the "book of original entry") as a journal entry: date, accounts debited (listed first), accounts credited (indented), amounts, and a brief explanation.

Watch for these
  • Cash received before the service is performed creates a liability (unearned revenue), not revenue.
  • Paying an account payable is not an expense — the expense was recognized (or the asset recorded) when the item was bought.
  • Dividends are not an expense; they are a distribution to owners.

A balanced trial balance is not proof of accuracy It will not catch an entry that was never recorded, an entry recorded twice, the wrong amount on both sides, or a debit or credit posted to the wrong account. It will flag a one-sided error, such as posting only the debit, or transposed digits on one side.

7. Subsidiary ledgers and special journals

Companies with many similar transactions use:

  • Subsidiary ledgers — detail for a general ledger control account. The accounts receivable subsidiary ledger holds a separate account for each customer; the total of the subsidiary ledger must equal the control account balance.
  • Special journals — record repetitive transactions efficiently: the sales journal (credit sales), cash receipts journal, purchases journal (credit purchases), and cash disbursements journal. Everything else goes in the general journal.
