ACCT 351 · Leases — Chapter 15

Operating Leases & the Short-Term Exception

Reading Notes

Operating Leases: Same Balance Sheet, Different Income Statement

If a lease meets none of the five classification criteria, it's an operating lease — closer to a rental agreement than a purchase. The lessee still records a right-of-use asset and a lease liability at the present value of the lease payments, exactly as in a finance lease. What changes is how expense is reported and what the lessor does on the other side.

The lessor in an operating lease does not record a receivable and does not remove the asset from its books — it keeps depreciating the asset normally and simply records straight-line lease revenue each period.

The lessee still amortizes the right-of-use asset and accrues interest on the lease payable, but instead of reporting those two pieces separately (as in a finance lease), it combines them into a single lease expense that comes out equal every period — a straight-line rental expense.

How the lessee gets to a straight-line number

Interest is calculated the normal way: effective rate × outstanding lease payable balance. Amortization of the right-of-use asset is then "plugged" — set at whatever amount makes interest + amortization add up to the flat lease payment.

Amortization (operating lease) = Total lease payment − Interest expense for the period

Finance vs. operating, side by side: in a finance lease, interest is front-loaded (highest in year one) while amortization stays flat — so total expense declines over the lease term. In an operating lease, the two components are deliberately balanced every period so total expense is the same dollar amount each year. That's why lessees, all else equal, tend to prefer operating lease treatment: it defers expense recognition and keeps early-year net income higher.

Choosing the Discount Rate

Every present-value calculation in this chapter depends on a discount rate, and the rate you use depends on what the lessee actually knows:

Rate implicit in the lease — the lessor's effective rate of return, used by the lessee if known.

Incremental borrowing rate — used when the implicit rate isn't known (often because the lessor's residual value assumption isn't disclosed). This is what the lessee would pay a bank to borrow and buy the same asset outright.

Short-Term Leases: A Shortcut

A lease qualifies as short-term if both:

  1. The lease term (including any renewal options reasonably certain to be exercised) is 12 months or less, and
  2. It does not contain a purchase option the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise.
For a short-term lease, the lessee can elect to skip recording a right-of-use asset and lease liability altogether, and instead simply expense each lease payment as rent as it's paid — the same treatment the lessor already uses for operating lease revenue.
Example

Same equipment, but now a 12-month lease: 12 monthly payments of $8,333.

Lease expense8,333
Cash8,333

Recorded each month — no asset or liability appears on the balance sheet at all.

GAAP vs. IFRS: IFRS offers the same short-term shortcut, but defines "short-term" more strictly — a lease with any purchase option is disqualified under IFRS, even one the lessee isn't reasonably certain to exercise. IFRS also extends the shortcut to "small ticket" leases (assets valued at $5,000 or less), a category U.S. GAAP doesn't have.
