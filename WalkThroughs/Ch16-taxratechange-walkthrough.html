Key idea: Deferred tax liabilities and assets are measured using currently enacted tax rates for the years the temporary differences reverse. When a new rate is enacted, existing balances are revised and the entire effect hits income tax expense in the year of enactment.

The company used in Problems 2–7

Cullowhee Valley Builders, Inc. develops and sells residential lots. Its pretax accounting income for 2026 is $600,000, which includes $200,000 of income from lots sold on an installment basis.

  • The installment income is reported in the income statement in 2026, the year of sale, but on the tax return as the cash is collected: $80,000 in 2027 and $120,000 in 2028.
  • At the end of 2026, the enacted tax rates are 25% for 2026 and 2027 and 30% for 2028 and later years (a phased-in increase already signed into law).
  • The installment income is the only difference between pretax accounting income and taxable income. Cullowhee Valley had no deferred taxes before 2026.

Explanation

  1. The enacted rate for the reversal year. A deferred tax liability represents tax that will be paid when the difference reverses, so it's measured at the currently enacted rate that will be effective in that future year. When a phased-in change is scheduled, each year's amount is multiplied by that year's rate.
  2. In the year the change is enacted. Expected but not-yet-enacted rates are ignored. The FASB favors reliability here: an anticipated change isn't recognized until it's actually law.
  3. Income tax expense. The whole effect of revising deferred tax balances flows through income tax expense (and so net income) in the period the change is enacted. Prior years aren't restated.

The rate change doesn't affect tax payable for the current year at all. Its entire effect runs through the deferred tax liability and the income tax expense plug.

Without the new law, the DTL would have ended at $120,000 × 30% = $36,000, a $20,000 decrease, and expense would have been $145,000 − $20,000 = $125,000. The rate cut lowers 2027 tax expense by $12,000, so net income is higher. A company with a net deferred tax liability benefits from a rate decrease.

Some companies record the rate change in a separate entry (debit Deferred tax liability $12,000; credit Income tax expense $12,000). The combined entry above has the same total effect.

Lower future rates are good news for a company's future tax bills, but bad news for its deferred tax asset: the decrease is charged to tax expense, reducing net income in the year the law is enacted.
