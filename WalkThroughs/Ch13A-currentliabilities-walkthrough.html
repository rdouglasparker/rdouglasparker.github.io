Explanation

Conceptually, liabilities are recorded at the present value of all anticipated future cash payments (principal and interest). In practice, liabilities payable within one year from the balance sheet date are ordinarily recorded at their maturity amounts, here $100,000.

Accrued Liabilities and Paid Future Absences

Problem 9 — Paid Future Absences: Accrue or Not?

For each situation at year-end, decide whether the employer should accrue a liability for the future absences.

Your answers vs. the answers
Your answers compared with the correct answers for Problem 9
ItemYour answerAnswer
Explanation
  1. Accrue. All four conditions are met: the obligation is attributable to services already performed, the paid absence can be taken in a later year (it vests or accumulates), payment is probable, and the amount can be reasonably estimated.
  2. Do not accrue. Holiday time, military leave, maternity leave, and jury time typically don't accumulate if unused, so a liability usually isn't accrued. (If it were customary to carry holiday time forward and payment were probable, a liability would be accrued.)
  3. Accrue. Sick pay doesn't have to be accrued because future absence depends on future illness. But when company policy or custom pays sick pay even when employees aren't ill, it's appropriate to record a liability for the unused sick pay.
  4. Do not accrue. If the leave is granted only to perform research beneficial to the employer, the right to payment hasn't been earned by past service. (If past practice showed the sabbatical was intended as unrestricted compensated absence for past service, a liability would be accrued.)

Current vs. Noncurrent Classification

Problem 16 — Classifying Debt

Balsam Biotech, Inc. is preparing its financial statements for the year ended December 31, 2026. Should each of the following be reported as a current liability or a long-term liability?

Your answers vs. the answers
Your answers compared with the correct answers for Problem 16
ItemYour answerAnswer
Explanation
  1. Current. Debt callable by the creditor in the upcoming year must be reported as current, even if it isn't expected to be called.
  2. Long-term. A violation that makes debt callable normally forces a current classification. But the exception applies when it's probable the violation will be corrected within the grace period or waived by the creditor, which is the case here.
  3. Current. The currently maturing portion of long-term debt is reclassified as a current liability.
  4. Long-term. Short-term obligations can be reported as noncurrent if the company (1) intends to refinance on a long-term basis and (2) demonstrates the ability to do so by actual financing (or a refinancing agreement) before the statements are issued. Here the ability was demonstrated by actual financing.
  5. Current. Intent alone isn't enough. Requiring companies to demonstrate the ability to refinance avoids intentional or unintentional understatements of current liabilities.

Explanation

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities = $1,000,000 ÷ $800,000 = 1.25 to 1.

Acid-test ratio = (Cash + Short-term investments + Accounts receivable) ÷ Current liabilities = ($130,000 + $90,000 + $300,000) ÷ $800,000 = $520,000 ÷ $800,000 = 0.65 to 1. Inventories and prepaid expenses are excluded because they're less readily convertible to cash.

Union dues paid by employees are $100. The Social Security tax rate is 6.2% and the Medicare tax rate is 1.45%. Record the payroll (the employees' portion only).

Explanation

Salaries expense is the total amount earned. Social Security = 6.2% × $60,000 = $3,720 and Medicare = 1.45% × $60,000 = $870. Net pay is what is left after the deductions: $60,000 − $12,000 − $1,800 − $3,720 − $870 − $100 = $41,510. Amounts withheld from paychecks are liabilities until turned over to the appropriate third parties.
