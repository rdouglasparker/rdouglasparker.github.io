Long-Term Contracts

Recognize revenue over time with the cost-to-cost ratio, for a profitable contract and one headed for a loss.

Key idea: When a long-term contract qualifies for revenue recognition over time, revenue is recognized each period based on progress toward completion. With the cost-to-cost ratio, % complete = cumulative actual costs ÷ total estimated cost. Revenue this year = (contract price × % complete) − revenue recognized in prior years. Billings and collections are recorded separately and never drive revenue. If the contract is expected to produce an overall loss, the entire loss is recognized as soon as it's anticipated.

The company used in Problems 1–6

At the start of 2026, Balsam Builders, Inc. signs a $6,000,000 fixed-price contract to build a medical office building for Cullowhee Health Partners. The project is expected to take three years, revenue qualifies for recognition over time, and Balsam measures progress with the cost-to-cost ratio.

Explanation

Step 1, is the whole contract a loser? Total estimated cost is now $6,120,000 against a $6,000,000 price, so the entire project is expected to lose $120,000.

Step 2, revenue as usual: % complete = $3,060,000 ÷ $6,120,000 = 50%. Cumulative revenue = $6,000,000 × 50% = $3,000,000. Less 2026's $2,400,000 → revenue in 2027 = $600,000.

Step 3, the loss needed this period: cumulative gross profit (loss) to date must equal the full $(120,000) estimated loss. 2026 already recognized +$600,000, so 2027 must recognize a loss of $600,000 + $120,000 = $(720,000).

Step 4, back into cost of construction: cost of construction = revenue + loss = $600,000 + $720,000 = $1,320,000. That's not the $1,560,000 actually spent this year; cost of construction is the amount needed to report the right loss.

Cost of construction $1,800,000 + $1,320,000 + $3,120,000 = $6,240,000 (the actual total cost). Gross profit (loss) $600,000 − $720,000 − $120,000 = $(240,000), the total project loss.

Why did 2026 "get it wrong"? Recognizing revenue over time means early estimates drive early profit. When 2027's cost revision showed the project was a loser, the $600,000 of 2026 profit had to be reversed, plus the full $120,000 estimated loss, all in 2027. It's a change in accounting estimate, so 2026's financial statements are never restated.
