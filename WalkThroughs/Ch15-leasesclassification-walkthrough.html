The lease used in Problems 1 and 3–8

On January 1, 2024, Balsam Ridge Bakery leased a commercial baking oven from Mountain Trust Leasing. Mountain Trust Leasing purchased the equipment from Ridgeline Ovens Corporation at a cost of $360,376, which is also the oven's fair value.

  • The lease agreement specifies five annual payments of $85,000 beginning January 1, 2024 (the beginning of the lease), and on each December 31 from 2024 through 2027.
  • The five-year lease term, ending December 31, 2028, is equal to the estimated useful life of the oven.
  • The agreement does not transfer ownership and contains no purchase option.
  • Mountain Trust Leasing routinely acquires commercial kitchen equipment for lease to other food-service businesses.
  • The interest rate in these financing arrangements is 9%.

Testing the Five Classification Criteria

Problem 1 — Balsam Ridge's Lease

Apply the five classification criteria to the Balsam Ridge lease above. The present value of the lease payments is $360,376 ($85,000 × 4.23972, present value of an annuity due, n = 5, i = 9%). Use 75% and 90% as the guidelines for “major part” and “substantially all.”

Your answers vs. the answers
ItemYour answerAnswer
Explanation
  1. Ownership transfer — No. Nothing in the agreement says title passes to Balsam Ridge.
  2. Purchase option — No. The agreement contains no purchase option at all, so there's nothing to be “reasonably certain” about.
  3. Major part of economic life — Yes. The 5-year lease term covers the oven's entire 5-year life: 5 ÷ 5 = 100%, well above the 75% guideline.
  4. Present value of payments — Yes. $360,376 ÷ $360,376 fair value = 100%, well above the 90% guideline. In effect, Balsam Ridge is paying for the whole asset.
  5. Specialized asset — No. Mountain Trust Leasing routinely acquires and leases this type of equipment, so the equipment would have alternative uses to it once this lease ends.

Decision: at least one criterion is met (two here: criteria 3 and 4), so this is a finance lease to Balsam Ridge and a sales-type lease to Mountain Trust Leasing.

Notice that only one criterion is needed. Criteria 1, 2, and 5 all failed, and it didn't matter. Also notice that the two parties classify the same lease from opposite sides: a finance lease for the lessee is a sales-type lease for the lessor.

Cataloochee Precision Works leases a CNC machining center from Tuckasegee Equipment Finance.

  • Lease term: 5 years, with payments at the beginning of each year.

Explanation

Criteria 1, 2, and 5 are answered directly by the facts: title doesn't transfer, there's no purchase option, and the machine is a standard model with alternative uses to the lessor. None is met.

Criterion 3: 5-year lease term ÷ 8-year economic life = 62.5%. That's below the 75% guideline, so the lease term is not the “major part” of the machine's life.

Criterion 4: $431,213 ÷ $500,000 = 86.2%. That's below the 90% guideline, so the payments are not “substantially all” of fair value.

Decision: none of the five criteria is met, so this is an operating lease to both the lessee and the lessor.

To be an operating lease, a lease must fail all five tests. Also notice that 86.2% is close to the 90% guideline. The 75% and 90% figures are one reasonable approach, not bright lines, so leases that land near a threshold are where estimates of economic life and fair value (and management's incentives to shade them) matter most.

The Lessee's Finance Lease (Balsam Ridge Bakery)

Problem 3 — Beginning of the Lease

Balsam Ridge's lease is a finance lease. Calculate the present value of the five lease payments, which is the amount Balsam Ridge records for its right-of-use asset and its lease payable. Use the present value of an annuity due of $1 (n = 5, i = 9%) of 4.23972.

Your answer vs. the answer
ItemYour answerAnswer
Explanation

The lessee pays for the right to control the use of the equipment, and that price is the present value of the lease payments:

$85,000 × 4.23972 = $360,376

Because the first payment is due at the beginning of the lease, the payments are an annuity due, so you use the annuity-due factor. (With an ordinary annuity factor of 3.88965 you'd get $330,620, which is too low by one period of interest: $330,620 × 1.09 ≈ $360,376.) On a calculator, use BEG mode with N = 5, I = 9, PMT = −85,000 and solve for PV. In Excel: =PV(.09,5,-85000,1).

Just as with an installment purchase, Balsam Ridge debits an asset and credits a liability for the present value of what it owes:

Jan. 1, 2024
Right-of-use asset (present value of lease payments)360,376
Lease payable (present value of lease payments)360,376

Balsam Ridge doesn't have legal ownership of the equipment, but it has acquired the right to use it. That's why the asset is called a right-of-use asset.

$360,376 ÷ 5 years = $72,075 per year

In a finance lease, interest expense and amortization expense are recorded and reported separately.

The amortization period. A lessee normally amortizes over the lease term. But if ownership transfers, or a purchase option is reasonably certain to be exercised (criterion 1 or 2 is met), the lessee will still have the asset after the lease ends. In that case it amortizes over the asset's useful life. In Balsam Ridge's lease the two periods happen to be the same five years, which is why the question above asks about a hypothetical purchase option.

The Lessor's Sales-Type Lease

Explanation

The lessor records a receivable for the present value of the payments to be received ($360,376), the mirror image of the lessee's liability. It also removes (derecognizes) the asset being leased from its books. Because the "selling price" (the present value of the payments) equals Mountain Trust Leasing's cost ($360,376), there is no gap and therefore no selling profit.

Jan. 1, 2024
Lease receivable (present value of lease payments)360,376
Equipment (lessor's cost: carrying amount)360,376

Mountain Trust Leasing is a financial intermediary: it acquired the asset and financed it for Balsam Ridge. Its only income from the lease will be interest revenue over the lease term.

Jan. 1, 2024

This is recorded just like any sale of a product, in two pieces: one for the receivable and revenue, one for cost of goods sold and the reduction of inventory.

Compare it with an ordinary sale. If a restaurant-supply store sells you an $850 commercial mixer on its credit account and paid $520 wholesale, it debits Accounts receivable and credits Sales revenue for $850, then debits Cost of goods sold and credits Inventory for $520. Ridgeline's entry has the same structure, with a lease receivable in place of the account receivable and equipment in place of inventory. Leases are just one of the arrangements sellers use to help customers buy their products.

(If the asset's carrying value had exceeded its fair value, the lessor would record a selling loss instead.)
