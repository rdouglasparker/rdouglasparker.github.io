Key idea: An NOL can be used to reduce taxable income in other years. A carryback applies the loss to the two prior years and produces a refund of taxes already paid; any remaining loss becomes a carryforward that reduces future taxable income and is recognized as a deferred tax asset. Either way, the tax benefit is recognized in the year the loss occurs.

The company used in Problems 1–6

Purple & Gold Property and Casualty Insurance Company had a difficult storm season. In 2026, it reported a net operating loss (NOL) of $700,000 for both financial reporting and tax purposes. There are no other differences between pretax accounting income and taxable income.

  • As a property and casualty insurance company, Purple & Gold is still allowed to carry an NOL back to the two prior years and carry any remainder forward, with no 80% limitation.

How NOLs Work

Problem 1 — The Rules for NOLs

Explanation

  1. Earliest year first. The NOL must be applied to the earlier year first and then brought forward to the next year. Any NOL left after reducing taxable income to zero in the two previous years is carried forward.
  2. Taxes actually paid. A carryback produces a refund of taxes paid in those prior years, so the refund uses the rates that applied then.
  3. Enacted future rate. A carryforward creates future deductible amounts, just like a deductible temporary difference, so it's measured at the enacted rate for the years it will be used.
  4. The year of the loss. Both the refund from the carryback and the future savings from the carryforward reduce the reported loss in the year the NOL occurs. That reduction is often labeled an income tax benefit.

For most companies, tax years beginning after December 31, 2017 allow no carryback: NOLs are carried forward indefinitely, and a carryforward can offset at most 80% of taxable income in any year. Property and casualty insurers (and some farm-related businesses) still follow the carryback rules, and many states allow carrybacks for state income taxes. Problem 7 shows the general rule.

Start with the earliest year. The NOL wipes out 2024's $200,000 of taxable income, then 2025's $300,000. That uses $500,000 of the loss:

Each carryback year can only be reduced to zero; you can't create a refund larger than the tax that was paid.

Explanation

A deferred tax asset for an NOL is recognized without regard to whether future taxable income will be enough to use it. It's then reduced by a valuation allowance if it is more likely than not (a likelihood of more than 50%) that some portion won't be realized.

$103,000 × ¼ = $25,750 valuation allowance

Journal entry, Dec. 31, 2027

The valuation allowance is a contra account to the deferred tax asset. Net deferred tax asset: $103,000 − $25,750 = $77,250.

Even though NOLs can now be carried forward indefinitely, a valuation allowance may still be needed: the same problems that caused the operating loss can also make it less likely the company will earn enough taxable income in the future.
