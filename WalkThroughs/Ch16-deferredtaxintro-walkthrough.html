The four-step process used throughout this walkthrough: Step 1 determine income tax payable from taxable income on the tax return; Step 2 determine the ending balance of the deferred tax liability (or asset) from future taxable (or deductible) amounts; Step 3 find the change needed to adjust the account to that balance; Step 4 income tax expense is the plug that balances the entry.

The company used in Problems 1–8

On January 1, 2026, Catamount Climbing Gear, Inc. bought a computerized rope-braiding machine for $300,000. The machine has a three-year useful life and no residual value.

  • Income statement: straight-line depreciation of $100,000 per year in 2026, 2027, and 2028.
  • Tax return: accelerated depreciation of $180,000 in 2026, $90,000 in 2027, and $30,000 in 2028.
  • Pretax accounting income is $500,000 in each of the three years (after the $100,000 of straight-line depreciation).
  • Depreciation is the only difference between pretax accounting income and taxable income.
  • The enacted tax rate is 25% for all years. Catamount had no deferred taxes before 2026.

Understanding the Temporary Difference

Problem 1 — What Kind of Difference Is This?

Before calculating anything, size up the depreciation difference. In 2026, Catamount deducts $180,000 on its tax return but reports only $100,000 of depreciation expense in its income statement.

Explanation

  1. Temporary. Over the machine's life, total depreciation is $300,000 on both the income statement and the tax return. Only the timing differs, so the difference is temporary. It originates in 2026 and reverses in 2027 and 2028.
  2. Lower. The tax return deducts $80,000 more depreciation than the income statement, so taxable income ($420,000) is $80,000 lower than pretax accounting income ($500,000).
  3. Future taxable amounts. Catamount uses up most of its tax deductions early. In 2027 and 2028, tax depreciation will be less than book depreciation, so taxable income will be higher than accounting income. Those later increases are future taxable amounts.
  4. Deferred tax liability. Future taxable amounts mean Catamount will pay more tax later. The tax it gets to put off today is a deferred tax liability (DTL).

Rule of thumb: taxable income lower than accounting income now → future taxable amounts → deferred tax liability.

Start with pretax accounting income and adjust for the temporary difference. The income statement subtracted $100,000 of depreciation, but the tax return subtracts $180,000, so taxable income is $80,000 lower:

$500,000 − $80,000 = $420,000 taxable income

Tax payable is always based on the tax return, never on pretax accounting income. This is the amount Catamount owes the government for 2026.

The income statement and balance sheet perspectives are two complementary ways to reach the same Step 2 balance. Use whichever is easier for the facts you're given.

The effective tax rate is $125,000 ÷ $500,000 = 25%. A temporary difference doesn't change the effective tax rate; it only changes when the tax is paid.

Income tax payable is due within the year, so it's a current liability. Deferred tax liabilities and deferred tax assets are always classified as noncurrent in a classified balance sheet, regardless of when the underlying temporary differences reverse.
